Theodores|parent|prev|next|

Each to their own, however, there is the matter of 'use case'. I worked in a bicycle shop where we had skateboards as a sideline, my product knowledge was not there for them, I expected the customer to know what they needed (and for their mother to pay).

My knowledge was in the folding bicycles, and a surprising amount of knowledge was needed to guide customers through to a sale. We had all the UK brands, including Brompton, Dahon, Airnimal and Montague. My goal was to take their money, so I was never going to talk them out of the over-hyped Brompton to sell them the far superior Dahon. (Dahon have the more useful patents).

However, if I knew the customer was going to be on a packed commuter train, I would sell them the Dahon, since it doubles up as a seat, when folded down, which the Brompton doesn't do.

Regarding the article, we had this brand called Airnimal that were designed to fold up into boxes that could go on a plane, so we sold Airnimal to those customers. Airnimal bicycles took a while to setup, whereas the Brompton or Dahon could be deployed as one leaves the train door, for the bicycle to be rideable on the platform.

There were also the folding bicycles with really small wheels. These would suit canal-boat people, or people with camper vans. These customers didn't need much, but their requirements were carefully checked.

Part of the sale was demonstrating the fold. To the uninitiated this is a challenge, so it was crucial to be able to know what you were doing and just do the fold effortlessly, several times, and, the sale was pretty much over if you could get the customer to do the fold.

I appreciate your enthusiasm for the skateboard and I can see it making sense if living in an urban area with lots of stairs and a crime problem. However, the skateboard just doesn't scale well.

I used to commute from Reading to London and then to north London, taking a folding bicycle on the big train and then a total of 22 - 25 miles under my own steam. I could have done that with public transport and a skateboard, to optimise the connections between trains, tubes (subway), bus and pedestrian areas, however, the time and expense would have not made my commute viable.

On the skateboard I am sure you have learned hacks to get across town, on a folding bicycle you find different hacks. For example, you might exit the station a back way, avoiding hordes of people and finding your way onto a network that is there for cycling.

There is also the matter of legal things, if you are riding a bicycle on the road then you have the rights and responsibilities of a vehicle, on a skateboard you are just a 'jaywalker', with important legal implications if you get involved in an incident.

Back to the shop, I just took money, as much as possible, with a view to having long-term customers that spread our reputation positively by word of mouth. There is only so far that a given customer can be rinsed, so the reality is that most of what goes out the door is upper-mid-range. It was important to sell them the right product, or solution, hence the folding bicycles, hybrid bicycles, e-bikes, fitness, and about a dozen MTB genres. On top of that we had the skateboard.

I can honestly say that, despite what you say with undoubted enthusiasm making perfect sense and known to me at the time, I never felt that I 'should' have sold a given customer a skateboard instead of a folding (or other) bicycle. I would if I could, but I didn't. When it came down to 'specialist sales of mobility solutions', there was always some obscure folding bicycle that just suited the customer better than a skateboard.

The holy grail of personal mobility is a device that

>Requires a little effort to use, but not too much

>Is cheap and easy to purchase/service

>Can be carried, or else stored with a minimal amount of space

>Is maneuverable but stable

>Has a failure mode with little chance of serious injury

>Is relatively easy to learn to use

>Is fun to use

Walking, but faster. Still haven't quite found it yet.

That's basically a skateboard as long as you don't go bombing down hills out of control. Learning curve might be tough if you didn't do it as a kid however. But if you did, you've unlocked a pretty useful ability that meets all these things. Skate parts are cheap, even going name brand, but a blank deck is the same plywood as any. You can walk into any skateshop across town and get standardized replacement parts. You can get some of the best equipment on the market and only be out like $100-150 or so, for the complete build.

Back to failure mode, if you aren't going at very out of control speeds it's no worry with big and soft longboard style wheels (vs harder skate wheels meant for tricks vs cruising). About as much as you'd do to yourself on a bike going reasonable speeds: maybe scrape up the palms catching yourself or twist an ankle or wrist if you really catch it awkwardly. Can't really have a non zero risk when walking itself is non zero risk of falling related injury or death.

Escooters are also pretty great. xaomi mi platform is pretty standardized, only a couple hundred or so as well. Lot of handwringing about them in the press, but if you don't plan to drive drunk with your buddy on your back you should be alright.

Skateboards really are the ideal if you can skate. Most people didn't learn as kids, though, and I think that's a disqualifier.
