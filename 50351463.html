>your complaints boil down to "I don't like it because I'm not familiar with it" which is not valid critique

dhx cited Fitts's law, and you answered with "you're just not used to it" and a pompous, hand-waving non sequitur about traffic signs you pulled out of your reactive ass.

Fitts's law doesn't give a shit what you're used to. The time to hit a target goes up with its distance and down with its size. A row of tiny unlabeled icons crammed together is slow and easy to miss on the thousandth click, not just the first. Getting used to them teaches you what they mean. It doesn't make them any bigger.

Paul Fitts published it in 1954. Card, English and Burr used it at Xerox PARC in 1978 to show why the mouse beat the joystick and the cursor keys. It's the first thing anyone who designs pointing interfaces should know. If you've never heard of it, you have no business telling anyone what counts as valid critique of a menu. Neither do the people at Microsoft who shipped that row of icons, whether they never heard of it or heard of it and ignored it.

As for traffic signs: they're standardized by international treaty, you have to pass a test on them before you're allowed to drive, and the European stop sign still says STOP. Show me the treaty and the driving test for the Windows 11 context menu icons.

Familiarity is something a design has to earn, not something users owe it. Pie menus are self-revealing: they lead, follow, and get out of the way:

1) A novice clicks, sees the labels, and the menu leads them to the item.

2) An intermediate starts moving before reading, and the menu follows, highlighting the slice to confirm the direction.

3) An expert flicks in the direction without waiting, and the menu gets out of the way and never even draws. It's the same motion every time, so using it is learning it through rehearsal.

Every item is the same short distance from the click, and every target is a whole wedge out to the edge of the screen.

Jack Callahan, Mark Weiser, Ben Shneiderman and I measured it at Maryland in 1988: pie menus were significantly faster than linear menus with fewer errors, even for novices.

An Empirical Comparison of Pie vs. Linear Menus, CHI'88:

https://donhopkins.medium.com/an-empirical-comparison-of-pie...

I've been building them ever since, in shipping open source and commercial products and reusable components: an X10 window manager (the one we ran that experiment on), NeWS, SimCity in both NeWS/HyperLook and X11/Tcl/Tk, ScriptX, The Sims, ActiveX, a personalizable Palm TV guide and universal remote you could stroke with one finger instead of using the pen, and numerous implementations in JavaScript for web browsers.

The earliest known pie menus were in PIXIE, at Cambridge in 1969, by Neil Wiseman, Heinz Lemke and John Hiles: radial buttons that traveled with the light pen's tracking cross on a PDP-7. Its 1972 program runs again in the browser, so you can try them yourself.
