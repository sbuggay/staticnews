I think the layman's perception of the increase in survival (~13 months versus ~6 months) will make this look underwhelming. Nevertheless, it's important to remember that the incentives for clinical trials is to only include very late-stage patients who have exhausted all other options (faster trials, smaller sample sizes, surrogate endpoints, a shot at accelerated approval, ...). The fact that a treatment shows promise for these patients that are often in very poor health and considered "hopeless" by today's medicine is exceedingly rare, and the signal from this trial is indeed strong.

The (somewhat reasonable) hope is that patients 1. with earlier forms of pancreatic cancer (non-metastatic) 2. that can undergo surgery 3. together with a (neo)adjuvant treatment with these kras inhibitors, will end up having a much lower recurrence rate and a chance for a long, healthy life.

I think this is genuinely good news worth celebrating!

There is a conversational habit that many people have, of responding to a specific situation described by someone, with a generalization of that situation, or by bringing up one of their own similar experiences.

None of these responses are terrible, these responses are no doubt true. But in conversation, those are low bars.

These segues come across, and frankly are, very dismissive of the original particular circumstances brought up.

It is best to respond to specific situations, with something thoughtful and specific to them. The specific situation was not brought up as conversation filler or general smalltalk initiation, but for its specific import, so respect that.

Its an easy bad habit to have, but once it is recognized, it is fairly easy to break. And the result is people will find conversations with you much more thoughtful, caring, and pertinent.

Need examples beyond your own? Don't respond to the news that someone lost a leg with amputation statistics, or the time you almost lost a finger. Don't respond to someone's pain with trivia or tautology. Respond with specific comfort or help, or not at all.

Nevermark|root|parent|next|

Every conversation I ever had where someone brought up an incident that had serious meaning to them, and I immediately and habitually brought up a comparable one of my own. Or worse, a similar situation I had heard of. Thinking I was saying "I understand", when what gets perceived is "The situation that is bothering them involves nothing unique or worthy of my focused attention". Hundreds of such conversations at least.

As a sibling commenter points out, the motive is often a genuine attempt to connect. It just happens to be inept.

My point isn't to shame. But to avoid the negative impact, or lost positive impact, due to missed nuance.

Someone had to point out this common communication misfire to me too.

When someone is sharing something, connecting and demonstrating understanding is much more successful by showing you are focused on what they said, and their situation, not a generalization or free associated version of it. And especially not turning things toward oneself or some abstract other.

Creative people can almost always think of something comparable in some way, that they experienced. But did we really need to talk about that? Obviously not, since we had to be prompted. But the person whose situation prompted us felt the need to bring up their situation. Put their expressed need first.

They are sharing a need to talk about something serious and specific.

When someone is sharing anything painful, this is especially true.

I am not saying never bring up one's own experience. Once the dynamic is recognized, its usually easy to tell the difference between someone sharing something serious, vs. just chatting about something. And the difference between a "me too" reflex vs. sharing an experience that offers some genuine insight or help. Even then, staying focused on their experience will be appreciated as care and attention given.

--

Specific example, made up but typical:

When someone says "My mother died", the best response isn't "that's tough, I lost my mom too", or "that's bad, and it happens to all of us", it is "I am so sorry, were you close? Tell me about her." They brought up their mom, their loss. Show you care by keeping your focus on their mom and their loss. Encouraging them to share as much as they need to.

Their mom was a special person. Not an example mom or "like" another mom, just because other people have lost moms.

There is no "comparable" mom or loss, and even an inadvertent implication along those lines is inaccurate, insensitive. and tends to shutdown that line of conversation prematurely.

That AIs have somehow managed to wrangle all human information to advance human capability in a variety of domains is promising because individual humans likely have no hope of wrangling, in a single human mind, all of the genetic, physical, and chemical pathways in a living organism as complex as a human.

I'm not predicting when this happens but given that there are already services that will print a custom DNA or RNA strand to order, I absolutely see a world in which viruses, custom-designed to attack an individual's cancer cells bearing genetic markers matching the cancer cell type (e.g breast, lung, etc.) and mutations should be possible since fundamentally biology is built on information (RNA and DNA).

So I agree with you that we shouldn't expect a "cancer cure" in the next 6 months.

But since cancer starts in the information-holding part of the cell, building therapies that are information-sensitive with a previously unimagined rapid turnaround, and packaged in a virus which may also be heavily genetically modified to suit this purpose, does seem like exactly the type of problem these new generative manipulators of information are built to solve.
