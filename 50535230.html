It is impossible to say for sure, but I think there is a very high chance these models do in fact run more efficiently:

* We can see in the Chinese open weight models that there has been a clear trend of moving to more efficient hybrid attention formats over the last year (see e.g. https://sebastianraschka.com/blog/2026/full-to-hybrid-attent...), and presumably the closed labs have been following a similar path, perhaps using the innovations the Chinese labs are using, or perhaps developing their own. And at this point the models themselves are good enough to accelerate developing all sorts of efficiency improvements which can then be used in the next generation.

* The most dramatic pricing cuts are often on cached read tokens, which suggests KV cache efficiency improvements. Other token types haven't had pricing cut so much. If this was just a subsidy they'd probably have cut pricing more evenly across the board.

Finally, I do not think it is in the interests of the big labs to slash their margins on inference for api (i.e. enterprise) pricing. Much of their high value comes from having very big margins on inference (and we now know for sure that they do have them, see Anthropic's S1). There is presumably a strong shared desire to not have a race down to commodity margins. So most likely if they are cutting prices, their cost of inference has fallen. Individual subscriptions are a totally different matter, they are heavily subsidized compared to api pricing, and those are indeed about generating hype and developer mindshare. But they are not where the labs are getting their revenue.

eli|root|parent|prev|next|

I have built my own benchmarks and it's very valuable to have them but I'm skeptical you can get good results vibe coding them.

Getting the framework right isn't that hard, but requires some careful thought. You probably don't want to just checkout a repo at a certain commit hash - agents will explore other commits and branches. (Public repos are also risky in general: agents with internet access can find later commits, and public repos could be in the training data anyway.) I use `git archive` and give the runner just the files at that commit and nothing else. I also do the run inside an isolated container that is recreated each run so it can't find e.g. related files left in /tmp from a previous run.

Getting the benchmark tasks right is harder. I don't think there's any way to get around writing the task and its evaluation by hand. I really tried to get a few different frontier LLMs to do it and they're all terrible at "imagining" what an agent would need to solve a problem without giving away the answer or providing too little information. Also quite bad at writing evaluation criteria that tests whether the solution matches the assignment (versus just how close it is to a golden solution, or how close it is to how they would solve it)

I personally don't like or trust using LLM as a grader either, but I guess people manage to get that to work well enough.
