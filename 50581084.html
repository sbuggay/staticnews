The answer is, "I cannot explain that force to you. It is just, as a matter of fact, one of the fundamental forces of nature."

He's taking it as read that the reason he is giving his extended answer is to explain why he cannot answer at all.

What he could have said to a physics student is that: wave-functions of charged particles have a property, "phase". The problem is that point values of phase cannot be observed, and if you formulate the laws of physics with raw "phase" values (like a measure of mass, at 5kg), they dont work. So something must exist to "track" phase, so that every pair of measurements "cancel out" the effect of this property. That something is the EM field. Magentism is a spatial aspect of this field. Eletricity is a space-time aspect of this field. So the magnetic force arises out of a need to "conserve phase", and that phase is how a pushing force is experienced: two particles experiencing a magentic interaction take on different phases (their wavefunctions interfere such that..).

This might "feel" like an explanation to the physics student, because they will be willing to make the assumptions needed, and they will find conservation-style explanations sufficiently satisfying. ie., they can already see a wavefn description will have a free phase term. But a lay person wouldnt feel satisfied because more unknowns have been introduced, and conserving "another unknown" is no advancement in understanding.

That's the gulf which is frustrating feynman. There's nothign to be said to the interviewer other than the force has no explanation in terms of anything he's familiar with. The question is too basic. And feynman is not going to go thru a formulation of QM in terms of wavefns in 5min -- it would be an entire lecture course.

martin-t|prev|next|

The times I have seen the "stochastic parrot" argument used seriously, I could count on one hand. But the times I've seen people mock it because it's easier to argue against than well-constructed criticisms...

Look, LLMs are models of the training data lossily compressed in a way that allows interpolating between patterns while decompressing.

A pattern can be "it's better to use int32_t than int", what a for loop in c++ looks like, that testing for primality can be done by trying divisors only until the square root of n, etc. Combining those patterns, an LLM can produce a working is_prime function in c++.

Older LLMs were only able to handle simple patterns so the code was more likely to be verbatim training data, newer LLMs work on deeper, more complex patterns so it's harder to detect where those patterns come from.

Similarly, the list of approaches a mathematician uses to approach a problem is also a pattern. One of them will be proof by induction - how to convert such a proof to Lean will is also a pattern. The idea that induction can be done separately for odd and even cases is also a pattern.

LLMs are no longer generating the final output directly - they are simulating human work but at a much larger scale. That's how they are able to generate novel results.

But it's all based on labor scraped and patternized without consent or credit, let alone monetary compensation.

And I still wouldn't call it intelligence because that gives people the idea that it's something which gives complete, correct and unbiased answers. It's still autocomplete under the hood, there's just more and more tooling built around it to keep it on track, to check intermediate results, to do arithmetic, etc. The LLMs in some cases is more like a heuristic for deterministic tools.
