Not necessarily. There isn't any specific amount price has to move as a result of a change in supply.

Suppose some part of the demand for something is inelastic, e.g. a pipe bursts and your basement is filling with water, and another part is extremely elastic, e.g. people stop building new housing if they'd have to pay more for trades workers to build it than they could sell the new housing for.

The second one is constantly at the threshold, i.e. we end up with as much new housing as it's currently economical to build. So any reduction in the cost of construction, even a small one, can create a large number of jobs building new housing, because suddenly a million construction projects that were just on the threshold of unprofitability are now out of the red and get the green light.

On top of that, existing zoning rules and other regulations are currently suppressing an enormous amount of construction, and that's something we ought to fix. And if we do fix it then the trades could absorb a large influx of new workers without lowering existing compensation at all.

An interesting thing about the Riemann hypothesis is that there are a lot of other conjectures that have no obvious connection to it but that turn out to be equivalent. Equivalent in this context means that if any of them are true then all of them are.

Here is one that is easy to understand with a high school level of math:

Let H(n) = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ... + 1/n, where n is a positive integer.

Let D(n) = the sum of the positive integers that divide a positive integer n. For example D(12) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12 = 28.

Conjecture: For n > 1, we have D(n) < H(n) + exp(H(n)) log(H(n)).

With so many different conjectures turning out to be equivalent, I'll not be surprised if it turns out that there are also multiple different ways to prove it.
