This book ius popularizing a thoroughly debunked misinterpretation of Godel FYI.

No algorithm can prove all true statements - but it is not the case that there are 'true' properties which no algorithm can prove.

Godel sentences are 'true' only on the assumption of consistency. If you allow the computer to assume consistency, it can prove the Godel sentence too. If you disallow humans from assuming consistency, they cannot prove the Godel sentence.

All such constructions follow the same pattern - they are 'true' only upon taking additional assumptions.

I heard an interesting question once: if we consider all the greatest discoveries of mankind and imagine a counterfactual world where their discoverer had not existed, how many additional years would it have taken for someone else to have made the discovery?

Subjectively, I think most of the major discoveries would have only been delayed by a decade or two. E.g., general relativity, quantum mechanics, the laws of thermodynamics.

For the incompleteness theorems, however, I’m not sure that we would even be aware of them today without Gödel. Tarski might have come the closest, but there were still a few insane conceptual leaps that had to be made beyond the undefinability of truth.

The theorems are often considered a curiosity at the edges of mathematics, but I’m not so sure. I think they might be more fundamental than typically given credit for. The problem is that the theorems aren’t really actionable since they rule out essentially all algorithmic methods of working around the limitations they entail.

coderenegade|parent|prev|next|

He's best known for the two incompleteness theorems; essentially, mathematics can't prove every true statement, and it can't prove its own consistency. The latter opens up the possibility that mathematics can prove mutually exclusive statements, though most mathematicians believe that mathematics is consistent. It's hard to overstate how deeply these two results shook the field. It was the original crisis in mathematics.

Goedel was also the first to come up with a solution to the Einstein field equations of general relativity.

…mathematics can't prove every true statement…

This isn’t at all what he proved. We humans have in our minds what the naturals numbers are. Gödel showed that we can’t find a nice set of axioms from which all arithmetic truth can be proved.

Assuming arithmetic is consistent then we can find an axiomatic system in which each true statement is a theorem. Just take as your axiomatic system the collection of all true arithmetic statements. Now every true statement of arithmetic is a theorem of this axiomatic system. It’s just that this system is useless since there’s no way to determine if an arbitrary statement is an axiom.

Regarding consistency. Consider this. A system that can prove its own consistency doesn’t tell you anything because an inconsistent system can also prove its own consistency.
