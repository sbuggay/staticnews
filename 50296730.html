Software robustness, at least in terms of "has the app crashed on any client machines today", is achievable. It's just expensive enough to achieve that most people (rightly or not) don't prioritize it, and the more moving parts (like third party libraries or language runtimes) your application has the harder it is to achieve. Maybe AI will lower the cost of achieving that.

When I worked on Guild Wars every server or user crash went to a shared inbox and was bucketed so we could identify and act on new crashes. IIRC we maintained very, very low production crash rates and could prove that many of the crashes were due to hardware faults.

Similarly, Mozilla tries to analyze and act on every crash report they get in the field. If a webpage visited by even a small % of the customer base was causing crashes, the team worked to try and fix the crash to deliver stability. "No bugs at all" is an even higher bar, but it probably is possible for simpler applications with the use of stuff like theorem provers. I'd love to see someone try it in production but I think the return on investment for that is probably super super negative.

Senior engineers shine for this, because they tend to have a better nose for code smell. - Reviewing still matters.
