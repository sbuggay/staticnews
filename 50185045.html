Nope; the compiler was supposed to end coding; when the word coding referred to taking an abstract program written on paper and translating it to machine code.

Compilers were first called automatic coders.

The word compiling referred to something more like linking: producing an image containing multiple library routines. In other words, similar to compiling an anthology of works into a book.

After compilers, the word coding to changed to writing program code in any language.

Because the tooling for automatic coding also compiled the bits of code into a larger image, the term compiler/compiling came to be used for the coding part also.

While the terms reshuffled, the concrete reality was simply that people worked less in assembly language, not that fewer people worked or that programming ended.

> With compilers there is a (roughly, barring optimizations) 1:1 correspondence between input and output.

This is repeated in a lot of comments here but hasn't really been true for a while. I remember, c. 2010, Kenton Varda complaining to Google's internal compiler team about how it generated some particularly non-intuitive assembly code out of the protobuf generated code, and the response was "Yeah everything is adaptive optimization now. Benchmark it and give us a profile and we'll put it in the compiler test suite. Trying to reason about what the compiler is doing and micro-optimize accordingly hasn't worked since the mid-90s."

I do think that LLMs are a step change in how we program computers, but the main step change is that the source language is now English, which opens up programming to a wider market accordingly. And this'll come with all the downsides of English: the ambiguity, lack of precision, multiple meanings, and so on. If you try to recover that control over what the computer generates, you end up reinventing computer science. (Which isn't that far off from how I use LLMs to program: anything that has widely accepted "best practices", I'll just let the LLM do, but if there's anything novel or requiring high-precision, I'll tell it "leave that function body blank; here are the inputs and outputs to the rest of the program; I will fill in the algorithm details myself.")

I also think that people are underselling the extent to which compilers were a step change to previous practices of programming. For someone accustomed to C, try doing register allocation or inlining yourself; you will suck at it, and it will take up the vast majority of your head space that could be spent on better things. For someone accustomed to a garbage-collected language like Python, try doing manual memory allocation and object ownership yourself; you will suck at it, and it will take up the vast majority of your head space that could be spent on better things.

LLMs are not this, and they cannot be this because it is their ability to operate on vague instructions that make them as powerful as they are. They can take an imprecise natural language description of a process and realize it as a process in a programming language. The problem is that understanding and manipulating the precise problem description is your job as a software engineer. The LLM can and will make its best attempt based on what exists in its training data, but it cannot know what is the precise behavior that actually fulfills goals and I think, contrary to the parent article, it cannot determine where the edges are to ask you with enough certainty that you will be able to take your hands completely off the wheel.

I think the nature of LLMs also explains why it is good at the verticals it is good at. Porting is an extremely well defined, replicate the behavior on another platform. There are far fewer vagueries in that. Game dev is not extremely well defined, but the consequences of imprecision do not have a lot on the line. Furthermore, games are pretty self-contained, so there's not a lot of guessing about what could happen or what the behavior of a counterparty system could be. With some pretty big exceptions, it is all there for the LLM to see in C++ (or whatever).
