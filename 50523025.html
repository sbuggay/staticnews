While Comcast has deployed plenty of fiber throughout the US, its cable Internet service makes the final connection to homes with coaxial cable. This results in upload speeds being far slower than download speeds. In contrast, fiber-to-the-home service can provide symmetrical upload and download speeds.

You can get symmetric DOCSIS, and apparently Comcast do offer that service[1]. Likewise, FTTH can be asymmetric if they set the traffic shaping that way. IMHO the only things that matter to the average user about an Internet connection are bandwidth, volume, and availability. As long as it terminates in WiFi or a standard Ethernet connector and passes IP traffic at the rate I paid for, I personally don't care what the physical infrastructure beyond the demarc is.

In fact, anecdotes from a few people I know have been that service interruptions actually increased after they upgraded to FTTH from DOCSIS.

> As long as it terminates in WiFi or a standard Ethernet connector and passes IP traffic at the rate I paid for, I personally don't care what the physical infrastructure beyond the demarc is.

I get that attitude, but coax connections are fragile in a way that fiber is not. Water infiltration, bad crimping jobs, unterminated ends in the neighbor's house all cause signal degradation that is extremely time and labor intensive to identify and fix.

Cable is an excellent solution for distributing broadcast analog radio signals over a private line cheaply and efficiently, but two-way digital signals over the same medium aren't as robust as their fiber-based counterpart.

ISP outages are one thing, but if you ever experience service degradation—and not a full outage, but tear-your-hair-out crap like "my internet goes down for 15 seconds once every 16 hours with no discernible pattern"—and it ends up being some particularly coax-flavored problem, you'll understand what I'm talking about.

martinald|root|parent|next|

Well firstly, DOCSIS is becoming an increasingly niche technology, and niche technology in telecoms means expensive. There are probably 10,000x more (XGS)G-PON CPEs being manufactured than DOCSIS4.0 ones. I assume similar scale for PON headend equipment.

Secondly, PON basically has no powered equipment outside the 'exchange', which can serve 30k+ customers. DOCSIS requires loads of powered equipment, amplifiers, CMTS etc. This then costs money to power (and in high temps, cool) and more importantly breaks down more often. DOCSIS4.0 requires a lot of upgrades to the 'last mile' part so most telcos have decided to just go the whole way and replace with PON instead of doing all that work and getting none of the operational cost savings.

The issue is the faster you push DOCSIS the more it shows up any problems in your last mile setup.
