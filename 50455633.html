Just yesterday I saw a talk from Spotify where they presented their own fast retrieval mechanism "Parallel Fusion Router" [1]. It's also in the "learned retrieval" paradigm - in their case they use a teacher LLM to train a much smaller router LLM how to retrieve, using an LLM-as-a-judge based reward function.

From my own experience, these learned methods from Apple, Spotify and others, are highly relevant to hyperscale only - when you are dealing with hundreds of millions of items to choose from (or more), and require super high relevance in the top-5. For all other use cases (up to a few million items) I found that just building a BM25 index, and giving the LLM a BM25 "search tool" and casting a "wide net" wins every single time compared to learned methods (though the latter are more publishable in recsys and icml).

Complexity of the original information and what accuracy means for your use case also comes into play I suppose.

I've been experimenting with hybrid memory retrieval using SQLite FTS5/BM25 for lexical search and dense embeddings from Nomic Embed Text v1.5 (768D), all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (384D), and Qwen3-Embedding (0.6B), with NumPy cosine similarity, Matryoshka-style dimensional truncation (64D → 768D reranking), and optional graph-based candidate expansion. In one 40-query benchmark, combining retrieval channels improved candidate recall from 30/40 with compact vectors alone to 40/40, although Top-1 accuracy was only 33/40. Another metric I was looking at was number of final candidates found to filter. If its very high, you can get better accuracy, but at the expense of resource use, and it can also lead to worse accuracy if your tying to choose top result efficently.. it makes a needle in a needle stack.

jillesvangurp|parent|next|

You definitely should use grep wherever you can (i.e. simple, unranked term searches) to 1) exclude things you are definitely not care about 2) to include things you definitely do care about. The reason is that this is cheap and easy and it reduces the overall results you still need to dig through. And because everything else is much more expensive this is going to help reduce the amount of compute needed from that. And results are cacheable as well which helps you with repeat searches for similar things.

For ranked searches, you can do more than one and combine their results. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) is a common approach to do that. A simple ranked lexical query and one or more vector searches. Can be combined to amplify results that perform well across multiple of these.

RRF is really easy: for each search result, take its position in each ranked list, add 60, and divide 1 by that number. Add up those values across all lists to get the final score. Results that rank highly in multiple lists get higher scores. The magical number of 60 dampens the effect of highly ranked results biasing the outcome to much. It's not going to be a good approach for all data sets. But it works surprisingly well and it's stupidly easy to implement.

For term search, expending some AI and other cleverness at indexing time to e.g. calculate categorizations is very helpful. If you can reduce your result set with some simple term searches, it might be small enough to pass to an LLM directly and skip ranked searches entirely. Using vector search as magic pixie dust is generally going to work a lot better if you can filter and combine it with a lexical search. Using e.g. a decision model at index time to calculate some categorizations and classifications is relatively cheap and easy relative to calculating vector embeddings.
