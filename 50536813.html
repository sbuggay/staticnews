From the comic: "Over the course of a year, Io's orbital time varied by 22 minutes".

This sentence implies that at some point in the year the time it takes for Io to do one single orbit, as viewed from earth is 22 minutes longer. That's not true. This is not a good explanation comic.

What happens is that if you have a clock that is very accurate and has a drift of less than a minute for a whole year, and you measure the exact moment that Io's eclipse happens (every 42.5 hours) over a full year, you might expect that it's every 42.5 hours all the way through the year. But what you actually see is that this moment varies over the course of a year by 22 minutes - it's later if Jupiter is far away.

Now you might ask how can somebody create such an accurate clock - in the 17th century? He used a pendulum clock which had a drift of like a second in 6 hours, and at the beginning of each night, he corrected that clock based on some other daily occurring measurement of the night sky, like when a certain star crosses a fixed point when viewed from his observatory.

Except the explanation doesn't make any sense. A period is a period. Earth doesn't look like its spinning half as fast if you are 1 light-day away...

The real effect is actually to do with making a graph and noticing an oscillation in the phase of the orbit. In particular the cumulative phase shifts that add up as earth moves further away and the cumulative small phase shifts that add up as earth moves closer to Jupiter. This looks like a sine wave.

Like if someone before you made a table of predicated date and times of io transits (Cassini's constant-period tables) based on the assumption that the oscillation is jitter-free, you could actually realize that there is this +-11 minute jitter in the real measurements after collecting a lot of samples.

There are lots of these textbook simplifications that make a line fitting problem look like some magic 2 point measurement impossibility.

Like the Millikan’s oil drop experiment wasn't some magic nonsense where you figure out how to balance a single drop in mid air.

The Cavendish weighing the earth wasn't a static twist on some wire where you cannot possibly measure the torsion constant precisely enough. It involved measuring a peak to peak deflection of some small oscillation.

The Foucault rotating mirror was not magically shooting a discrete laser pulse and measuring an RPM, as it came back in the second gap it was realising that you could modulate the returning intensity as you ramped angular frequency. Fitting angular velocity against harmonic number was what gives you time of flight not a single tooth.

adrian_b|parent|next|

The pendulum clock had been invented by Christiaan Huygens a couple of decades earlier.

It became quickly widespread, especially at astronomical observatories.

It is likely that the recent availability of accurate pendulum clocks was essential for Rømer's measurements, so such a result about the speed of light would have been impossible for the previous generation of astronomers.

Nonetheless, inside a fixed astronomical observatory, where there has been built a meridian circle with a telescope, and if there is available a good catalogue of stars, it is possible to measure the current "sidereal time" and use differences in sidereal times to compute the corresponding differences in time, even without a clock. The sidereal time is not a time, but the angle of rotation of the Earth vs. the stars. The rotation of the Earth vs. the stars is much more uniform than the apparent movement of the Sun on the sky, which determines the days.

So it would have been possible, but in a much more difficult way, to measure accurately the duration of some astronomical events even without clocks, but only with accurate instruments for measuring angles, used to determine the celestial coordinates of the stars, then compute the difference in the transit times of some stars whose transits coincided with the beginning and the end of the duration that must be measured, from the angles between them.

When good clocks are available, it is easier to measure the transit times of the stars and compute the angles between them from the times, than the reverse procedure, which would be needed without good clocks.

dghlsakjg|parent|prev|next|

The eclipse is the same length. When an observer on Earth sees it will vary since our distance to Jupiter varies.

Think about lightning and thunder. The thunder happens the instant the lightning does (the lightning being the eclipse). When you hear the thunder depends on the you, the observer's, position. Imagine we know exactly where and when the lightning/eclipse happens. If hearing the thunder (seeing the eclipse) happens later than that time, we can then calculate the speed of sound based on our distance from the event, and the time we observed it. The final trick is that they didn't know when the eclipse would happen exactly, but they did know when it would be observed by an observer in a given location. If you then observe it from a second location that is farther away from the eclipse, you can determine the speed of light. They used the fact that the earth moves through space to change locations enough for the time differential to be super obvious.

Accurate clocks aren't super duper necessary. You just need one that can be accurate enough for a few hours (since you can fix your time based on celestial observations several times per day). Pendulum clocks were invented about 20 years before this discovery and were accurate to about 1 minute of drift per 24h.

To add an even more fascinating take;

The "speed of light" is not a speed like the commonly understood speed of an object (eg. ball/car/etc). It is all mind-bending but worth trying to get a grasp on.

"c" is a geometric property of the "unified spacetime" fabric and is actually the conversion factor between space (3 dims) and time (1 dim). See Spacetime Diagram - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime_diagram. Thus there is a "motion through time" and a "motion through space" (this is the commonly understood meaning of the term motion).

This article explains it - https://www.aoc.nrao.edu/~smyers/courses/astro12/speedofligh...

One way to understand this; when an object moves through spacetime its combined rate of motion (i.e. in space + in time) is always fixed to c. So when an object is at rest all its motion is "motion through time" but when it starts accelerating in space the "motion through time" is converted into "motion through space" and in the limit becomes c. This is why you have length contraction and time dilation at speeds close to c.

It's not really possible to measure the time it takes light to move from point A to B. You can really only measure the time it takes for light to move from point A to B and back.

But you can do measure the difference between light going from point A to point B and light going from point A to point C to point B (and adding point C in the reverse direction as well) and I think you can use that to get reasonable confidence that light has constant speed in a given medium in all directions.

But this is totally relevant for trying to figure out asymmetric delivery delays in packet switched networks... You can't measure the delay directly, and if you use the network to synchronize your clocks, you can't use the clocks to measure the delay either, because most synchronization assumes symmetric delay. You need an externally synchronized clock, like a GPS reference etc.

Maxatar|root|parent|next|

Usually when people are introduced to the issue of measuring the one-way speed of light, one of the first ideas that comes to mind is exactly what you suggest, and it's a very good first thought, but of course it doesn't work since implicit in your approach is an assumption about what the one-way speed of light already is.

As you "slowly" move the two clocks away from one another, the two clocks will no longer be synchronized due to time dilation, and so you need to account for time dilation, but in order to account for time dilation you need to already know what the one-way speed of light is.

I put "slowly" in quotes because why would it possibly matter whether you move clocks slowly or quickly? It's the principle of the matter that is at issue, not the practicality.


cyberax|root|parent|next|

> As you "slowly" move the two clocks away from one another, the two clocks will no longer be synchronized due to time dilation, and so you need to account for time dilation, but in order to account for time dilation you need to already know what the one-way speed of light is.

That's why I said "slowly" so that the effect of the time dilation can be kept negligible. If you insist that you MUST account for it, get an additional clock that you move at a different velocity. It'll provide you another data point for the system of equations that you can solve to get the "c" value.

You can measure velocity precisely without any reference to the speed of light by timing how long (according to the clock itself) it takes for it to traverse a given length.

To make it even more watertight, do the experiment in an inertial reference frame with pre-accelerated clocks. Launch two clocks with arbitrary but different velocities along a race track. At the start of the track you have the pulse source along with the reference clock, you synchronize with it as your moving clocks cross the starting line.

Then you measure how long it takes for each moving clock (according to its measurement!) to cross the finish line. This will provide you enough data to solve the equation for the time dilation.

Maxatar|parent|next|

This is a subtle misunderstanding. As you approach the speed of light, physical processes including clocks or other time measuring devices appear to slow down relative to an observer. Exceeding the speed of light, at least as a conceptual generalization, doesn't mean that you end up going back in time and experiencing past events like Back to the Future, rather it would allow for future events to influence the outcome of past events, what is called retrocausality [1].

Retrocausality is not the same as time travel. With time travel it's as if you get to go back in time and reexperience things that happened in the past, which is not what faster than light travel implies. Retrocausality is more about things happening in the present that are influenced by future events, as opposed to causality where only past events can influence the outcome of future events.

[1] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrocausality
