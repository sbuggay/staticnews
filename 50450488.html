A complication on a wristwatch is anything other than telling time. Phases of the moon? Tides? Calendars? Complications. They make the innards more complex and fragile and difficult to build and repair. Complexity is the inherent difficulty in building the thing.

Together, what that's saying is that sometimes the minimal baseline complexity is going to be pretty high. If you're making a library to support all of HTTP/2, it's going to have lots of codepaths, and that's unavoidable. It's a complex problem requiring a complex solution. But consider SMTP, the "simple" mail transfer protocol. The protocol itself is stupidly simple. I can use — and have used — telnet to port 25 to send email. But because the protocol is so simple, implementing it is extremely complicated. Were the protocol a little more complex, the implementations could be vastly simpler.

Or perhaps more simply, if you're writing a textual protocol, define it as using UTF-8 encoding and move on. UTF-8 is more complex than simpler encodings like ASCII. Allowing your clients and servers to decide that they'd rather support something goofy, like Shift JIS or UTF-16, just makes everything vastly more complicated. Accept that the complexity of UTF-8 is inherent and sufficient, pay the cost, and then let all of your consumers be much simpler.

jacobolus|root|parent|prev|next|

The two words are both centuries old, so have had many chances for various authors and fields to adopt them for differing purposes, sometimes in contradictory ways. So for example a "complex system" (in the sense of the Santa Fe Institute) means something moderately different than what Tim Peters means. In plain English, they are broadly synonymous, so if you were writing a newspaper story you shouldn't expect readers to find subtle differences between them.

But if you know somewhat what implication a particular writer intends in drawing a distinction between the two words, then there's no inherent problem. "Simple is better than complex. Complex is better than complicated." is a pithy way to express a valuable idea:

We should try to make our interfaces, data structures, implementations, systems, etc. "simple", that is, dealing with just one thing at a time, in a clear and straightforward way, with few components each with a well defined purpose, etc. Sometimes, however, there is inherent difficulty in a problem or in the world requiring many interacting pieces, but we should still aim to avoid making things "complicated", that is, tangled, messy, difficult to reason about, ambiguous, unpredictable, gratuitously mixing unrelated concerns, etc.

zestyping|root|parent|next|

This will probably sound crazy to some of you, but I believe the introduction of the boolean type was a mistake.

Every primitive type has a null value, and the "if" statement tests for such a value. Whether a value is true or false is a property of the value. Notice that "true" and "false" are adjectives, not nouns.

That is a simple, internally consistent model with one concept of trueness.

The introduction of the boolean type made this a lot messier: it makes "if" look like type coercion, and introduces two different ways to think of boolean-ness (as an adjective or as a noun) which you must hold in your head at the same time. For example, it became possible to compare things as equal to True or False, doubling the number of ways to express a condition: "if x" and "if x == True" are now very similar, but subtly different, ways to express the same thing. The fact that we have to talk about things as "truthy" or "falsy" as if these were different from "true" and "false" is a further illustration of the mess this has created.

If we had stuck with 0 and 1, we would have avoided all this confusion. It is imperfect but simple; what we ended up with is still imperfect and needlessly complicated.

In the 1990s I was a novice developer, though I considered myself a hotshot because I was better than those around me in college and grad school.

I was an active participant in the comp.lang.python Usenet channel in the late 1990s.

I learned much from reading Tim Peters' posts. To many, including me, he was affectionately referred to as "Uncle Timmy."

One of the things I learned was that I still had much to learn about the art of programming.

Much of the little I know about numerical methods comes from reading his posts. It was fun to read his stories about Kendall Square Research and the (for me) old days of computing.

I remember posting questions about things I didn't understand, which he figured out and explained. [1]

I remember bringing up concepts I thought were specialized and esoteric, only to find that it was actually just my lack of experience. He gently corrected misapprehensions like mine. [2]

He wrote a lot on comp.lang.python. If I recall correctly, at the 7th International Python Conference in Houston, November 1998, there was Festschrift in his honor. He would use <wink> in his messages, and often end with something like "let's-hear-it-for-lambda-in-curly-assignment-stmts-ly y'rs - tim" (that's from the posting where he outlines the Zen of Python). I wondered if they were collected by looking for messages containing an '-ly' only to have Andrew Kuchling remind me about the "From" header. <wink>

Here's an interview of him at PyCon 2006, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1wAOy88WxmY

[1] In one case, I was parsing a file format where a field was usually three spaces, but was sometimes an integer. I couldn't figure out why one parser was so much faster than the other.
