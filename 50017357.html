Artists value "originality"

From what I have seen over the past thirty years, the vast majority of programmers are generally not interested in "originality". They might even challenge the very notion that software can be "original"; certainly I've seen this perspective in HN comments over the years. These programmers are eager to copy and may even pride themselves on fast they can do it ("implement" a preexisting idea used by someone else)

Even more, culturally, these programmers are generally not fond of other programmers who operate in their own, "original" way. There is a gross amount of peer pressure to conform to "convention" for lack a better term. By and large, they conform; independent thinking is not popular

There are a small number of programmers who elevate programming to an art form. But this is not the majority and these individuals are not representative of programmer behaviour in general

NB. I'm not arguing for or against orginality, utilitarianism, etc. Everything has its proper place. I'm just drawing attention to a frequent difference between programmers and artists

> Code (and math, for that matter) is almost the same except for one key difference: they are not easy to appreciate without deliberate training, training which is often far to the side of anything most people need/want to deal with.

I would argue that this exists on a spectrum. There's lots of art and music that's pretty difficult to appreciate without deliberate training, and indeed there are whole categories of music which we would probably call music for musicians.

For example, the album Kind of Blue by Miles Davis is loved by many and is considered by most people to be the best gateway record into the world of jazz, because it's easy to listen to and appreciate. An album like Sketches of Spain, and especially an album like Bitches Brew, are more difficult for the average non-musician listener to get into.

An even more out-there example for music is Allan Holdsworth. In certain circles, he's a well-known and well-regarded name, considered highly influential. Most people have no idea who he is or what he did. (A bit like that cutaway in Family Guy where they jumped to Benjamin Disraeli and he simply said: "you don't even know who I am!").

Similarly, in art and painting, you often need training to understand why something was especially impressive. Now that we have photography, some old paintings can feel "boring" if you don't know how to appreciate the level of difficulty in producing a near-photorealistic work in the 1700s.

Conversely, some math is very easy to appreciate without training! I had a buddy who I got excited about math simply by explaining the Fibonacci sequence and the golden ratio, then showing trivial examples of how they appear in nature. My friend got lit up about math that day in a whole new way.

Coding is not really programming. It is certainly a highly adjacent skill, but coding is not the same as programming. To quote Leslie Lamport, coding is to programming as typing is to writing.
