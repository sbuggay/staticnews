Part of the importance is due to the fact that while the space shuttle was a technical success in terms of being able to build and operate a reusable upper stage, it was hella expensive. Here's just one big hairy example: to fly manned missions, starship either needs to be redesigned to accommodate an escape system, or it needs to show a level of reliability and safety never previously achieved by a rocket.

tristanj|root|parent|next|

The space shuttle program was doomed from the start because it was designed as a manned vehicle. Unlike Starship, it couldn't fly unmanned.

Shuttle had to fly humans, and had to maintain its human-rated safety certification. This meant that any design change or modification had to be tested extensively to ensure it didn't kill the crew. This took multiple design iterations, took a long time, and was not cheap.

After the initial design of the ship, it made the program very risk averse. Changing anything required a lot of testing, so it was easier to not change it. Shuttle got stuck because it became too difficult and expensive to iterate; Congress did not give NASA enough funds to overcome this, so the project stagnated.

Because Starship flies without humans on board, it doesn't have these issues. SpaceX can make radical design changes, perform minimal testing, then put these changes on the next flight and immediately see what happens. Sometimes this fails catastrophically, which is the main reason why Starship keeps blowing up. They can also push the ship close to failure to see how it performs, without worrying about humans on board. This is why Starship burned up multiple times when returning from orbit - SpaceX intentionally ran the ship through excessively steep reentry profiles to measure how the heat shield performed.

The overall result is that SpaceX can iterate on Starship faster and more cheaply than NASA ever could with Shuttle.

Not an aerospace engineer, but roughly:

I don't think catching (vs. landing) is a "new space era" game changer, but it offers several advantages in a game (chemical rocketry) where some margins are very thin -

- No need for a separate landing pad. Which might need its own flame trench, for reasons others have noted.

- You already have the chopsticks for pre-launch stacking.

- Because you're stacking with chopsticks, you already have those hard points on the Starship. (Hard points are spots engineered to bear heavy loads, in contrast to fragile areas. On large aerospace structures, hard points are expensive in engineering, manufacturing, and weight.)

- Landing legs on a hard surface require larger safety margins. Or a lot of springs, shock absorbers, and such. (Falcon 9's landing legs include those.) That's because the transient loads during an off-by-5-cm landing can be 3X or more the static loads on your landing legs. Vs. if you land on the chopsticks - all the springs and stuff can be built into them. With chemical rocketry's thin margins, not needing to carry that extra weight to space and back is a serious win.

To have manned missions you're going to need redundancy. That means more than one catcher available most likely. You need an escape system if the booster itself is faulty. Was Space Shuttle safe enough for human crews?

Space Shuttle was a compromised design from the beginning. They originally wanted the Space Shuttle booster to also have wings and return to launch site (with a crew!). But they couldn't afford to develop two new vehicles, so they compromised by making the external tank as cheap as possible and adding solid rockets (which led to the Challenger disaster).

But their biggest mistake was having the engines on the orbiter (so they could be recovered) but not the fuel tank. That meant the orbiter had to hang on the side of the external tank, which made it vulnerable to foam strikes (leading to the Columbia disaster).

Landing with wings is aesthetically pleasing (compared to tower catches) but consider the waste: You have to boost the weight of the wings, landing gear, etc. all the way to orbit and then bring it all the way back. It's much more efficient to just bring fuel and use that for landing. And if your guidance is accurate enough, a pinpoint landing means you can land on the launch tower and not worry about landing legs.

> There weren't too many runways available to use as backups

In particular, because the shuttle used an all-but-obsolete terminal guidance system, called Microwave Landing System (MLS).

While MLS was intended to be the "next generation" instrument landing system (to replace VHF ILS approaches) for the entire national airspace system, MLS was basically only ever installed at a few runways intended as Shuttle abort locations, and Shuttle was basically the only aircraft that used it (aside from some other NASA test aircraft used to validate MLS approaches, and a couple other one-off airports).

It's a shame, too, because MLS is pretty technically interesting, allows other-than-straight-in approaches, and offers horizontal, vertical and high-precision distance guidance all in one package.

But the point is that Shuttle had very slim margins, so the fact that it landed horizontally very explicitly did not mean it could land "anywhere": It required an MLS approach. The plan for situations where an MLS runway was not available was to have astronauts bail out at altitude.

the satellites are life limited whether they have noticeable faults or not (mostly by the remaining propellant, but also by TID radiation and payload obsolescence), and any sensible repair-in-place mission aims to proactively repair satellites near the end of their practical life rather than reactively responding to issues. The robotics (and fluid transfer for refuelling) are basically there, you just need satellites with an architecture that enables repair/replacement/refuelling to move beyond demonstrators. As for whether it's worth it, it depends a lot on the satellite and the orbit. Starlink: probably not worth upgrading. A mission for one satellite to repair a lot of others is complex (it's basically an extra-hard version of the travelling salesman problem) but when the alternative is inserting many entirely new satellites into n orbits, sending a tiny fraction of the mass to n orbits via a reusable servicer starts to make a lot of sense...

dkbrk|parent|next|

They don't need to pay the mass penalty of landing legs, just some much lighter pins that stick out to the side.

The Falcon 9 landing legs were some excellent engineering, but scaled up to the size of starship they would weigh many tons (note the square-cube law makes them more unfavorable proportionally, not just massive considered amsolutely).

This would be a big deal for the booster, but it's absolutely vital for Starship itself because any additional dry mass subtracts directly from the payload (actually, it's even worse since it also increases the fuel needed for landing).

Another advantage is that it's better for reuse, as they don't have to spend hours or days restowing legs, recharging deployment cylinders and replacing crush cores.
