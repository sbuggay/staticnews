Sharlin|parent|prev|next|

A highway or railway curve between two straight segments is usually not a simple circle arc. If it were, the sudden discontinuity in higher derivatives (acceleration and jerk) at the transition point from straight to curved would feel uncomfortable, be harder to anticipate and slow down appropriately by a driver, and cause unwanted shear stresses to mechanical parts. So transition curves are used that smoothly vary the curvature from zero to that of the circular segment and back to zero again. The typical transition curve in engineering is called a clothoid, which is one of the squircle types mentioned in the article.

In digital graphic design curvature discontinuities don’t usually cause mechanical issues, but discontinuities in curved shapes can still look jarring – for whatever reason the visual system is rather good at detecting such sudden changes in shapes, even those that naively "should" look smooth.


srean|root|parent|next|

Ha! Must you tease me so ?

This is one of my pet fascinations (nerd snipe pits) - deciphering transition curve markings along railway tracks while travelling by train, preferably peeling an orange [*]. These transition curves are usually some approximation of an Euler Spiral (aka Cornu Spiral, Clothoid), typically a cubic parabola. To compute the Euler spiral as a closed form one gets into Fresnel integrals. Cubic parabolas are a lot easier to compute and for short arc lengths almost indistinguishable.

These get used in roller-coasters too, for pretty much the same reason (to avoid the "jerk")

What is not so well known is that Markov (of Markov chains) worked on the problem of optimal curves for joining straight railway tracks constrained by steering dynamics of trains. I think this was before his work on chains. His formulation was later rediscovered independently by Dubins.

alanbernstein|parent|prev|next|

On a rounded rectangle, the shape of the corner is continuous, but the curvature is not - it jumps from 0 to R. Rounded rectangles look fine, but if you're attuned to it, this subtle discontinuity can look just a little bit off.

A squircle is one (actually several variations) approach to making the curvature continuous.

I would say it's still a subjective aesthetic difference, but you can use math jargon to explain why you like one better than the other.


YuechenLi|parent|prev|next|

A regular rounded rectangle is G1 continuous while a squircle (superellipse) is G2 continuous, which means that it is not only continuous on position and tangent for its outline, but the curvature rate of change for the outline is also continuous, so it's smoother and doesn't have hard difference between "line" and "rounded corner" on the shape.
