Option trading was my first job.

Some things to note:

There's a reason why you need the squared term in the Taylor expansion, which is a thing called Ito's lemma that is the stochastic calculus version of the chain rule.

If you thought that it is a bit odd to assume constant volatility, you are right. People don't just use the BS formula to make a price for an option, but they DO use it to describe a price they made, funnily enough. So you would use whatever more complex model you wanted to come up with a number, and then back out the volatility via the BS formula to say what volatility that corresponds to.

In practice you end up with a surface, whose dynamics will vary depending on what you're looking at.

Option traders are speculating on volatility (not direction), and the mechanism is like this:

- You can buy a call and short the market, or buy a put and go long the market. If you draw this on a graph, you can satisfy yourself that this roughly gives you a u-shape such that you are locally neutral in the price, ie a small up or down does very little to your portfolio. (BTW, this is why traders call all the high strikes calls and the low strikes puts, it doesn't actually matter whether it is the right to buy or sell the asset, and the in-the-money side is just more cash for the same risk)

- If the market moves and you own hedged options, your portfolio will be worth more regardless of whether it went up or down. You can then hedge again to re-seat the flat portion of the U-shape. If the market moves again, you do it again, locking in your gains.

- Why is this not free money? Well, we only looked at the price dimension. Every day you are long the option, it becomes worth a bit less, since there's less time left for it to move. This gets steeper and steeper with each day, since days are a constant length of time and you are losing a greater and greater proportion of the life of the option.

- So why is the opposite not free money? Well, the two effects push against each other. Sometimes, the movement is enough so that the loss on theta (time) is outweighed by the buying low and selling high. Other times, it is not.

> Black and Scholes' choice of of Brownian motion for the model is one I cannot swallow

Well its a great assumption and works most of the time. It is in fact, probably the right choice for pricing options in normal market conditions. If you have other needs thankfully we do have other models that are in use

- Merton jump-diffusion, this uses Poisson jumps (usually log-normal) to GBM. it shows Crash risk, and is used for short-dated options

- Heston, models using Stochastic volatility (CIR process) + correlation with the asset, is mostly used to model the Volatility smile/skew, term structure of vol

- Local volatility (Dupire), its advantage is that Volatility is a deterministic function of spot and time. It excels at modelling an Exact fit to the smile (used more for hedging/exotics).

- Kou jump-diffusion, I'll admit i don't use this and I'm not as familiar with it but its models using Double-exponential jumps and it excels when you need to model the skew awhile still being relatively tractable

EDIT

as someone asked. These are models that keep brownian motion but account for jumps.

We do have other models that replace the Brownian motion altogether. The most common are Levy based models such as Variance Gamma and Normal Inverse Gaussian models.

I can't speak to their use in the industry as I don't work for an option market maker.

And for completeness i should mention the very common Binomial / trinomial trees (Cox–Ross–Rubinstein, etc. family of models). THese provide a discrete approximation and can converge to Black–Scholes or be calibrated differently.

Their claim to fame was their ease of calculation back when this was a consideration.

seanhunter|parent|prev|next|

If you think about it, it's somewhat beside the point to take exception to the Brownian motion in Black-Scholes. The assumptions of constant volatility and the ability to trade in continuous time are far more unrealistic. The point is, it's a model and the real breakthrough in the original Black/Scholes/Merton paper is the idea of pricing using replication in continuous time. If you're able to hedge in continuous time, the error introduced by the difference between a Brownian motion and a distribution with higher kurtosis is necessarily smaller, because you will hedge it away in tiny bits. This is why billions of dollars are traded using local vol models like Dupire every day - notwithstanding the assumption of normality, assuming vol is a locally deterministic function of price and time gives you a good enough model for most day to day applications where you don't need term skew (eg not forward-starting).

It's kind of hard to come up with good models for continuous random time-series. With a few reasonable demands you end up with a Levy process, which you can decompose into Brownian motion with jumps. [1]

Some of these demands may look unreasonable if you purely see the variable as 'the price' but become reasonable when you view the variable as 'the current state of affairs'. I think the efficient market hypothesis is that these two are similar enough, but that is indeed a bit of a weak spot.

Anyway once you have stationarity and independence you start running into a few issues because if Var(X_(t+s) - X_t) is infinite for some s, then it is also infinite for s/k for all integers k. And then you get that even the smallest increment in time gives infinite variance which becomes messy.

[1]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9vy_process

gituliar|parent|next|

Just a few remarks for clarity.

The Black-Scholes _formula_ for the European option price is a particular solution of the Black-Scholes _equation_.

The beauty of this PDE equation is that it also describes various exotic options (American, Bermudian, Asian, etc.), volatility structures, multi-asset options, etc. In practice, such products are priced numerically with Monte-Carlo or finite-difference methods, but the equation remains the same.
