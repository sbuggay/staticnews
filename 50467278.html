a passing wave, even a distant plane wave, will couple to the EM surface waves of the can surface.

also novices always home in and hyperfocus on reflectivity and transmission attenuation lengths, which works when done properly, but is a complete PITA without using absorbers.

simply placing an unopen plastic water bottle inside the cavity means that any EM energy that does make its way in, would be absorbed, instead of repeatedly reflecting from your perfect metallic reflector walls...

Consider a small 2D patch of metal sheet with some resistivity: any attenuated tails of EM waves will induce currents in the metal, and those currents can flow in all directions in the sheet.

Now consider the same metal sheet with a very fine unintentional slit, while currents can still run parallel to the slit, the metal doesn't admit significant current (ignore electron tunneling across the slit) across the slit, this means a current can circulate around the slit, this is why some of the commenters here point out the issues with slits. Simply placing mating metal lids / doors / ... against metal containers does not guarantee identical conductivity across the formed slit: imperfect planarity (think placing the Alps upside down on the Pyrenees), different metallic phases (annealing etc) at the surface, the spontaneous formation of an oxide layer (aluminum has a thin corundum layer, iron/steels/metals oxidize,...), protective coating layers, ... all contribute to the failure to text-book-reflect an EM waved with a perfect isotropic conductor.

Suppose you wish to protect your empire from an overrun by the barbarians: proper border control is important, but in practice imperfect, so you need a way to absorb or apprehend any barbarians that made their way in.

Animats|parent|next|

The Oahu info is interesting. That was a series street light system, something rarely seen any more. The idea is that all the bulbs are wired in series, the street light system is a big loop, and there's a constant-current transformer at the head end, providing a few thousand volts.

Constant current lighting is kind of a mirror world of normal constant voltage systems. Each lamp is equipped with not a fuse, but an insulating shorting disk between two contacts, wired in parallel with the bulb. In normal operation, each lamp sees 1/N of the system voltage, usually 120V. If a lamp burns out, no current flows, all lights go out, the failed lamp's insulating disk sees the full system voltage, the high voltage arcs through the insulating disk, the contacts close, and the dead lamp is thus bypassed. Now the lamp string has less resistance, current goes up a bit, the constant-current transformer adjusts, and things go back to normal, minus one lamp. The Oahu system had a mix of incandescent and mercury vapor lamps, running at different voltages. complicating the situation.

This is apparently a bad case for EMP vulnerability. But all that happened was that some of the shorting disks at the voltage conversion points arced through and cut that part of the loop out of circuit.

Those old series lighting systems are almost totally gone. But one application for series lighting remains - airport runway lights. That's because it's the easiest way to get uniform voltage for a string of lights several miles long. No need for a distribution transformer every few hundred feet.
