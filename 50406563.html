Historically, that happens now and then. Examples include color television cameras and fluorescent lamp ballasts.

RCA developed the first good color TV cameras. They were monsters. Originally, electronic TV (as opposed to mechanical scanning) used Zworykin's iconoscope. This needed huge amounts of light. It didn't have a photo-multiplier stage to boost sensitivity, but it did accumulate charge from light over the whole frame time. There was an alternative approach, Farnsworth's image dissector, which also needed huge amounts of light. It had a photo-multiplier stage but only the light from the dot currently being scanned by the scanning beam mattered.

So RCA threw money at the problem. They developed the image orthicon camera tube. It combined the advantages of both approaches, integration over a full frame and a photo-multiplier stage. It was insanely expensive, costing thousands of dollars in the 1950s. It needed about twenty different voltages and multiple independent power supplies. It worked reasonably well.

Then came color. That took three image orthicons (RGB) inside a box the size of a small refrigerator, along with optics to split the incoming image into three colors. Plus a rack of analog electronics per camera, connected through a large cable. Plus an alignment problem from hell, with a huge number of adjustment knobs.[1]

Scaling worked! From the 1950s to the 1970s, color TV broadcasting was powered by those monsters.

Now, independent of this, other people were working on other approaches. Vidicons, silicon detectors inside camera tubes, and finally fully solid state image sensors. Eventually, in 1983, after thirty years of image orthicon monsters, RCA produced a usable solid state camera. Today, a cell phone camera costs about US$10 in bulk, and it's far better than the monsters.

It took thirty years of low-priority development to get past the giant image orthicon cameras.

Electronic fluorescent lamp ballasts had a different problem. That was a suppressed invention. The magnetic ballast industry was dominated by Magnetek. Someone invented a solid state replacement, which was smaller and cheaper, and licensed their patent to Magnetek for royalties. Magnetek just sat on it, not wanting to gut their core market. The inventor hired Townsend, Townsend and Crew in Palo Alto and sued. (Read this in the law firm's brag book in their office.) Eventually, the inventor got a hundred million or so, solid state ballasts went into production, and Magnetek seems to be gone. (The company called Magnetek today, which makes crane controls, is an offshoot of a Litton unit, and seems to be unrelated.)

There are also parallels from the steam locomotive industry, the Baldwin Locomotive Works being the best known. Baldwin actually built the first Diesel locomotive. They made a few half-hearted tries at Diesels over several decades, then gave up and went back to steam. Baldwin ceased all locomotive production in 1956. The corporation lingered on for a while, not doing much.

[1] https://www.tvcameramuseum.org/rca/tk40a/pix/rcatk40aart.pdf

Suppressed inventions aren't "very rare" per se. You simply don't hear about those that aren't being rescinded while the inventor is still alive.

According to official data from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO), there were 6,653 active secrecy orders in effect at the end of fiscal year 2026. Roughly 100 to 350 new secrecy orders are added every year. Many remain locked away for decades or more.

If the U.S. government puts a secrecy order on a patent application against the inventor's will, they are legally forbidden from talking about it. The inventor cannot talk about how it works, publish research on it, sell it, or market it. augment_me|root|parent|next|

"Smarter" is ill-defined.

Are you smart if you see that cognitive scientists had something figured out, connected it to something in your field of chemistry and made a breakthrough treatment for some disease?

Does any of our existing intelligence build on completely unknown information? You could argue that any new solution/emergent intelligence is an association of existing information in new ways.

It also brings us to what is brute forcing? Humans are brute forcing stuff most of the time. You will have 10 stupid thoughts for every good thought. 10 stupid ideas for every good idea, you will discard most of them so fast that you won't think of that you did that. It's very rare, if not near impossible, that a non-mathematician with no prior knowledge, goes into a room on day 1 of deciding to think about a conjecture they don't know anything about, and solve it on the blackboard. They need to use their collect associations, formalize their axioms, think of first principles (known information), and associate it.

So to answer your question you need to answer:

1) What does "smarter" refer to? Is it accumulation of information about the universe?(if yes, a database is smart) Is it an efficient, abstracted representation of accumulated information? Is it prediction of the future state of the universe given a learned abstraction?

Or going further, is it solving tasks useful for the human in the world? Is it acting as an agent with it's own goals and interests in the universe? What if the goals are hard for humans to understand?

Does it count as smart if you make an evolutionary approach that spawn 1000 instances and the most fit one survives and spawns 1000 mutations of itself?

2) What does "genuinely new approaches" refer to? Is it fine to use math axioms to prove things? Does interpolation between two existing solutions count as new? At what granularity of concept does novelty happen (Does using two higher abstraction concepts, like combining some new, modern neural network architecture with a database count as a new thing?), even if the underlying lower abstraction of the same information can be expressed as storage, latency and compute, and is not novel?

---

As you can imagine, this is all not really defined or agreed upon. Here are some examples of projects that are set up to drive these ideas:

The Hutter Prize poses that intelligence is defined by compression, so if you manage to compress the English Wikipedia efficiently, you are closer to better intelligence (http://prize.hutter1.net/)

Compute: Intel CPU

The ARC-AGI foundation poses that being able to be goal-oriented and understand higher level abstractions is intelligence: https://arcprize.org/leaderboard

Compute: NVIDIA T40s

Modded NanoGPT competition poses that intelligence is minimizing entropy/loss over a dataset in the least amount of steps: https://github.com/kellerjordan/modded-nanogpt

Compute: 8x NVIDIA H100

---

All of this also exists in the context of the compute medium. It could be, that you need a certain capacity of memory/computation to perform whatever we have defined as intelligent tasks. Hutter Prize takes the bet of that to make intelligence, you don't need a lot of computation, an Intel CPU laptop and 50GB RAM should be enough.

A lot of the big labs are instead taking the bet on that you just scale everything, make trillion-sized models and things will fall out of that automatically, for this you will need a very different compute medium (data centers) compared to (a laptop).

Nevermark|prev|next|

I think the dichotomy of open-research vs. commercially-driven innovation might be easier to coordinate, with three stages, not two:

1. Open research: Great creative freedom, with great resource constraints. Lots of ideas, lots of things tried, at insignificant cost relative to commercial work.

2. Results filtering, vetting, regularization: Opportunistic selection of apparently successful work from stage 1, for tentative testing at increasing scale. The best ideas end up with moderate budgets that verify benefits, which are reworked for compatibility before any handoff to stage 3.

3. Commercial efforts: aimed strictly at improving quality and economics of current revenue sources.

Direct negotiation between #1 and #3 involves too much dissonance over globally complementary but locally incompatible values.

#2 can be a relatively small, high payoff group, keeping #1 and #3 from butting heads, while facilitating them both.
