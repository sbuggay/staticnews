I’m wondering if we read the same article because you are actually arguing for the same point that the author made. He called that surface-level, shiny stuff “decoration”.

I completely agree too that design should be about solving a problem so effectively and thoroughly that it becomes invisible, by the way. But I suspect that it’s not a controversial opinion among people reading articles about software design on a Friday night.

I actually think that point about decoration is partly a more general problem of experience signaling vs counter-signaling, and partly a matter of fashion.

The types of people who make really shiny stuff that doesn’t work are usually inexperienced. Making a button shiny and glossy is very simple and self-contained, so it’s something they can nerd out on while staying in their comfort zone.

Once you start thinking about design at the level of “what if we organized all our documentation as a tree of arbitrary depth instead of a List of Lists?” you have to start writing a lot of code across a lot of files, and think of all these edge cases and how your users will want to navigate between things, and implement collapsibility by either modifying a library intended for filesystems or write your own, and then some stuff on the backend that you don’t even know enough about to know whether it’s easy, hard, or impossible. You might struggle a lot to get anything working at all, or expend a lot of work but then get feedback that it’s bad or confusing. So you work on the small safe thing that you know you can’t fuck up (because it doesn’t matter).

But it’s also a signaling vs countersignaling thing. If you’re not confident in your skills or just genuinely have very little real experience then you’re trying to prove you can design something like a designer would, and do every thing you can to avoid being perceived as lacking skill. But the more confident and competent you become, the less you need to pass yourself as a designer, and the more you need to set yourself apart from other designers (often by breaking the rules).

Fashion also comes in to play because sometimes a certain kind of aesthetic becomes so commonplace that it’s seen as tacky and cheap. In my opinion, this is currently happening to the “shiny Vercel tutorial app” and “Framer landing page” aesthetic.

A problem I've seen in programming too: understanding and articulating the value of quality is necessary but nowhere near sufficient for actually doing good work! So you end up with folks who've gotten past the initial hurdle—developing taste and confidence—but, either individually or in an organizational context, are only able to do okay quality work. Not awful but not especially elegant or insightful either.

And so we get people making totally reasonable arguments about the value of design and then producing polished but tasteless and shallow corporate products. Or we get people who start understanding the value of maintainable, understandable code, but get stuck on design patterns, "clean code" rules and "best practices" rather than conceptually clear, coherent and effective code design.

I have some sympathy. Getting past that initial hurdle rests on skills and tacit knowledge that take time, practice and mentorship to build after you've developed an appreciation for good taste. And, especially in a corporate setting, you have to get most of that practice and mentorship in public.
