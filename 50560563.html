> Color Dreams hit on an audacious idea: wrap its games in Bible stories, sell them through Christian bookshops rather than games retailers, and bet that Nintendo would never risk the headlines of suing a maker of Christian children's games.

I’ve always had a slightly different impression: that Nintendo had already eliminated Color Dreams’ products from toy store shelves through economic pressure, by threatening to pull its own inventory. Wisdom Tree was able to succeed in the Christian bookstore market because those stores didn’t sell any Nintendo products anyway, so Nintendo couldn’t pressure them to stop selling Wisdom Tree titles.

These days when repeating stories like that, even if I’ve been repeating them for years, I like to refresh myself with some evidence that they’re true. I did find a 1990 article (“Behind the Nintendo Seal of Quality,” VideoGames & Computer Entertainment, Issue 20, p. 92: https://archive.gamehistory.org/item/26843147-5564-4a44-bbec...) that says:

“Are retailers pressured not to sell products by independents like Tengen and Color Dreams? This is a question that almost all those involved choose not to answer—or, for some reason, can’t.”

That’s not hard evidence, but it’s certainly suggestive, and from a contemporary source. (Best I could do in ten minutes of research!)

I owned Exodus as a kid, and probably still have the cartridge lying around somewhere.

The religious part was just a thin veneer. The background music was “Father Abraham,” and every five levels there was a quiz about the book of Exodus (answering correctly gave extra lives), and a nice still illustration of a Biblical scene. Otherwise it played like other arcadey games of the era.

The DOS versions of Exodus and some other Wisdom Tree games are available on Steam if you’d like to try them out, although I think the DOS version has aged the worst of all the Exodus ports: https://store.steampowered.com/app/624550/Spiritual_Warfare_...

In my opinion, the version of Exodus that’s aged the best audiovisually is the Genesis port (ha, the Genesis Exodus, how appropriate). Apparently it was rereleased on cartridge during a Kickstarter in 2016; wish I’d known at the time, I would love to have a copy.

If you’re curious enough to play a Wisdom Tree game today, I recommend Super 3D Noah’s Ark, a first‐person shooter using the Wolfenstein engine, even compatible with open source engines like ECWolf. https://maniacsvault.net/ecwolf/

Per the Wikipedia, it appears Wisdom Tree was formed both due to a perceived lack of Christian games (thus opening them to an unestablished market) while also providing ample avenue to bypass Nintendo’s restrictions (because Nintendo didn’t want to sue Christian businesses and hurt their image). Color Dreams, their parent, was formed by Dan Lawton in part as a means of sticking it to Nintendo because he disliked the licensing and lockout chips on the NES.

Ultimately they were correct: no good Christian games existed at the time. Unfortunately, they were also not the ones to bring them.

golbanno|root|parent|next|

Making a "good" religious games is challenging from a game-design perspective.

Most games are about competition or mastery. A naive goal of a religious game would be to teach submission to the Lord, which runs counter to granting the player a sense of empowerment through mastery or other forms of success.

Narrative or exploration games have more leeway in this regard. A recent award winning game that comes to mind is Indika, where you play as a nun, and falls in more of the "cautionary tale" camp.

It's a standout success, but I think that's precisely because "depressing tales of flawed characters" is not a sustainable, stand alone, video game genre. That seems to work better in media where you are not granted agency over the doomed protagonist.
