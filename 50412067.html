When I had started my PhD, I thought the writing part of a paper was just the last thing I needed to do to summarize my results, like a boring epilogue to the more exciting science. What surprised me was how much of the scientific process relied on my ability to convey my ideas to someone else. And to be able to do that you need clarity of mind, you need to have worked through multiple drafts to be able to get to your point as efficiently and clearly as possible. So many times I only discovered what I was actually researching during the writing part.

The problem with AI writing is that it makes people believe that writing is just writing and not a tool for thinking very deeply about the topic you are writing about. I am not completely opposed to AI assisted writing, but if I see AI writing, it’s telling me that the author tried to take a shortcut in their own thinking and it’s a good heuristic that the ideas will not be thought through enough to warrant my complete attention.

I spent an unreasonable amount of time recently thinking about how to color- (and symbol-) code my notes. I wanted to create a simple generally applicable system that I can use for any project.

For thinking, nothing beats handwriting. It's just the unconstrained freedom of movement in every direction, shape or form, and the lack of friction and distractions.

But then you sometime also want to organize or share stuff, so that's where digital comes in. I have an automation that takes a photo of my notes with my phone, uploads it to Obsidian, and OCRs it into markdown and tags it, so I basically end up with a "symbolic" 1-1 equivalent of analog handwriting + digital text

My system still evolves, but what I currently use is:

Color: - black = just "normal" text - red = critical/"must" - yellow = important/"should" - green = highlighted/"could"

Symbols: - bulletpoint [ ] task -> reminder

I also do brackets and underlines which translate to # heading1 and ## heading2 in markdown

I write by hand with a fountain pen with black ink + use 3 fineliners and small colored sticky flags.

Some observations: - yellow is not great on white paper due to bad contrast; it also rarely comes as ballpoint, but fineliner options are available - blue and green are visually very similar, to the point of kinda being redundant - for sharing with other people, the system must be as generally understandable as possible. Here the traffic norms such as ISO 7010 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_7010) could be considered - also there's the aspect of accessibility, with a relevant portion of population being colorblind - The simplest most effective system seems to be: one main color (black or blue) and one highlight color (red I think works best), plus a couple of symbols such as bullet point, task, reminder that translate well into markdown

Writing is a good thinking tool.

But, to the claim of how to think in the age of LLMs, I think it’s worth contrasting the other approach, which is a dialog with the LLM; two things are needed IMO to keep things honest:

First, you need to keep asking the LLM to explain things, until you understand what it did. Lots of angles here; have the LLM make a design/product doc, write one-off architecture explainers, write throwaway data-viz tools to explain the data distribution. Then ask a fresh copy to adversarially review the core concepts. Try asking a different frontier model too.

Second, you need to push for “proof”, in other words, ask for demos and try it yourself. Imagine yourself a CEO or VC; how do you know if the output of an entire org is vaporware? One thing to beware is launching into a field where you don’t have expertise and can’t actually validate the output. This takes time of course, but for example if you write a stress test script and get CPU/QPS numbers, these complex validators are IME quite reliable when you apply the same rigor from 1) above to their construction.

Words are most effective when they buck the trend in just the right way.

I'll use the conference room example for clarity, even though a lot of people still work remote these days.

Reading the room could be as subtle yet significant as reacting to the speaker that was on immediately before you. It could be reacting to the temperature of the room or the catered lunch everyone just had. It can be noticing or participating in or critiquing a peculiarity of the audience (hawaiian shirt fridays, etc.). It can also be a million other things and they'll never let you get away with choosing at random. You have to just "get it" or you're going to be ignored at best.

You're at once trying to both signal in-group status while also asserting your own identity and setting the tone for everything you'll be saying after. Remote work has its own idiosyncrasies, but regardless, you need a pulse for this kind of work. And, all this is just the introduction. What about emphasis choices in the rest of your writing/speaking?

Trying to pin that down is simply not logical and it's not just a matter of "adding" a handful of tricks unless you want to alienate everyone. Sometimes it's also the things you don't say or do that make the difference. You also have to accept that you can't please everyone.
