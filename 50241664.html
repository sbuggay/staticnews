jillesvangurp|prev|next|

Debugging css issues related to print can be a bit of a PITA. Adding print options for dynamic content in a full stack page is not technically that hard. But getting it looking right can be a bit fussy. If you don't care about what things look like once printed, it's easy. But if you do care, it actually needs a bit of consideration.

I've done a few custom things with an application that generates labels for a label printer using just css and javascript. This is not that hard. But it does involve dealing with a lot of weirdness related to native print dialogs, vs. browser specific print dialogs (and their print previews), feature interaction with native printer settings and their scaling options, browsers adding footers to the printed document, and just general weirdness related to layout issues, page sizes, margins, etc.

My general feeling dealing with this is that CSS for print just gets a lot less scrutiny than regular CSS by browser developers.

One of the hacks you can do is generate a dom tree just for print. Replace the root of your dom tree with that, trigger the print, and then put the old dom tree back (with your full stack application and all the rest). Seems very hacky to have to do it like that. But it kind of works and is simple enough to do. And the benefit is that it stops the rest of your application (which probably does not take print into account at all) out of the equation and will stop that from interfering with your print layout. Conceptually, makes you wonder why you can't just hand a separate document tree + CSS to the print api call. This is a lot simpler than having one unified dom tree and one flattened unified CSS file. Separation of concerns is a good thing.

For a couple of applications I have built recently, an offline view of the data in print form has been part of the design requirement. It's still reasonably common in situations where people will meet in real life and a physical clipboard will be needed with a register or some physical signatures are needed, or in situations where paper goes home with people from meetings for them to read and sign. So, lots of school/community/disclaimer tasks where you can't presume people have devices (or a device wouldn't be usable/practical/useful, especially in outdoor contexts).

These days PDFs are also useful for marking up in some devices.

For my own work, making an app printable is a basic thing I always do — it's part of the normal process.

But that often means little more than disabling headers/footers/sidebars, rendering simple text where a logo was, putting in page break guidance at sensible points, perhaps overriding a few text colours, maybe a couple of media queries for items that need to only appear/not appear in print. Basic stuff. From there, most "print this" views are little more than a slightly simpler template.

The cool thing about is that printability often comes up late in the requirements, and it's a thing people try before they ask you about it — they try to print from the demo. So it's an easy positive surprise if the print view does more or less what the user would hope for, right from the beginning.

Making it so all of an interface is at least basically printable is actually easy if you plan for it, and it's a good way of keeping focussed on broader usability.

Go talk to the browser vendors and have them roll in the CSS L3 PMM[1] specs, which have been in draft for . . . let's see here . . . oh, huh, two decades.

I guarantee the instant - the millisecond - that CSS PMM goes into the browser, that is the second the PDF ecosystem goes poof[2].

Aside from all that, back to the core point, it's quite late in the day to restrict HTML to page-based UX. It's been at least a decade since the "browser" has officially become a sorta-crappy sandbox for what we used to call client-server computing and just-in-time software development. But the thing about software, writing software, is that it's diametrically opposed to language, natural language, like the kind you use in documents. That's why we call one a "formal" language versus what we call "natural" language. The interface of these two, trying to make them the same thing, is an ever-bountiful cornucopia of suffering.

You see this in complex markup constructs, like conditionals and transcludes[3], that cross blades with natural language, i.e., documents. This is a big part of why every WYSIWYG "just like paper" XML editor ever made sucks so horribly. It's not that the developers are all idiots - I know a lot of them, and they're some of the smartest/canniest developers I've ever known - it's that they're taking on a task that is conceptually unworkable. A document markup that supports conditionals, or transclusion, the file stops being a document and becomes a part of a system that makes documents. Oh look another cornucopia what could be inside . .
