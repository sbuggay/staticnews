What people seem to be missing here is that enums are just a reflection of functionality. If you make your enum non exhaustive then add a field, and something stops working because it's now missing critical messages, it's still a breaking change!

It's always up to callers to decide to handle everything, or decide that additions are non-fatal and use a wildcard branch to ignore them. The same enum could need to be exhaustive for one use case, or subset matched by another.

The granularity of crates shouldn't really impact what kinds of abstractions you build. You can still have as many modules and types as you need, and as big of functions as you need, but its saying you should organize the modules into separate crates instead of one large crate. They even call out the trait coherence rules, saying that you can just make your library bigger instead of forcing a more awkward design in the pursuit of a smaller crate.

I think one benefit of small crates is that it gives you more granularity in linking and dependency graphs, which is especially relevant for rebuilds. At a previous job we had a monolithic "utilities" crate, and this caused problems when you tried to depend on it for the IP address utilities but this brought in the radio frequency utilities. When one of these submodules got an update the versioning would excessively propagate downstream to every consumer. If this was organized as small "net-utils" and "rf-utils" crates then maybe it would not have been as much of an issue.
