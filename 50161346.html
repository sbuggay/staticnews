On 16 Oct 2026, Don Hopkins wrote:

Yes, it's Sun's US 5,187,786: "Method and apparatus for implementing a class hierarchy of objects in a hierarchical file system." Owen and David filed it in 1991, it was granted in 1993, and it expired in 2011, so anyone can use it now, including people writing esolangs about folders.

https://patents.google.com/patent/US5187786A/en

You're right about the prior art, and the idea came straight from your side of the family tree. Tom Stambaugh helped Owen work out how to adapt Smalltalk patterns to it, scribbling on napkins at a poolside table at the old Hyatt Palo Alto. In the patent, directories are classes and instances, PATH files encode the inheritance chain, and you send a message from the shell with something like "SEND aF001 methodA args".

It's the same trick Owen used for the NeWS object system in PostScript. Classes were dictionaries, and sending a message pushed the object's class chain onto the dictionary stack, so ordinary PostScript name lookup did the method lookup for free. NeWS 1.0 had single inheritance through a parent dictionary. TNT added ordered parent lists and multiple inheritance. The patent does the same thing with the shell path playing the dictionary stack: walk the directories, and the first match wins.

Owen's 1986 Monterey paper on object-oriented PostScript:

https://donhopkins.com/home/monterey86.pdf

The NeWS Book, chapter 6:

https://donhopkins.com/home/The_NeWS_Book_1989.pdf

I've been running with it in MOOLLM, which leans more toward Self than Smalltalk. A directory is an object, its files are slots, and a "parents:" list delegates to other directories, in order, first match wins. It's prototypes rather than classes, and the LLM does the message dispatch by reading the directory. The filesystem is the object graph, so it's inspectable, diffable, and versioned in git for free:

https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/blob/main/kernel/DIRECTO...

https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/blob/main/skills/file-sy...

The lineage from Smalltalk and Self through NeWS, TNT, the patent, HyperLook, and The Sims to MOOLLM is written up here:

https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/blob/main/designs/object...

https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/blob/main/designs/postsc...

Owen and David can correct my history, since they're on the thread.

-Don

I have been keeping to "80" IQ points in the saying (thus avoiding inflation). However very few people have noticed there is no sign bit in the aphorism, and that it works just as well for "poor perspectives being worth negative 80 IQ points" (and I think we could claim inflation of stupidity in many bad directions these days ...).

I don't like to claim priority for any ideas (including my own). Bob Barton liked a great saying of Goethe's: "We should all share in the excitement of Discovery without vain attempts to claim Priority" and I imprinted on that in grad school.

So, without claiming priority, I would point to Engelbart's NLS for unifying the structure of the file system with the basic hierarchical structuring of their content (and this included how code was represented, written, and read). A few years later I used a different kind of unification in the FLEX machine that Ed Cheadle and I did in the mid-60s to have "every large thing" be an instantiable process, including things that were more "file-like".

This is just FYI, partly because of the above Goethe quote, partly because the FLEX project was done within the aerospace conglomerate LTV, and was proprietary. My thesis advisor Dave Evans had to go to considerable work to get permission for me to do my degree write ups on a tiny subset. The later Smalltalk was a "cold restart" on these ideas (and LTG was not a problem by then).

And -- for Smalltalk -- the notion to make "everything large and small" an object was a quick way to get going on the comparatively speedy Alto, and Dan Ingalls (and his Smalltalk group) were able to do most of the SW engineering to make Smalltalk act semantically as though everything was an object.

Best cheers

>When the Macintosh was new, Bruce “Tog” Tognazzini wrote a column in Apple’s developer magazine on UI. In his column, people wrote in with lots of interesting UI design problems, which he discussed. These columns continue to this day on his web site. They’ve also been collected and embellished in a couple of great books, like Tog on Software Design, which is a lot of fun and a great introduction to UI design. (Tog on Interface was even better, but it’s out of print.)

>Tog invented the concept of the mile high menu bar to explain why the menu bar on the Macintosh, which is always glued to the top of the physical screen, is so much easier to use than menu bars on Windows, which appear inside each application window. When you want to point to the File menu on Windows, you have a target about half an inch wide and a quarter of an inch high to acquire. You must move and position the mouse fairly precisely in both the vertical and the horizontal dimensions.

>But on a Macintosh, you can slam the mouse up to the top of the screen, without regard to how high you slam it, and it will stop at the physical edge of the screen – the correct vertical position for using the menu. So, effectively, you have a target that is still half an inch wide, but a mile high. Now you only need to worry about positioning the cursor horizontally, not vertically, so the task of clicking on a menu item is that much easier.

>Based on this principle, Tog has a pop quiz: what are the five spots on the screen that are easiest to acquire (point to) with the mouse? The answer: all four corners of the screen (where you can literally slam the mouse over there in one fell swoop without any pointing at all), plus, the current position of the mouse, because it’s already there.

>The principle of the mile-high menu bar is fairly well known, but it must not be entirely obvious, because the Windows 95 team missed the point completely with the Start push button, sitting almost in the bottom left corner of the screen, but not exactly. In fact, it’s about 2 pixels away from the bottom and 2 pixels from the left of the screen. So, for the sake of a couple of pixels, Microsoft literally “snatches defeat from the jaws of victory”, Tog writes, and makes it that much harder to acquire the start button. It could have been a mile square, absolutely trivial to hit with the mouse. For the sake of something, I don’t know what, it’s not. God help us.

Another great technique he documented in the original Apple Human Interface Guidelines was the "drag delay" of popping up "pull right" submenus, to mitigate a problem that linear menus have, but pie menus don't. People keep forgetting and re-inventing it in sometimes better, sometimes worse ways, but he invented and implemented it for the original Mac, then most importantly documented it in the first edition of the Apple's 1987 Human Interface Guidelines, and the Mac UI still supports it. It's the kind of thing nobody notices if it works well, that's invisibly built into the toolkit, that nobody appreciates how much thought and nuance went into it, that deserves a lot of user testing and iteration to get right. (Or you could just use pie menus and not have that problem! ;)

noduerme|prev|next|

I've followed and agreed with this OP's critique for awhile.. it's also a justifiable and entertaining take. As a UI designer for 30 years, I have a sympathetic but slightly different view.

I grew up in the 80s with DOS software, when dropdown menus were new and our IBM PC didn't even have a mouse. Since the design idea emerged of using an icon in a menu, which I'd probably locate to the Apple menu on the 128k Mac, my whole life I've seen this push/pull between phases of design and various fads that have emphasized either information density or some kind of simplified graphic/hieroglyphic gloss of the language and intent.

The gloss that works, imho, isn't precisely what has iconic meaning, as the OP postulates. It's what the user becomes familiar with. There's a subtle difference there. An arbitrary icon can be made to mean something over time. But that has to be an intentional process, and it should be tied to a larger theory of the branding and iconography of the company. Otherwise, it's probably worse than useless, even if it does literally signify the action.

Lately, in the last 10 years, I've found myself doing hybrids. I treat icons in selection modes as something like an emoji. If an icon is so universally understandable that no text is necessary, I might prefer it. Otherwise, it would be included only if I need to draw a user's attention to something more than the other items. For example, an extra icon next to a pending issue waiting for approval.

When available too, I'd much rather show a keyboard shortcut than an icon. That's useful information for most users, instead of decoration.

People who don't design for a living often think decoration is design, or vice versa. Decoration is antithetical to good design. And good design is never decorative. If it's not either informational, or what I'd call "aspirationally informational", in that it's training the user to recognize a meaningful design artifact, then it should go. Decorations belong in illustration and background art, to tie the piece together. Not in UI.

I think the main thing is to consider these design choices. Does the icon help someone identify and navigate to something they need to do more quickly? No one is perfectly correct, making that call. It's one of those things where asking the question and thinking about it is 90% of the effort.
