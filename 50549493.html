> SID (Synthesis of Integral Design) was a logic synthesis program used to generate logic gates for the VAX 9000. From high-level behavioral and register-transfer level sources, approximately 93% of the CPU scalar and vector units, over 700,000 gates, were synthesized.

> SID was an artificial intelligence rule-based system and expert system with over 1000 hand-written rules. In addition to logic gate creation, SID took the design to the wiring level, allocating loads to nets and providing parameters for place and route CAD tools. As the program ran, it generated and expanded its own rule-base to 384,000 low-level rules.

> Initially it was somewhat controversial but was accepted in order to reduce the overall VAX 9000 project budget. Some engineers refused to use it. Others compared their own gate-level designs to those created by SID, eventually accepting SID for the gate-level design job. Since SID rules were written by expert logic designers and with input from the best designers on the team, excellent results were achieved. As the project progressed and new rules were written, SID-generated results became equal to or better than manual results for both area and timing. For example, SID produced a 64-bit adder that was faster than the manually-designed one. Manually-designed areas averaged 1 bug per 200 gates, whereas SID-generated logic averaged 1 bug per 20,000 gates. After finding a bug, SID rules were corrected, resulting in 0 bugs on subsequent runs.

zackmorris|prev|next|

It will be interesting to see if AI really starts innovating in circuit design or just continues doing the wrong things faster. But I'd bet that it will soon run circles around the status quo, and rightly so.

We're way out on the long tail of a dead-end approach to computation that started in the late 1990s with GPUs when we chose to optimize SIMD at the expense of general-purpose computing. On top of that, Moore's Law ended in roughly 2007 with the arrival of smartphones, when the industry chose cost and power minimization over compute maximization via a multicore approach.

Now only servers have access to highly multicore chips like AMD EPYC, at 10-100 times the cost and with poorer performance than if they maximized local/shared memories with content-addressable caching. Apple's M line of processors is ok, but its specialized cores just stand in the way of the higher abstractions available with symmetric multiprocessing, upon which specialized algorithms can be built. We're still missing the multicore foundation.

I used Synopsys and Cadence back in college in the late 1990s to automatically place and route circuits from diagrams in SPICE (I think, my memory is foggy) and they already performed 80% as well as humans. I don't remember if that was for chip area, speed or power. But the big players were obviously using tools at least as capable as whatever we were trained on.

Then in the 2000s I had hoped to do what OpenAI is doing and simulate small/open CPU designs on FPGAs. But the economics just weren't there. Even though some people like me (the poors) knew just exactly what could work, there was a tech winter between roughly the Dot Bomb around 2000 to the start of the Mobile Bubble in the early 2010s. So we didn't have a way to communicate what was possible to the people with capital, without having to network to get through the gatekeeping. Which may be fun for enthusiasts, but it's anathema to hackers wanting to get real work done.

I'd nearly given up all hope of progress until last year when AI went mainstream. Now I see 3 steps to the epiphany:

[x] Time

[ ] Money

[ ] Resources

AI solves the time component, since specs (ideas) are orders of magnitude easier to create than implementations.

But money and resources may not be available for quite some time. It's looking like the music may stop for AI funding next year, now that the election is over and the writing's on the wall as far as doing things without consent goes.
