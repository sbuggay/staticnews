bcrl|root|parent|next|

Given that the stated plan of Micron is to focus on 3D scaling, keeping per-mm^2 production costs low makes perfect sense. When Samsung introduced their first volume V-NAND flash, the 2D feature sizes were larger than leading edge NAND flash because of the tolerances inherent in producing 90 layer flash (aspect ratio matters!) as well as the cost of production equipment. That trend has continued as it is cheaper to produce flash with more layers than to compensate for difficulties arising from decreasing amounts of charge kept in ever smaller cells.

In layperson DRAM-speak this translates to the following: rowhammer attacks are mitigated at the hardware level by increasing the size of and spacing between adjacent cells. At some point further feature size scaling makes it no longer be possible to produce 4F cells that are sufficiently robust against rowhammer type attacks to meet Micron's internal reliability targets. This would be akin to when Intel hit the power wall with the Pentium 4 in the early '00s forcing a change in engineering direction away from merely scaling clock speed.

With Micron's insight into the DRAM order pipeline, management likely has to factor in the chance of oversupply in the next couple of years as significant, which makes the shift in direction even more sensible. DRAM price crashes are a regular occurrence in the industry which lead to anti-dumping tariffs and legal battle in the 1990s (as well as Intel's shift away from DRAM towards complex logic devices in 1985). I'm pretty sure Micron's management is consciously working to avoid a repeat of that protracted legal battle.

I'd rather have an engineering problem over a legal problem any day of the week.

adrian_b|root|parent|next|

Those are completely misleading, because they are not the true cell layouts.

Searching for the Micron patent on 6F^2 cells (US 6,975,531) is much more informative.

The essence of the problem is the following. An ideal memory array cell is formed at the intersection of a vertical line with a horizontal line. A line has the width 2F (i.e. metal width + spacing width), so the area of the cell is 2F * 2F = 4F^2.

Instead of that, most DRAM cells are at the intersection of 2 horizontal lines with 1 vertical line. The single line is a bidirectional data line with the value of the stored bit, while the pair of lines are a write select line and a read select line.

Using separate read and write transistors provides better performance.

The naive layout for a cell where 2 lines intersect with 1 line has an area of 2F * 4F = 8F^2.

An optimized layout where many parts are stacked one over the other and where some lines are shared by adjacent cells, so the spacings are omitted, can have the smaller area of 2F * 3F = 6F^2.

In the 6F^2 cell, there are are 3 transistors, but they have the drain stacked over the source and one transistor is stacked above another, to minimize area.

To make a 4F^2 DRAM cell, there are various approaches, e.g. one could try to stack even more components vertically or one could go back to one-transistor cells, but in modern technology, but it is not obvious whether the much higher processing cost is compensated by the reduced die area.

Micron claims that despite the reduced area the cost would be higher, while the competitors, including the Chinese, claim the opposite.

toast0|root|parent|prev|next|

I don't know any of the details, but dram is basically a whole lot of bits and and each bit is one capacitor and one transistor.

In the diagram for 8F Squared, there's a capacitor at every other junction between word lines and bit lines. If the lines (and inter line spacing) are 2F wide, you have one bit in a 2Fx4F rectangle, or one bit per 8F squared.

In the diagram for 6F Squared, there's a capacitor at every bit line on every other word line. If the lines (and inter line spacing) are 2F wide, you have one bits in each 2Fx3F Rectangle, or one bit per 6F squared. (I guess the word lines without bits are needed for spacing or to drive the transistor or maybe it's just illustration and doesn't mean much)

In the diagram for 4F Squared, there's a transistor at every bit line on every word line. Now you have a bit in every 2Fx2F square, or one bit per 4F Squared.

If you can make dram in all three styles with the same F, 4F squared is twice the density as 8F squared. If the F doesn't hold constant, it's squishier, of course.
