sigbottle|parent|next|

OK. The core distinction is this.

The philosophical issue I have can be decomposed into two parts:

1) What is the base ontology of what the neural nets thinks exist? 2) How do you recover, and begin to understand said ontology?

Modern researchers obviously want the goal of simplicity & composition, but to arrive at that, they build abstractions like transcoders and SAE's to get into their pet ontology (with the assumption that neurons themselves are garbage to understand). This obviously raises the question - why privilege your basis? Having a grand result, actually be an artifact of your bad representation is a tale as old as time. Even things like the PRH need to compare higher-level concepts like this.

The specific empirical claim is that, for the rightly structured & trained & large models that don't need to encode hodgepodges of bullshit, the neurons themselves are sufficiently atomic to be studied, dissolving any need to go to some intermediate layer in which we need to question "is this an artifact of our representation?" The resulting interactions between said neurons may be incomprehensible, but the point is that this provides a layer of objectivity (note: not claimed that "the neural nets found the objectively right abstractions in idea space", but rather that the neurons themselves are atomic).

There's still an issue here in that okay, even if neurons are atomic, humans have to ascribe some interpretation to them, separate from the problem of the composition of the atoms, which humans also have to interpret. But if he's right, then you do actually have this privileged base layer to work with, even if you don't know what the base layer means. That's pretty important, actually. Assuming this holds up in reality, ofc.

(There's an argument as to why we should even start from this line of inquiry but whatever lol)

antonvs|root|parent|next|

Yes, (digital) neural networks are algorithms.

You probably know that non-determinism in real-world inference comes from deliberately stochastic features like temperature, from implementation choices like distributed and parallel computation, and numerical effects like floating point precision.

The fact that a neural network's execution can be nondeterministic doesn't mean that the network stops being an algorithm.

The point you're making boils down to speculation that the accidental non-determinism involved in real-world inference is significant enough to undermine or negate interpretability findings based on the algorithms. That would need to be demonstrated.

The CGoL and BB examples demonstrate what might be possible in a worst-case scenario, but they're only analogies in this context. It's far from clear that real models suffer from such effects to a significant degree. You might actually be better off talking about the butterfly effect!

A TLDR, since most comments here seem to misunderstand the result.

The idea is: a model may only have 100M neurons or whatever. But there are infinite concepts in the world. Therefore, models have to "stuff" multiple concepts per neuron.

How do you do that? Theres two ways:

1. Each neuron handles similar concepts. One neuron for dogs, a separate neuron one for cars, etc.

2. Each neuron handles a bundle of unrelated concepts. One neuron handles dogs and cars. Another neuron handles cats and numbers. And so on.

Historically, people thought #2 was correct because many neurons were found that seemingly represented multiple concepts. #2 had a cool name (polysemanticity) and was promoted in OpenAI/Anthropic blogs so it had a lot of traction.

However, this blog says #2 is wrong. Actually, the dog/car neuron is just a "V shape neuron". So #2 was just methodological error, and #1 was right all along. This is all the blog is saying.

羊.

This is a Chinese character that can refer to goats, rams, and several other kinds of "medium-sized animal with thick wooly coats and horns". English has no direct equivalent; we have to be too specific when we talk about these animals.

A model with a small number of neurons has to compact its concepts in order to fit within the parameter count while reducing loss. OP's article shows that the compaction is usually generalization: "goat" and "ram" get compressed into "羊". The canonical example of superposition, an image model where "car" feeds into a bunch of "dog" features, was shown to be actually generalization. In a sense, the model was so small that its optimizer didn't have the parameter budget to distinguish between "car" and "dog"; it has a single set of "thing that moves fast" features.

So basically, really small models are merging features into semantic buckets that are so generalized they don't make sense to big human brains unless we really, really squint. The buckets chosen happen to be what has the lowest loss, of course.
