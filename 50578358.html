rhdunn|root|parent|next|

No. It's about connecting two areas/domains of mathematics.

An example would be how you can specify complex numbers in multiple forms:

- as a (real, imaginary) pair using the definitions of operations like addition and multiplication;

- as a 2x2 matrix, with 1 and i having matrix representations;

- as a 2D vector (angle, magnitude).

The idea is that if you prove something in one area you can apply that proof to the other domain using the equivalence mappings without having to prove all of those in the target domain. The basic construction is:

- have the problem in domain A (e.g. a complex number in (re, im) form);

- apply mappings converting domain A to domain B (e.g. representing the complex number as a 2x2 matrix);

- apply the proof from domain B;

- apply mappings converting domain B to domain A;

- QED

In a way, the Langlands program is like a subset of Concept Theory.

Could someone explain the difference (if any) between the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis and what has historically been called the Riemann Hypothesis?

simonreiff|parent|next|

The Riemann hypothesis is a statement about the nontrivial complex-valued zeroes of the Riemann zeta function given by zeta(s) := sum to infinity of 1/n^s, namely that such zeroes all have real part 1/2. It has been extended and specialized many ways since being posed by Riemann over 150 years ago, but perhaps the most important is the generalized Riemann hypothesis which replaces the Riemann zeta function with the Dirichlet L-function. In other words GRH says: All nontrivial zeroes of the Dirichlet L-function L(chi, s) = sum to infinity of chi(n)/n^s, for primitive Dirichlet character chi, have real part 1/2.
