Just In Time only works if you can correctly forecast the lead times for parts to arrive when requested.

e.g. if you know you use 20/day and it takes 2 days to get the new parts then you order parts when your inventory hits 40.

> The benefits of CVTs are independent of whether or not the vehicle is a hybrid.

Being a hybrid allows the CVT to keep the engine at the optimal RPM and load. If the engine would be under-loaded, the additional power can be stored in the battery. If it would require more power than the optimal engine output, it can use less engine output and more electric assist.

But there is also another factor here. Electric motors and lithium batteries are both pretty efficient, something like 90% each, but that's not 100%, and it's especially not 100% when you take losses from the generator, the battery and the traction motor and multiply them all together.

So you then have a trade off between running the engine at a higher output than the current vehicle load and taking the conversion losses to store the excess, or running the engine at a lower RPM or load, which for some values of RPM and load would be less of an efficiency loss than the round trip. A parallel hybrid can then choose the engine output level with the least efficiency loss at any given load.

The big ones that use series hybrids are trains and diesel submarines.

Submarines do it because they can't run the diesel engine while submerged so everything is required to run on batteries underwater and as soon as they surface the priority is topping up the batteries. Notice that nuclear submarines generally run the screws directly on steam from the reactor.

Trains do it because their design predates modern CVT parallel hybrids and they essentially use electric motors in place of a transmission, since a mechanical transmission that could get a freight train moving from a stop would need an impractical number of gears. The traditional design didn't even have batteries. They'd save brake pads by using the electric motors as generators to slow the train but then run the electricity through resistor banks to radiate it off as heat because they had nowhere to put it. They'd run the diesel engine at all times, even going down a hill, because it was used rather than the non-existent batteries to run air compressors and climate control. The design was really a result of needing a CVT before they were available, and now it's likely to stick because if something replaces them it would be full battery electric trains rather than designing a new transmission to switch from series to parallel hybrids right before diesel engines go out of style anyway.
