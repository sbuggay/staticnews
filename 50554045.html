We've had spectacular infrastructure outages for decades; I think that a cluster of incidents in one week doesn't establish that AI is directly responsible for declining reliability.

IMO, the issue is that AI is reducing the cost of building and modifying systems much faster than it's reducing the cost of understanding them. For example, an engineer can work on a problem or project for a week that could otherwise be done by AI in an hour or so. But, that week spent was not "wasted time"; it was understanding what it is they are building, discovering undocumented assumptions, knowing about different dependencies, developing intuitions about the system and how it works, etc.

This causes comprehension debt. Yes, the system does ultimately work, the tests also pass. But the level of comprehension goes down significantly.

That said, AI can also help reduce this debt by asking it what you need help understanding.

> We've had spectacular infrastructure outages for decades

And yet, in the entire history of electromechanical switching in the Bell System, no central office was ever out of action for more than 30 minutes for any reason other than a natural disaster. (With one late exception, a major fire in New York City in 1975.[1])

That was achieved by design. Electromechanical telephone switches were designed to have no single point of failure. That was achieved in practice. Failures occurred all the time, but the problem was confined to a small group of lines, if not a single line. I'm not going to go into how that was done; read up on #5 crossbar if you're interested. Even complex functions such as long-distance routing had backdowns to static routes stored in read-only devices, in case the telco equivalent of BGP failed.

That reliability record has not continued into the computer era.
