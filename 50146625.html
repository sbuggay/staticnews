And the late Marc de Groot developed Meme, the Multitasking Extensible Messaging Environment: a multi-user networked virtual-world system programmed in multitasking Forth. His June 1994 announcement emphasized portable executable behavior for objects, not just geometry, with code that could travel efficiently over the network. Another wonderful part of this history is Coco and Peter Conn’s HOMER & Associates. They hired Forth, Inc. to program their Homer II visual mixing console, and got Chuck Moore himself to write the host software!

Paul Rother worked alongside Chuck, learning Forth and programming the individual visual channels. The console had 24 channels with their own Z80s, a master Z80 running multitasking PolyForth in 32K, touch-sensitive sliders, and a “joystick bumper” like a Mustang gearshift. You could perform visual sequences, record them, and overdub corrections like an audio mix, then take the recording and slide trays to the optical printer.

Paul later got a CAT-700 framebuffer running as a Forth paint system alongside the optical-printer controls. Coco remembers its early use on Abracadabra and Atomic Dog.

I’ve been bringing Forth and hand-controlled graphics together again on a somewhat unlikely machine: a PDP-7 running in the browser.

Mitch Bradley and Claude recently wrote a beautiful PDP-7 Forth in one day. Its inner interpreter takes three instructions, using XCT to execute thread cells whose opcodes select the behavior. I integrated it into my Cabinet PDP-7 emulator, with a Type 340 vector display, a 3D turtle written in Forth, and an emulated fictional hand-tracking peripheral, as if the Data Glove + Polhemus existed in 1967, that plugs into the PDP-7 cabinet with its own IOT instructions wrapped with a Forth API.

The browser tracks your hand with the webcam and exposes the coordinates through PDP-7 device registers. Forth running inside the emulated machine reads those registers and draws the hand. The camera processing stays in the browser; only tracking numbers enter the emulated machine.

Here’s ULTIMATE HAND, implemented in PDP-7 assembly and Forth:

https://hyperties.org/cabinet/ultimate-hand/

It’s Minsky’s Ultimate Machine, except your hand becomes its hand. Allow the camera, hold up your hand with your palm away and fingers up, and the box opens and carries your hand toward the switch. Touch the switch with a fingertip and it retreats into the box. The arm positions the hand, but you control its finger shapes.

Really? The CYBERSPACE trademark application wasn't rejected. It was abandoned in 1991 after the VR project was cancelled and we developers left Autodesk to start Sense8. The recorded disposition was "abandoned—failure to respond or late response."

https://uspto.report/TM/73816876

And yeah, Autodesk was hardly a tiny startup. Its market cap at the end of 1989 was about $945 million.

But the "street" line was a deliberately smartass quotation from Gibson in a letter I wrote, not some corporate attempt to pass itself off as counterculture. Gibson subsequently had the better rejoinder: "Autodesk is not the street."

Judging the Autodesk Research Lab of 1989 by the reputation of the company Autodesk later became seems rather ahistorical. Even founder John Walker had already declared himself "Bored (sic) of Directors" by 1988:
