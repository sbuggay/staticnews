Back in 2018, when I was at Greenplum, my team worked through The Internals of PostgreSQL together alongside Andy Pavlo’s CMU database courses. It was invaluable for anyone working on the database kernel. Today, a beginner can get that same interactive learning experience simply by chatting with AI, much like the discussions our team had back then.

Now I’m building Lavik, a new cache service for PostgreSQL. Traditional caches like Redis are DRAM-based, so they can only hold a small slice of hot data. Once your data outgrows memory, you’re exposed to cache miss storms, where a flood of requests falls through to PostgreSQL all at once. Nobody likes those. (OpenAI encounter the similar cache miss storms problem: https://openai.com/index/scaling-postgresql/)

Lavik takes a different path: it runs on NVMe SSDs, and it’s still faster than Redis running on DRAM. That’s not what most people expect. Conventional wisdom says SSD-based systems trade speed for capacity, but Lavik gives you both. You can keep TB-scale data in cache while getting 1M QPS and sub-millisecond P99 latency.

Lavik is open source under the Apache License. GitHub: https://github.com/eloqdata/lavik Website: https://lavik.dev

Chapter 12 (full-text search) quietly changed under this book in Postgres 18: FTS now reads its dictionaries and config through the cluster's default collation provider instead of always libc, so clusters on ICU or the new builtin PG_UNICODE_FAST can see different behavior in FTS and pg_trgm — the release notes explicitly tell you to reindex those indexes after pg_upgrade. Also in 18, LIKE with nondeterministic collations stopped being an error, which was the main reason people avoided case-insensitive ICU collations on searchable columns.

getbrew|prev|next|

The HOT chapter is the one with the most direct practical payoff. Two things worth knowing from it: HOT only kicks in when no indexed column changes and the new tuple fits on the same page, so an index on a frequently-updated column (status, updated_at) quietly disables it for every update to that table. And lowering fillfactor (say 80-90) on update-heavy tables leaves free space on each page so HOT can actually happen. Comparing n_tup_hot_upd to n_tup_upd in pg_stat_user_tables is a cheap way to see whether you're getting HOT updates before tuning anything.
