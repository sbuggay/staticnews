I think this is an outcome of playing with systems that we don’t actually understand very well on a deep level. We have found recipes that work, and have used graduate descent (so many PhD theses) to understand and optimize these systems, but we’ve built a model of language that also encodes reasoning, and those things are extraordinarily complex. Providing proper mathematical rigor for their explanation requires, as you say, the level of commitment equivalent to a math degree. Also, because language is so complicated and subtle, you need highly precise statistical models of it, and that just takes a shitload of data; hence the scale. You can train toy models at home, but they will suck because you actually need massive amounts of data to capture the nuance that language requires. And that’s to say nothing of multimodal models.

elzbardico|parent|prev|next|

Machine learning IS applied mathematics, especially statistics, probability, and optimization. Period.

The "baby steps" are not there just for intelectual satisfaction, the baby steps give you the building blocks to understand things that become progressively more complex. The baby steps are tools, that once you get familiar with them, you'll find new and new uses everywhere.

A good practical illustration of this: It is pretty hard to figure out how differential equations can be useful if you don't understand what they are. And yet, I bet that on january of 2020, there was at least one math teacher deep down in the amazonian jungle playing with a compartimental model based on a system of ordinary differential equations, to figure out the diverse scenarios this new epidemic could play out if that scary new virus escaped China and those few cruise ships.

Another example: take the concept of backpropagation: the underlying idea behind it is the chain rule from calculus, used to work backward through a computation and calculate how each parameter affects the error. Now, it is way easier to understand it in a small CNN that you are training to recognize handwritten digits than trying to understand it in the context of a full transformer model. A digit classifier is something that is way easier and affordable for you to fiddle with stuff and develop and intuition on loss functions, gradients, backpropagation and overfitting.


libraryofbabel|prev|next|

I feel there's a misconception in a lot of the comment threads about what an LLM-from-scratch tutorial is for. Of course you're not going to come out of this kind of tutorial being able to train a useful LLM from scratch. (It requires too much compute, and datasets you don't have access to, and there are a lot of tricks and operational problems to solve for real training runs.) If that's your bar for "impracticality", then sure, this is impractical.

But that's not the point; the point is understanding these things better by seeing how they are made (or grown, if you prefer). Building something at a level further down the stack than you will ever go forces you to confront details that shape the layers above that you do deal with. This is one reason why Crafting Interpreters is such a beloved book here on HN.

LLMs are now at the core of tools we all use everyday... don't you want to know how they are made? Maybe not everyone has this itch to understand the platforms and tools they use, but I sure do, and it's helped me over and over again. Sure, LLMs aren't like operating systems or compilers. They're a quasi-organic mass of billions of parameters learned during training and honestly nobody (not even the mech interp folks) really deeply understands why they work so well. But that's no reason to completely give up and there is still a lot you can learn.

Take RLVR ("Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards"). This is the training technique that made LLMs get so good at programming and math in 2025 and 2026. A lot of people who confidently talk about LLM capabilities either haven't heard of it (and thus end up parroting out-of-date factoids about LLM training derived from the pre-2025 era) or have only a very dim idea of what it is. In this course you actually do RL training of an LLM to get it solving math problems better. You're right down in the details. It's interesting for its own sake but it will also give you a grounding for discussing questions such as: "why did LLM writing get so weird and bad in mid-2026?" and "why is it so hard to train LLMs to be good at things that aren't like math and programming?"

Anyway, Sebastian's two books are great and highly recommended; I worked through the first one a year ago and it was one of the best investments of my time in all of 2025. If you prefer video for learning this is great as well (although I'd still say - try the books first).
