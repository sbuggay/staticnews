Anyone that's never had the (dis)pleasure of working with tapes, there's some gotchas to know about before archiving and forgetting about it.

When you create an archive, do not skip the step of reading it back. I'm not talking about just having the system confirm it is readable. I mean actually restoring the data from the tape. I've had tapes fail to retrieve data off of the tape even though the writing app confirmed it was readable.

Drives can have issues reading data written by another drive. Drives can have issues reading a tape it wrote itself.

Why write one when you can write two at twice the time/cost? In all of my experience with tape, I've never written just one tape. In the early days of DVDs, the images were written to LTO to send to have the discs pressed. Two were always sent on the chance one wasn't readable and schedules were so tight that the loss of a couple of days to send a new tape would wreck delivery dates. Another facility wrote three tapes: one kept on site, one sent to IronMountain, one sent to the client directly. Plenty of occasions saw the local copy unreadable and the IronMountain copy was retrieved. These were managed by large robotic tape libraries so each tape was written in a different drive at the same time so it was 3x cost while only 1x time.

Use the drive cleaning cassette as recommended. Your dirty heads can cause the data to not be written/read correctly depending on which head is dirty. If you're having issues reading, clean the heads before giving up on the tape. If you're having issues reading a tape just written, do not think someone else will be able to read it.

Ensure whoever might be using a tape you've written understands what was used to write the tape. Not all archivers are compatible. If you use the tar command to write to tape, people using a GUI app won't be able to read it.

> The read-write head has 32 read-write elements. Each element contains a write component sandwiched between two read components – one for reading, the other for read-after-write checking. The overall read-write head reads or writes 32 adjacent tracks at once. This group of 32 adjacent tracks running the length of the tape is called a wrap. The write tracks are partially overlapped, leaving a clearly readable read track, narrower and in the middle of its write track. This track overlapping is called shingling after shingled roof tiles, and is a way of cramming more write tracks into a data band to increase the tape’s capacity.

* https://www.blocksandfiles.com/tape/2025/07/29/why-lto-10-fe...

> The tape drives use a strong error correction algorithm that makes data recovery possible when lost data is within one track. Also, the head assembly includes separate read heads positioned just "behind" each write head, to verify the data just written without making a separate verification pass. This allows the drive to write a second copy of any data that fails the verify without the help of the host system.

Great points. To add some more:

* Don't rely on vendor-specific compression and/or encryption which may vary between different tape drives.

* If you do use encryption, try to ensure you have a way to recover from corruption in the middle of the bitstream (see parent's point on two copies, or CTR/GCM mode, or FEC/erasure coding).

* Index the contents (file names, sizes, at least), or use an archive format that puts the index at the start of the tape (note: tar does not).

adrian_b|root|parent|next|

I do not know how standard such a practice is, but I am old enough to have witnessed enough disasters caused by data lost due to carelessness, so I have no intention for something like that to happen to me.

Therefore with HDDs I have always written the same data on 2 HDDs, and this saved me a few times, when after several years of storage both HDDs contained a few corrupted sectors, but not in the same places.

With tapes, I also always write at least 2, but frequently even 3, because their low cost makes this affordable. With tapes, the greatest danger is that if a drive malfunctions mechanically while a tape cartridge is inserted, it might damage that tape.

Having at least 2 copies of each tape or HDD also allows their storage in different locations.

Like I have said, I always combine this redundancy of having duplicate storage media with intra-archive-file redundancy, so I typically add 10% redundancy to each compressed and encrypted archive, so that for any corruption that affects less than 10% of the file the content is still recoverable.

I am still using the older LTO-7. While I might upgrade to LTO-8 or even LTO-9, an upgrade to LTO-10 is unlikely, unless great price reductions will happen in the future, which I doubt.

So I am very concerned by the lack of a good archival solution that is affordable by ordinary people and by small businesses, not only by very rich companies.

Microsoft has demonstrated an ideal archival medium (in glass), but for now the cost of their laser system is too great for commercialization. That appears to be mostly a chicken and egg problem, the lasers and the other optical components are too expensive because they currently are produced only in very small quantities, but they cannot be incorporated into a high-volume application, because they are too expensive.
