miki123211|root|parent|prev|next|

If libraries were invented in the 21st century, it would have been by a Silicon Valley entrepreneur "disrupting the copyright monopollies" with the innovative practice of "book sharing". They would quickly be criticized by authors and journalists alike and banned by some local governments.

Libraries that required their members to pay fees did in fact exist historically. It's how many libraries in the US got started. They were private enterprises meant to make money (or at least be self-sustaining non-profits relying on a mix of donations and fees), not government-run projects.

Libraries are an obvious workaround for the western copyright system[1]. Because this system lets us share existing book copies without the permission of the author, but doesn't allow us to print new ones for free even when the capability exists, the obvious way to make books cheaper is to serially share them across many readers and have each pay the fraction of the price. This is essentially what these libraries did, with fees as a stand-in for the price sharing that simplified their operation.

[1] Yes, I'm aware that copyright isn't exactly necessary for libraries to exist; high printing costs would do it too. I do think that they would be entirely unnecessary and almost extinct (in well-functioning 21st-century democracies) if not for copyright, given our printing costs.

commandersaki|parent|next|

I didn't find the answer that interesting:

Vint Cerf: In the early 1970s, while we were developing this, we asked ourselves, should there be patents associated with it? Should there be other constraints? Should it be a licensable thing? The government does license technology that it has paid to develop, but it can also release it openly. And we went through a kind of analysis. Remember that we were thinking this would be a substrate for the American command and control system that would use computers to support command and control. And that we knew that it would have to be interconnected to our allies. So then in 1973, '74, we're saying, well, who are our allies now in 1974? And there's this list. And then we said, well, who were our allies 25 years before that? And it was a different list. And we said, who will our allies be 25 years from now? And we didn't know. So we thought the only way we can be assured that the allies will have access to this is to simply open it up. So we published this openly. All the protocols were made available openly. We encouraged implementations and interoperability. Interoperability. Could I just say one thing about that word, interoperability? Literally, everything in my career has revolved around the ability of disparate and distributed systems to interwork with each other. And interoperability is its own reward. And so I think if there was any theme in my history and in my career, it's been the seeking interoperability among disparate systems


lioeters|root|parent|next|

> interoperability is its own reward

That was one of my favorite points of the interview. He found value in building relationships, improving the space between systems and how they work together. That explains why TCP/IP was not patented - the reasoning was that the protocols needed to adapt to an unknown future when your allies could become enemies, or vice versa, so the best solution was to publish the invention and open it up to everybody.

"Adversarial interoperability" is a phrase I saw somewhere, and may apply here too.
