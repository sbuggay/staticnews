Sharlin|parent|next|

0.9… should not be seen as any kind of process. It’s simply another way of spelling 1, just like 4/6 is another way of spelling 2/3, as is -2/-3. There’s nothing special, mysterious, or non-numeric about it. It’s simply another, redundant encoding of the same number.

Any place-value numeral is a shorthand for writing a particular geometric series. Only considering numbers between 0 and 1:

  0.abc… = a*10^-1 + b*10^-2 + c*10^-3 + …
If there exists one last nonzero digit in the place-value representation, there is one last nonzero term in the series, and the sum is trivial to compute.

But if the place-value string is infinite? Well, we know that infinite series can have finite sums, and in particular all of these place-value series converge and have finite sums [exercise: show why].

Specifically, the sum of a converging infinite series, and thus the value of the corresponding place-value expression, is defined as the limit of its finite prefix sums. The general formula for the sum of a converging geometric series such as 0.9… is

  S = a / (1 - r)
where a is the first term and r the ratio of two consecutive terms. In this case S = 0.9…, a = 0.9 and r = 0.1. So

  0.9… = 0.9 / (1 - 0.1)

       = 1
Intuitively, the prefix sums of 0.9… are

  0.9
  0.99
  0.999
  0.9999
  ...
and it’s "clear" that the limit of this sequence at infinity is 1. And because 0.9… is by definition that limit,

  0.9… = 1.

bhattid|root|parent|next|

https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/12906/the-staircase...

I would say this is a good example where it's geometrically "proven" that pi = 4. It's convincing enough where if someone didn't already learn the value of pi, I might expect them to believe this proof.

The idea is that if you circumscribe a square around a unit circle and take its perimeter, you obtain 4. If you "invert" the corners of the square until they hit the circle, the perimeter remains unchanged. If you iterate infinitely, the sequence of polygons eventually converges to the circle.

In this case, the sequence truly does converge to a circle. However, the subtle detail is that, in the limit, this manner of measuring perimeter is the taxicab metric (and NOT the Euclidean metric), where pi actually does equal 4. It's not so obvious geometrically, but it shows that there's a distinction between a sequence of curves and the corresponding sequence of lengths.

EDIT: Though, having said all this, I feel the proof given about 0.999... equaling 1 is rigorous, but I suppose there's some subjectivity involved.

I haven't read or understood the author's proof, but the "standard" proof probably goes like this (any corrections? anyways though, I have to admit this approach sucks all of the joy out of the blog post)

We take the standard definition of the reals as equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences.

Let a_n be the mixed-base number up to n digits, e.g. a_4 = 0.1234. Clearly it's Cauchy

Let b_n = 1 - a_n, e.g. b_4 - 0.00001

Obviously, lim n->inf (a_n + b_n) = 1

But since the sequences a and b are Cauchy, we can exchange limit and sum

1 = lim n->inf (a_n) + lim n -> inf (b_n)

But the second limit = 0, therefore the first = 1
