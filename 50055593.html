octoberfranklin|parent|prev|next|

Why 4ghz?

mikewarot|root|parent|next|

I've edited it, but generally you don't want to have problems with mirror images getting through and creating spurious responses. Real world RF filters don't have infinitely steep edges, so initially I figured a 4 Ghz low pass filter would have enough loss by 5 Ghz to get the job done. I revised to to a 5 Ghz low filter, and a 7-12 Ghz Local Oscillator to give the filter an easier job. Also, the lower the swing in percentage terms for a VCO, the easier it is to work with.

So the signal path in receive mode would be:

N connector / 20 dB 100 watt pad / Switched attenuator / Low pass filter / Front End diodes / Mixer / Router board for DSP

Alternatively, there's usually a second path that skips the attenuator, with a lower maximum power level.

The reverse path would work for generating signals from DC (almost) to 5 Ghz.

Many consumer temperature, humidity, rain, and wind sensors use simple radio protocols in the 433 MHz band to report their readings back to their indoor display unit.

Stick an RTL-SDR dongle on your computer and use the program rtl_433 to monitor that traffic. It comes with built-in decoders for the protocols for a very large number of those sensors.
