kragen|root|parent|next|

Let's ballpark it. 20% capacity factor, 22% efficiency, 50% coverage in the sense of square meters of panel per square meter of land (this depends on your tilt angle).

An "acre" is about 4047m² in modern units, so at "one sun" of 1000W/m² you would get 4.047 megawatts per acre. Multiply by the factors above, you actually 89kW/acre. A year is 8766 hours, so that's 780MWh/acre/year.

Suppose you get a PPA signed for 3 cents a kilowatt hour, US$30/MWh. That's US$23400/acre/year. But that's revenue, not profit. You can compare it to the US$1125/acre/year you give above. So what are the expenses?

The most fundamental cost here is the solar modules. 4.047MW at 50% coverage and 22% efficiency is still 445kW peak. At the current US$0.15/Wp in international markets, that's US$67000 to plant that field with panels, noticeably more than the US$675 you report to plant it with corn.

But, if you're still measuring land in acres, as if we were in the Canterbury Tales, you're probably in the US, where modules cost roughly double what they cost in the rest of the world, due to bipartisan tariffs to protect US fossil fuel interests. And the balance-of-plant costs are typically about double the cost of the modules alone. So, instead of US$67000, you're probably looking at US$300k for those 445kW (or 597 horsepower, or the power of roughly 1100 American-football running backs, if you're more comfortable with those units.)

US$23k per year from a US$300k investment is about 7% ROI, which is probably profitable but not wildly so. And if you don't have a signed PPA, you can expect that revenue to decline, as cheaper panels and power electronics drive the value of daytime generation to zero, and household units that don't need such high balance-of-plant costs reduce demand for utility-scale generation. Also, you can expect heavy industry to move to countries where solar power costs less, reducing demand.

The cost of the land isn't really significant at these investment levels.

Up here in Scotland (can't speak for England, it's half a day's drive south of me just to get to the border, but watch Clarkson's Farm) we tend not to make hay very much these days. Mostly we make silage, which is where you cut long grass but instead of allowing it to dry into hay you bale it up while it's still green and wrap it in plastic sheeting. It undergoes anaerobic fermentation, which preserves a lot more of the nutrients, and it doesn't spontaneously combust if it's still a tiny bit damp when you store it.

The silage that you get, then, is basically like a kind of grass sauerkraut, and smells exactly like you'd expect.

That combined with "draff" - distillery mash, the spent malt from brewing "pot ale" for making whisky, and a certain amount of shredded sugar beet tends to make up the majority of winter fodder.

Draff is great because it's still fermenting slowly and generating a lot of heat, so the cows are eating hot food in the winter. It's mostly the fibrey bits of the barley malt, which we can't eat but which cows and sheep - having cellulase in at least one of their stomachs - do incredibly well on. It's absolutely no use for anything else, humans can't eat it and it doesn't really compost down. It just rots, stinks, and emits masses of carbon dioxide and methane when it does. Far better for the environment to lock all that up in a cow.

Ideally your fields don't flood because you plant crops in the autumn that stay in the ground over winter to germinate in the spring, and if they get flooded that's it, you've lost the lot.

You tend not to see soya-based or other leguminous food here very much, because it's expensive, it has to be shipped halfway round the planet, and it's ineffective - you get shitty meat that no-one wants to buy.

You need the cows to graze pasture, chew up the grass, break down all that tough cellulose and poo it out as incredibly highly refined fertiliser.

We eat, just about everywhere in the world, because there's six inches of earth, it rains sometimes, and cows and pigs shit solid gold.

I believe that, while a cool technological innovation, the cost of installing and maintaining these panels will be significantly higher than doing the same on open land.

The article’s biggest claim is that devoting one percent of US farmland to agrivoltaics could cover 20 percent of the country’s electricity, pointing at the Texas to Nebraska to California corridor, which is exactly where big mechanized farms are most common.

They run large equipment (combines, sprayer booms, center pivot irrigation systems[1], etc). To keep that working under panels you need tall posts, wide spans, and a layout that matches the equipment paths, which would push structural cost significantly above a ground installation. All maintenance would require a boom truck, not just a ground crew. And center pivots in particular (very common) need an entire circle cleared for the system to pivot, meaning a multi-acre span with no posts for the pivot to hit, which is simply infeasible.

That’s before accounting for pesticide spray and dust on the PVs, or the logistical burden of running PV crews through a working farm.

It’s also worth noting that the study itself was broccoli, which is hand harvested and about the best case crop for this. Even so, the broccoli took 25 extra days to mature. On a farm running multiple rotations a season that lost time has a real cost, separate from the structural costs.

This innovation has a lot more promise on smaller plots. Co-ops, market gardens, specialty crops, and the like that don’t rely on large equipment. I’d be surprised to ever see it on a large farm.
