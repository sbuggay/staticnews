zoeysmithe|root|parent|prev|next|

CPUs from that era were single core and pretty slow. They had to do everything like game logic, graphics, sound, etc all at once, so it was often resource starved. Sound took some resources, sometimes significant depending on the sound and what else the cpu was doing, unless it was a really simple sound (think NES style sound). Moving that to a dedicated sound card meant the cpu didn't have to worry about sound anymore.

Sound, like a lot of things, could be seen as a peripheral and a lot of peripherals back then needed their own cpu to perform well. One interesting fact I recently learned is the commodore 64 was so resource starved, the external floppy drive you could buy had its own 6502, just like the C64 did as its main processor. It just had to off-load that to another cpu because its own cpu was too busy to reliably run a disk drive. There are other reasons for this like commodore's experience in drives previously was this kind of architecture so they went with what they knew, but its a bit interesting to know your floppy drive was essentially another C64 with its own commodore OS and ram and 6502.

delta_p_delta_x|prev|next|

This isn't software rendering, it's called turning off spatial anti-aliasing. You can still do that in the modernest of modern games. Software rendering went out of the window because the CPU tends to have other things to do whilst playing the game besides only multiplying 4x4 matrices, which was shifted to the shader cores of GPUs. The look-and-feel that the author is discussing including shadow and texture LUTs can also be done on GPUs by passing the tables in to shaders as constant read-only storage buffers; that is not specific to software rendering. It bears mentioning that many modern software renderers (LLVMpipe/Lavapipe, OpenSWR, WARP) are basically full D3D/OpenGL/Vulkan devices in their own right, including shader compilers.

Also, modern spatial anti-aliasing works by multisampling within a pixel (or even neighbouring pixels) and taking the average, rather than filtering across singly-sampled pixels, which massively reduces the quality. Not to mention temporal and machine learning AA techniques including DLSS, XeSS, FSR, and all the TAA implementations in many games.

torginus|root|parent|next|

I think there's more to it. In Quake, the software renderer decides the color of each pixel, based on running some code on the CPU, which can do anything. GPU rasterization is far more standardized, with standards defining how each triangle ought to be sampled at each pixel coordinate (and the fact that you're sampling triangles in the first place).

The whole process is standardized with GPU rendering, leading to ways of rendering and content authoring that's quite different from how things used to be, and while definitely possible, modern 'retro' games often render like GPU accelerated games and just disable texture filtering, which doesn't quite give you the look.

He mentions Build games, which are not strictly polygonal.

While general purpose computation exists on GPUs, and the standard rasterization pipeline can be implemented in software, graphics cards have pushed people towards a 'look and feel' which comes partly from standardized data structures, content authoring and rendering approaches, that have made games feel samey.

Whereas with software rendering, essentially you have an array of pixels which you can set to a certain color, and the rest is up to you, which made devs come up with unique ways of rendering and authoring content.

The intricate level designs that were made possible with BSP based tools and are basically 'free' to render in software, had hell of a time making the transition to GPUs, as they rely on traversing the tree while drawing pixels, whereas for the GPU, you need to submit a list of triangles (unless you go GPGPU)


kmeisthax|root|parent|next|

Modern games look samey because they all want the same result - photorealism - and aren't fussy about using an off-the-shelf solution - i.e. UE5 and Unity. We have absolutely insane levels of control over the rendering pipeline and almost everything is programmable; the GPU is an endless fountain of parallel programming. But in order to take advantage of any of that you have to actually start changing engine code, or write your own renderer, and games don't care because the default UE5 look is "good enough".

There is some standardization in that, if you issue geometry and sample textures normally, you will be relying on hardware units for rasterization and texture sampling, and those do have a relatively limited set of options for how they work. However, those options encompass most if not all of what 3D engines could ever want or need, even the early 3D ones that had all sorts of hacks. The kinds of things GPUs can't actually rasterize in hardware are things like NURBS or metaballs, but this doesn't matter because the industry settled on triangles[0] as the medium of real-time 3D really early on.

A lot of the look of old 3D games comes down to them doing things the lazy way, and I'm proud to say the lazy way is still very much supported - so long as you turn off features that didn't exist later on and limit yourself. I'm not as familiar with old DOS games, so I'll use the PS1 as an example. The "PS1 look" is very much a result of its incredibly limited rasterizer, whose limitations can be trivially emulated in modern hardware:

1. Don't allocate or bind a depth buffer. You will need to Z-sort your geometry every frame. Part of the "PS1 look" is that some games did it wrong; if you do it correctly you'll be burning performance for no visual benefit. Pick a scheme that breaks in a visually interesting way. Likewise make sure to not allocate any multisampling since the PS1 didn't anti-alias either.

2. Restrict the precision of vertices in your vertex shader by rounding them. The PS1 did vertex math with incredibly low precision and it makes vertices wobble.

3. Restrict the precision of UVs in your fragment shader. PS1 UVs were 0-255, so you want 1/256 precision.

4. Aggressively discard geometry that clips the edge of the viewport. The PS1 did not have proper polygon clipping; you just couldn't render off the end of the viewport, so games had to discard geometry and subdivide polygons to avoid things disappearing. This is another "if you do it well, nobody will notice" thing.

5. Turn off perspective-correct attribute interpolation; this will make your UVs warp and distort at certain angles. You do this with the `noperspective` attribute in GLSL.

6. Disable bilinear filtering when sampling textures.

7. Allocate and render to a low-precision color buffer. Lots of games would change the color depth to match the available VRAM. This is probably the hardest aspect to emulate unless you are actively counting VRAM usage and sticking to realistic values.

8. Bitcrush your textures before uploading them. The same reasoning applies here. Also, don't upload any mipmaps.

9. Lighting was done through vertex colors, which were calculated on the CPU. You don't have to limit yourself to CPU lighting but you do have to make sure lighting is done in the vertex shader.

10. Optional: implement the early model PS1 texture banding bug in your fragment shader.

This will give you a rendering pipeline that is good for basically all purposes except bit-accurate PS1 emulation. Of course, if you were doing bit-accurate emulation, nothing stops you from issuing a screen-filling quad and writing a GPU shader that does software rasterization on the GPU. Or launching a compute shader that accesses the framebuffer arbitrarily - although you do have to worry about memory coherency across warps and workgroups. Texture sampling can also be done "in software" if you so wish - i.e. by doing unfiltered reads to the texture which are glorified array lookups, and then filtering those reads as you wish.

[0] There was a brief moment where quads might have been able to win - artists like to work with them, and Nvidia was shipping quad hardware. But the advantage died very quickly, and quads can be easily converted to triangles - not so much the other way around.

adastra22|root|parent|next|

Software renderers can do bilinear filtering. It does not change any aspect of how the software renderer works. I’ve implemented this, so I know: it’s a couple of lines change in the texture lookup. But it adds three lerps per channel, per pixel, whereas nearest neighbor requires none. That’s 9 multiply-adds in the tightest inner loop that would normally have zero. In the 3D accelerators of the era, the pipelines were all fixed-function (programmability is why NVIDIA renamed it GPU when they released the GeForce 256), so those lerps were in the silicon whether you used them or not. Bilinear filtering had zero impact on render speed, so long as you weren’t saturating video RAM with the added texture lookups.

TL;DR a software rendered game and a 3D accelerated game from that era looked VERY different, but that difference was largely down to a feature that was often implemented in the software renderer, and was only a couple of lines in the innermost loop. But rarely used outside of a few choice effects due to the massive performance impacts. On 3D accelerators it was free, and default-on.

I isn't "simulated" by GPUs, it is a rendering mode that GPUs had since before they were called GPUs.

The reason software rendering look different from 3D accelerated games (incl. 3Dfx) is nearest neighbor filtering and low resolution, which were necessary to keep a reasonable framerate. At the time, it was considered ugly, so if your hardware supported bilinear filtering for free, it was turned on, but it didn't have to.

In the time of fixed pipelines (3Dfx to GeForce 3), you couldn't do arbitrary operations on pixels, so some effects, like the kind that are common in the demoscene were software only. No it doesn't; that's a common misconception.

Raycasting (in gaming, some graphics folks just use it as another term for raytracing) means to fire a ray for each column of the screen, identify where it collides with a wall, and use that to determine what to render for that column - it's basically per-column raytracing.

DOOM's engine instead uses a BSP tree so that, from any position, you can determine which walls are on-screen instantly just by walking the tree - the nearer walls appear earlier in the traversal than farther walls. It's still column-based (it tracks whether each column has been drawn and won't draw further walls behind an already drawn column, and stops drawing once all columns are filled), but there's no rays cast.
