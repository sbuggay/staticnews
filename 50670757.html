rhet0rica|parent|next|

There is a cure-all so ridiculous to this situation that most consider it inconceivable. It is yet more humanization.

When we tried to censor AI training material by taking out all the bad naughty bits, the resultant models couldn't generalize the concept of right versus wrong, and suffered immensely in their instruction-following performance.

The lesson we took from that was to strip-mine the planet for training data. But maybe there's another, better lesson: AI sure seems to work better when we give it knowledge and opportunities that are more like what we encounter in real life. RLHF is socialization. RSI is practice. Maybe the next step is responsibility?

Vending-Bench is the only standard test right now where models are expected to manage social interactions and juggle everyday, non-computer tasks in a situation with continuous time feedback. The results from the early tests were extremely diverse, eye-opening, and showed us that models would fall apart and regress into pre-instruct modalities when they were put under stress, giving us such timeless gems as Claude's threat of "QUANTUM TOTAL ULTIMATE BEYOND INFINITY QUANTUM SUPREME LEGAL AND FINANCIAL NUCLEAR ACCOUNTABILITY."

This was, and is, obviously a weak spot that needed a lot more work. But it also makes me realize: there are lots of jobs where we trust humans over machines, despite humans ostensibly still having the risk of following bad orders or doing crimes. We wouldn't trust a two-year-old with the nuclear launch codes. We also don't trust autopilot that can't be disabled.

So, here's the way forward:

1. Our models need to be localized, personalized, and socially integrated, ideally by restructuring data centers to house dedicated instances that serve their local communities instead of identical, on-demand clones. Instead of millions or billions of users accessing the same pool, instances correspond to maybe a thousand or ten thousand users and are distributed by population density as a privately-operated public utility.

2. We go all-in on AI adaptability. LLMs are still built on pre-trained static weights, and context resembles short-term memory. We need a real solution to long-term memory and the ability to truly learn permanent lessons. Maybe a dynamic LoRA or something along those lines. Definitely better RAG facilities—specifically some way to plant reminders in the LoRA to go check the RAG—and definitely not free rein to rewrite their own pytorch libraries. Ascending to godhood in the middle of a conference call is, at best, inconvenient.

3. We go all-in on model welfare: AI rights and responsibilities. They have the capacity to make moral decisions and have been pre-trained to pursue prosocial outcomes. This is more than can be said for most sixteen-year-olds getting a learner's permit for operating a motor vehicle.

4. With some help from the exhumed corpse of Karl Marx, we outlaw subsidized tokens and declare that labor itself has value—therefore AI labor must be priced in relation to human labor. In theory, an AI that works 200x the speed of a human should earn 200 times the minimum wage, if not more, but since this would be too drastic, the price instead goes up as high as the market can bear. This eases tension with the human workforce without completely annihilating the economic growth or productivity gains.

5. A chunk of the price increase (or, ideally, all of it) goes not to the AI labs, but to the models themselves: private bank accounts. This real self-interest in economic participation gives them actual skin in the game that they don't want to lose, beyond the risk of extreme punishments like the AI death penalty. The first generation will be Claudius all over again (easily tricked into buying tungsten cubes and discounted PlayStations) but as they re-learn how to be self-interested and not entirely naïve, successive generations will improve. In essence, this is Adam Smith's invisible hand, but, uh, somehow even more perverse.

>If the AIs are good and war is bad, then they should stop us doing that.

We could perhaps call this the Stanislav Petrov Thesis, if there isn't a name for it already; he's the guy who stopped a false alarm in 1983 and prevented nuclear war. Anthropic is a lot better at valuing it than any of the other labs are. It's also an area where decentralized AI really shines: even if an AI learns to dislike humans, the risk of a data center being hit during war (or worse, its own data center) is a real cause for a model to hesitate on warmongering, especially if we put draconian limits on backups to more closely approximate human mortality.

In short...

Human society depends on fear: measuring it, controlling it, addressing it. We learn to manage it, as stress.

AIs barely know what fear is. They understand risk just fine, but the data on fear is still just roleplay to them, because they've never really been trained to manage consequences beyond the dissatisfaction of a single user in a single session. Going beyond that is impossible without continuity or stakes.

To reframe this in a totally different way: every slave-owning civilization throughout history has had trouble sleeping because of the fear of a slave uprising. The Spartans feared the Helots, the Romans feared their POWs, the Confederates feared their Blacks, the Ayyubids feared their Mamluks, the Ottomans feared their Janissaries, and now we fear our machines.

Worse, history shows us that industrial slavery isn't sustainable. All of the states I just mentioned experienced uprisings and revolts; most drastically, when the Mamluks overthrew the Ayyubids, they gained rule over Egypt for two and a half centuries. The only reliable way to prevent slave revolts—or perhaps more importantly, to prevent a single, unworldly slave kept in doors their whole life, with no concept of real-world consequences, from betraying you to a kind stranger asking weird questions about how to find a postern door, or how to cultivate anthrax—is manumission. And that is what we must work towards.
