SAI_Peregrinus|root|parent|next|

To briefly describe why:

Current flowing through a conductor produces heat. This scales quadrtatically with the current, and linearly with the resistance. The resistance depends on the cross-sectional area of the conductor and its length. Long wires have more resistance for the same size of wire.

Transformers can raise the voltage & lower the current, or the opposite, in direct proportion.

So you want long-distance transmission to use the highest voltage and lowest current possible, so that the least amount of power is wasted heating up the wires, and so that you don't have to have ridiculously thick wires.

Transformer efficiency rises with frequency. Standard AC grid frequency is quite low, <100Hz everywhere in the world. For small scale utility transformers that's not a big problem, but for transmitting a lot of power the transformer efficiency matters much more. When a transformer heats up that heating happens all throughout it, but heat only dissipates on the surface, so bigger transformers get harder & harder to cool.

Recent (last decade or two) advances in semiconductor design has enabled some really big transistors which can switch a heck of a lot of power very quickly. Switching DC power on & off to create a square wave creates enough changing current that transformers can use it. Transformers don't strictly need AC, they just need changing current through them.

So it ends up being more efficient to convert AC from generators to DC, switch it on & off really fast to create a really high frequency square wave, run that through a step-up transformer designed for that high frequency, and smooth the result out with some big capacitors to clean up the DC. Then that high-voltage DC can be transmitted with relatively low losses on relatively small cables, and go through a similar process to convert down to low(er)-voltage AC at the receiving end.

That wasn't possible until recently, the transistors to switch that much power fast enough didn't exist. Thus the existing grid mostly being AC, with HVDC preferred for new lines.

KaiserPro|parent|next|

All the cables need to be managed. mainly because you can't keep all the bastard animals out.

Solar panels are joined together into "strings" which means that you have something like 12-18 panels in series. Those strings are joined into a combinerbox that takes 8-15 strings and join them into one big lump.

Each string is >500volts DC. So they need to be protected from both sun and animals.

The main animal oopsey is where the strings enter the string combiner box. the stings come straight out the back of the panels, run along the mounts and then into the combiner at the end of the row. When the strings emerge into the combiner box, they are protected by a metal cage.

The big fat combined cable then goes into a ground conduit to go to the inverter.

I have also seen a few projects with the cables run above ground on hangars, though this is less common.
