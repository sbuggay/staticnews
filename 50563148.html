Trouble is you can declare things to be true that are strictly speaking mathematically false, and from there get to proving any falsehood.

The Dirac delta function is a famous example of something mathematically non-rigorous at its inception, and mathematicians objected to it, but a few decades later Laurent Schwartz developed generalized function theory, that made it work.

Or going even further back, calculus was done in terms of ratios of infinitesimals (like dy/dx) or infinite sums of them (integrals). A century or two later the formulas of calculus were proven rigorously through classical analysis (delta-epsilon proofs) but that lost sight of the original motivations. A century or two after THAT, nonstandard analysis made a rigorous theory of infinitesimals, but reliant on relatively high powered mathematical logic that didn't exist til the 1950s or so.

Right now I think quantum field theory (renormalization, path integrals etc.) have been put on a sound mathematical basis but physicists mostly don't care. And very recently, an AI proved something about Yang-Mills theory that helped rigorize part of the Standard Model of particle physics. I don't understand that last part at all.

Besides physics, a lot of the procedures of machine learning and AI are mathematically suspicious but people use it anyway because it empirically works. People in the field have told me that yeah, they expect mathematicians will someday get around to backing it up, but for now, practitioners are moving forward with what they have.

I think everyone asking for this kind of thing doesn't really understand the scale and complexity of solving a big problem with many agents.

For any of the big things I've worked on, it isn't one "prompt", it's a goal that keeps getting updated and a bunch of back-and-forth steering of the model and sub agents, where what the human is saying to guide the model is heavily context dependent on what the model is currently doing or saying. Much of that guidance will ultimately be useless guidance on dead-end explorations. Also, the model will likely have written huge programs to solve things symbolically or simulate things numerically, and both the programs that were written, and the output of those programs are critical to understanding how the model came up with the things that led to a solution.

Now, I'd be all for publishing the full transcript, but the transcript is likely to be massive. Gigabytes of text, and reproducing the local computation (often sympy or numpy) may be hundreds or thousands of CPU-hours.
