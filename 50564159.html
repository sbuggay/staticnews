This analogy doesn't hold at all. Casinos are engineered for the house to win and the customer to lose because the casino is paying the customer directly. They are disincentivized to let the customer win. If the customer always won, the casino goes out of business very quickly.

Ad auctions are completely different, since they are just a marketplace. A customer's (advertiser's) win comes from a third party, and the point of the ad platform is to connect that third party to the customer. The ad platform would love to be able to make every single customer win all the time. Although, if they did that, it would create a lot more demand and even out the ROI with increased ad prices. But that's not the "evil ad platform" engineering the customers to always lose in the way casinos do, that's simply basic supply/demand economics.

The majority of cases where I see paid ads really crushing a company's budget, particularly among startups, are kind of interesting because they often follow a really specific dynamic. This is anecdata though, so take with a grain of salt.

The startups I see lighting money on fire with performance marketing are usually coping with the quiet understanding that they simply don't have product market fit. I've seen this come up a lot around fundraising cycles. A founder really wants to show a graph that goes up and to the right. Everyone on the team knows that their product simply doesn't win in most deals and isn't generating excitement among users, but no one feels comfortable saying they need to make major changes. And so, instead of investing a ton of marketing budget into a content marketing strategy or something more complex, you put that money into ads and you guarantee a "result". Not a great result, nor a super meaningful one for your business, but if you set a Key Event in Google Ads for signups and then turn on the faucet with little discernment, you will get a lot of signups. And now you don't have a low-quality signup problem, you have a "mid-funnel" problem. And a founder can delude themselves into thinking they just need to nurture these spam signups better, just need to get their onboarding right or "realign their brand".

And then this kicks off a self-perpetuating cycle. Your next-quarter targets are super inflated by this quarter's nonsense, but the only lever you have to hit such lofty goals is the same thing that inflated the numbers in the first place.

In ideal cases, you have a founding team that are simply intellectually honest enough to avoid this or stop it early, but it's surprisingly common to see this play out. Marketing agencies are often a part of this as well, as they can't really control how successful your product is once it's in users' hands. So their best-case scenario is often to sell you on the outcomes they can control, and they can absolutely control top-of-funnel metrics (before getting into how their comp is structured).

Sure, we can make some analogy between ads and gambling, and transitively apply that to ad platforms and casinos. But this article frames ad platforms as exploitative entities engineering advertisers to lose money, when the reality is that the ad platforms provide a marketplace, and via the consequences of supply and demand this marketplace can end up being negative ROI for some advertisers.

Ads have a positive EV while gambling has a negative EV, which completely changes how the entities hosting these products behave. Casinos try to get you to stay on by tricking you into thinking you have a positive EV, while ad platforms get you to stay on by genuinely trying to give you a positive EV. If you aren't getting positive results on your ads, you genuinely have the agency to adjust your ads to try to get positive EV. Casinos are explicitly engineered to give you negative EV regardless of how you behave, and kick you out if you try to change that (e.g. card counting).

If we say that you shouldn't buy the products of an ads platform because the platform tries to get you to buy more, we may as well say you shouldn't buy the products of a grocery store because the store tries to get you to buy more.

> if advertising didn't work people wouldn't use it.

Advertising works, and the unfortunate end result for fungible products is that the product price increases to pay for the advertising.

Producer A and B each cover half the market and compete on price. When A starts advertising to get more market share, the loss is for producer B who's margin decreases due to smaller volume. B also starts advertising. To make ends meet A and B have to increase product price to cover the cost of advertising.

This is very simplified reasoning. The gist is: because advertising works, consumers have to pay more for their products. The more advertising there is, the more prices will increase without products becoming better.
