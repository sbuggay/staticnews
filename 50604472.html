A couple points:

1. Random knowledge can often be used to build a "map" to conclusions that turn out to be useful

e.g. someone once asked me what what country was number 2 in terms of total airplane flights (the US is number 1).

I deduced:

- had to be a big country

- with spread out cities

- and required flying instead of driving

I remembered reading that Brazil has traffic that is so bad and such poor security that people commute to work via helicopter. Even though I wasn't sure it was Brazil, I could guesstimate that it was indeed Brazil.

2. Random facts are great for creating shared references or metaphors across cultural or organizational boundaries.

e.g. I worked with many people from India over the years and being able to quote a famous Bollywood movie or Indian mythology was excellent at helping build rapport etc

3. I was watching a documentary on an Army jungle survival school. The instructor mentioned:

"People ask me if they should read survival guides or biographies of people who survived a calamity. I 100% say yes b/c the after action reports of people surviving are filled with stories of 'then I remembered this random fact from years ago that helped me survive'."

This is the fallacy of composition. Because a small subset of abstract math has been found useful centuries later, spending money on any area of mathematics is justified, regardless of opportunity cost and the fact that physics would eventually lead to the development of the same mathematical concepts.

I often compare trivia and even factoids to a well-stocked larder. The more ingredients you have, the more potential recipes you can devise.

Ken Jennings also made a great point in one of his books: one of the most undervalued things about trivia and knowing "a little about a lot" is that it enables you to engage with people about their hobbies and interests at a more meaningful level.

For example, if somebody tells me that they enjoy juggling, asking if they've mastered mills mess or the box pattern is likely to engender far more engaging conversation than the rather pedestrian "gee golly mr wizard that's nifty keen. How many objects can you juggle at once?"
