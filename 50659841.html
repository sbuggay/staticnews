caniscrator|prev|next|

I got to spend time in a couple of his buildings and it is hard to put into words how different they feel from an ordinary concrete house. The walls are lime, not cement, finished in limewash tinted with natural pigment, so the colour lives in the surface and the plaster breathes. The air inside stays dry and faintly mineral. No trapped damp, no paint smell.

The roofs are what stopped me. No reinforced concrete slab overhead anywhere. The domes and vaults are brick set in lime, shaped so the whole thing stands in pure compression, which is why they need no steel inside them, the thrust runs down into thick masonry walls instead of being held by rebar. His craftsmen rebuilt methods that had nearly died out, the flat brick dome and the muqarnas, and one of them said it had not been done in 300 years. The dome ceilings are finished in ghalibkari.

Why it matters past the look: a reinforced concrete building is on a clock. The steel inside corrodes, expands and spalls the concrete off, which is why so much RC gets written off around the 50 to 60 year mark. Lime has nothing in it to rust. It also heals itself, a hairline crack takes in moisture and the lime redeposits as calcite and closes. And when a lime building finally comes down, the mortar is softer than the brick, so the bricks lift out clean and get used again. Cement mortar is harder than the brick, so you are left with rubble.

As someone from the subcontinent, this is the part that gets me. Indian forts, temples and havelis were built in lime gauged with surkhi (brick dust) and tempered with jaggery and plant extracts, and plenty are still standing after 200, 500, some close to two thousand years. Cement is little more than a century old here, and we took it as progress while forgetting a material that outlives it. What he is keeping alive in Lahore is the craft my side of the border let slip.
