I really don't understand why most people here act like this is some weird unproven and unsafe technology. This is literally a solved problem. They are everywhere in Germany and it's no issue.

The inverters follow the grid AC and shut off their output if they are disconnected or unplugged, so the plug is never "hot". The power they input into your home wiring/the power grid is limited to 800 Watts as to not overload any installation. And the power meters know not to run backwards if you happen to feed power into the grid. Most can even meter how much power you fed in total (you're not going to get paid for it, though)

If you want, you can add battery storage that follows the same rules but lets you save excess power during the day to feed into your home at night or during demand spikes (washing machine, ...).

The setups are dirt cheap (often 200 - 300 EUR for an inverter and 800 - 1000 W(peak) of solar panels) and pay for themselves in about 1-3 years in saved electricity costs.

robshep|root|parent|prev|next|

You might be thinking about it as you might a DC circuit or a battery with electron shuffling one-way down the wire.

But it’s AC, the electrons are shuffling in both directions at 50/60hz

Voltage goes forwards and backwards and odd but current also goes forwards and backwards, for both appliances and generators.

The difference between consumer and producer is only the phase angle between the voltage and current waveform.

Because power is VxA, do that across the wave cycle and the power direction changes for consumers and producers.

At every junction, Kirchhoff’s current law will sum the currents so you get a “net current” waveform on a wire that might have both a kettle and a plug in solar panel.

That summation means the kettle will chomp as much current as the panel can deliver and than take more from the grid. Swap the kettle for a small LED which takes a fraction of the current and the sum gives a NET supply, and on to the next junction.

It took me a while to get my head round it too.

bluGill|parent|next|

The biggest problem with plugging a generator into an outlet is you need a double ended cord. If you use everything correctly they work fine, but there are too many ways things can go wrong. The most obvious one is you plug everything in, start the generator, and the trip over the cord on you way back inside, thus sending the live male end flying toward you. Once in a while a kid will see these dual male plugs and plug them in - lots of ways things can go wrong here.

There is also the problem of backfeeding, but this is more theoretical - even if you know to limit your power use, your neighbors won't and so your generator will trip its own breaker. Linemen are taught to either treat power lines like they are live, or to short them to ground - it can happen they don't do this and you kill a linemen, but still not as big a worry as it is made out.

They make generator "inlets" that sidestep all these problems when used correctly. Most common I've seen is a block so you can't have the inlet and the main breaker both on at once.

How do these work when plugged in to only one of the split phases on a US-style 120 system? Because I'm a nerd, I know which legs of the phase go to which outlet in my house, but I don't expect everyone to pay attention to that. I assume something has to take that into account somewhere.

bluGill|parent|next|

There is no problem and so nothing needs to account for it. A split phase is just a transformer with three windings, and one is connected to the other. We call one side (high voltage) the primary, and the other two secondary, but the transformer doesn't care at all, it can send power either way (there are limits but we are well under the maximum rating of the transformer so we can ignore them). If you only draw 240 volts then the two connected windings act as a single secondary, if you only draw 120 then one of the two secondaries provides power. Any other combination - the transformer doesn't care.

The power from the plug-in outlet is consumed by any devices connected on the same circuit first. Any extra flow through the electric panel’s bar connecting the circuits on the same 120V phase, where the devices on them use the extra next. Any excessive power flows back out of the meter on the 120V phase line on your service connection to the transformer at the street block. The transformer might feed it to your neighbors. In any case the transformer feed electricity to the other 120V phase line back into your service connection, the meter, the panel, the other split phase bar, the circuits on it, and the outlets on them. The devices on the other split phase circuits technically are not using the plug-in power directly.

Most modern meters measure electricity going in and out on both split phase lines as a whole, as a net amount, so the electricity consumed in the other split phase circuits is offset by the plug-in power. However some old meters add the outgoing electricity up as consumption so you are paying for the electricity fed into the grid. Call your utility company to make sure.

Also, unless you have a net metering agreement with your utility company, you won’t get paid for any excessive electricity fed back into the grid.

causesothere|parent|next|

Yes, there are serious safety concerns. Both islanding, which is where a circuit is hot when it shouldn't be, and from a circuit overload.

Your circuit breaker protects your circuits by putting all the load (eg your microwave) on one side, and your supply (mains power) on the other. If too much load is present, the circuit breaker trips.

However, plugin solar puts invisible supply on the load side, which your circuit breaker cannot see. Let's make an absurd example so you can see what the problem is, then scale it back.

Imagine someone buys 5 panels that can each supply 5amps. They plug them into outlets on a 40 amp circuit. They then proceed to run their toaster, microwave, vacuum, and hair dryer. They can draw 65 amps through the wires before the circuit breaker trips. (40 from mains, 25 from the panels)

Let's say they had a good electrician who installed 6AWG wiring on that circuit, which is rated for 50 amps. The circuit breaker used to trip at 40, so no problem. But now up to 65 can go across the wires and suddenly you are exceeding rating for wires.

Now you might say, "most of these are 3.5 watts, and they clearly say don't use more than one". But I say that's a "good intentions solution". We should make it mechanically impossible to do the wrong thing. Maybe these panels could try to communicate over the electrical wires, and if they detect a signal from another panel they should self limit. Maybe we need to rethink circuit panel design. But as it stands people will absolutely buy one of these, decide they love it, and then buy 4 more.
