The site is taking forever to load but complexity theory doesn't really have the hard walls at things like O(nlogn) that people seem to think.

You can always always tradeoff time vs space. It's actually a fundamental part of complexity theory called Blums speedup theorem by the guy who invented complexity theory no less.

As an example you give me an nlogn solution and i take a portion of the inner loop, put it in a lookup table (who cares if the lookup table is 2^n in size, that's not what we're measuring here since we're specifically measuring time not space) and suddenly i have something better than nlogn since that lookup table is O(1).

That's not to say there's some merit to all of this. It's just that people shouldn't be as astonished by these results as they are. It's complexity theory. There's literally no such thing as a hard wall where something is nlogn in time and can't get lower. It can always get lower. But you need both the time and space complexities together to reason about this.

ComplexSystems|root|parent|next|

This is a very loaded blog post. A unit-cost RAM is a fairly standard model of computation when working with algorithms. When we say that comparison sorting of strings takes O(n log n) time, for instance, we are also implicitly assuming a unit cost for comparing two strings. In real life, comparing two strings scales linearly with string length. Maybe that's silly, but it's par for the course. The original FFT is also assuming unit cost multiplication when we say it is O(n log n) time - we mean O(n log n) multiplies.

Similarly, yes, it's a galactic algorithm, but these things are still viewed as meaningful. The fact that it is even possible at all with a unit cost RAM asymptotically is an incredible result that nobody expected.
