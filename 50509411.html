I personally operate a fairly advanced closed-loop chlorination system, and this is beyond screwed up.

These systems can easily fail, and they tend to fail in a way that causes them to measure low or zero chlorine and thus overdispense chlorine. More below on why.

First, this means that an operator must always ensure safety, both against excessive chlorine and against insufficient chlorine. In a commercial context, that means that a real human (or maybe a totally different sensor design, e.g. a colorimetric sensor) need to measure the pool chlorine level, preferably with a high quality titration (drop test) every day before opening the pool. They should probably record the result in pen on water resistant paper, but this part is icing on the cake.

Second, hoping that an email gets noticed is ridiculous. If you do not have positive confirmation that the pool is safe to open, don’t open it.

Third, the systems should be sized so that they cannot overdose quickly. The maximum dispensing rate should be such that hours of continuous operation would be required to reach an uncomfortable level, let alone a dangerous level. (Hot tubs are sort of an exception here.)

Fourth, everyone should be using a reasonable amount of CYA in their pool. Even at a lowish level of 30ppm CYA it takes a lot of chlorine to become dangerous unless the operator has also lost control of pH. I realize that some codes are confused about this.

Fifth, chlorine is smelly. Someone should have noticed, even just casually. Admittedly, lots of swimmers may be conditioned to stinky pools, and checking for safety isn’t their job. But the staff should notice.

Sixth, the systems I’ve seen beep when there’s an alert. It’s easy to add flashing lights, too. (In fact, the system I’m familiar with automatically shuts down if it feeds for more than X minutes without reaching the setpoint, and you cannot turn this feature off. You can, however, defeat it by switching the power to the whole controller.)

—- sensors —-

Actual free chlorine sensors intended for pools use an amperometric probe immersed in iodide solution or gel, separated from the pool water by a fancy membrane. Free chlorine oxidizes iodide to iodine, and the sensor measures the iodine concentration. If the membrane fails (or general wear and tear or excessive pressure swings) cause the iodide to be lost, then the chlorine can’t react and the sensor reads zero (or low if there’s tiny bit of iodide left).

Interestingly, these sensors are rather expensive devices that appear to mostly come from France and Germany. The Chinese vendors seem to be rebranding European parts (!), and the major US vendor is very clearly reselling a French company’s product.

There are some vendors who sell a cheap (or absurdly marked up) potentiometric ORP-based closed-loop system. In my considered opinion, these are worse than useless.

amluto|root|parent|next|

lol

CYA stands for cyanuric acid, not cover your ass :) It’s added because it reversibly binds to chlorine. This causes the majority of the chlorine in the water to be in a mostly inert state in which it’s not irritating, doesn’t offgas and is protected from UV. You need very little active chlorine to control bacteria, viruses and algae, but you need considerably more chlorine to avoid the water becoming locally dechlorinated due to any sort of contamination that consumes chlorine. CYA helps fix this mismatch.

There are a couple of pathways by which free chlorine (hypochlorous acid / hypochlorite) can turn into chlorine gas. The kinetics are complicated, and I’m not sure a fantastic model exists. In general it seems to be faster in acidic conditions and also faster if air is bubbled through the water.
