woodruffw|root|parent|next|

> Can you explain your decision?

Not easily, because it's closer to a convergence of smaller engineering decisions into an emergent choice to ship a CA bundle.

But the short version: lots of tools ship their own CA bundle. This is not ideal for distributors like Homebrew, because people expect one CA root program for the entire distribution. As a result, Homebrew tries (and sometimes fails) to apply a consistent root program across its tools. Apple's own roots are not inherently best suited for all Homebrew users, so we end up shipping a composite of their roots and the Mozila root program.

> What happens if they are in disagreement?

They are in disagreement, all the time! Root program operators are totally free to manage their trust roots as they please. The only forcing function is a desire to honor users' connections; differences in root programs end up getting papered over somewhat by cross-signing between different CAs.

jeroenhd|root|parent|next|

> There needs to be an easy way for end users to personally drop trust of CAs they don't need and have that decision respected and remembered.

There is. On macOS go to Keychain Access > System Roots and disable the kind of trust you want to disable. There's probably a scriptable method for that.

On Windows, open the management console to "Certificates". Then go through the list and move/remove/distrust any certificates you dislike. There are also powershell tools you can use.

On Linux, the situation is a bit annoying. You can remove all CA certificates manually and make the directory read-only to break any attempt from your distro to interfere with the list. You need to check your distro documentation to see if they use /etc/ca-certificates or /etc/ssl or something like /etc/pki for reference. Some distros offer a partial GUI as well.

Programs that deem themselves smarter than the OS (true in the case of macOS and various Linux distros), like browsers, the JVM or NodeJS applications, can have their own cert stores.

You will need to acknowledge half a dozen certificate prompts for every website you visit after this, though. Make sure to compare the fingerprints of the CA root certificate with the real ones you might find elsewhere, because it's easy to generate your own CA and copy all certificate information signed by your self-generated key.

SSH in typical use is not vulnerable to a compromised root certificate, instead it's "just" vulnerable to you having no idea whether you can trust that host key or not, so you just blindly say yes and hope for the best. At least if the key changes, you'll know something was wrong; maybe it was this connection that got compromised, maybe it was the first connection that got compromised, maybe the legitimate host just rotated their keys, who knows. SSH security against MITM is mostly based on lack of motivated attackers and wishful thinking.

Its no more wishful than expecting a root of trust to stay trustworthy or expecting a trusted name to consistently resolve to the same party.

And there's a lack of motivated attackers for a reason, the attack scales poorly. If you're trying to take over the world, or sell to somebody who is, you're better off focusing on compromising a root of trust or tampering with name resolution, because attacks like that are sustainable at scale.
