> worked around these types of devices and their interfaces..

This is not a problem of devices and interfaces. You are supposed to generate the wrapping key on the HSM itself. And the HSM ensures that the keys are generated with a strong enough security parameter, not some silly 1 byte string. Then the HSM wraps the key inside of itself and exports it in the encrypted form. You take this ciphertext, and the wrapping key and send it over to the other party.

So if they generate the wrapping key with a funny value, that means they didn't use HSM to generate it but their system openssl. But then, HSMs typically don't allow you to export private keys without wrapping them first unless you first tweak around with security flags (not recommended at all), or simply generate the private key using your system openssl too (LMFAO).

Source: I work for one of the major HSM vendors.

Using RSA as an example, the prime factors are supposed to have the same bit length (plus or minus 1 for carryover) for a similar reason. If the smaller factor has 1 fewer bit than the larger factor, it will be discovered twice as quickly by brute force, and 4x faster if it has 2 fewer bits, 8x faster if it has 3 fewer bits, etc. You could technically create a 4096 bit RSA key where one of the factors is 3 and the other is some other giant prime, but it would be immediately discovered by a naive brute force attack. The key generator is responsible for selecting prime factors that cannot be easily derived from the algorithm's output.
