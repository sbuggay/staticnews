sho|parent|next|

> the main reason is that LLMs are generators, they like to generate code where code removal or reduction would solve the problem

Don't over-index on this choice of words. LLMs can just as easily generate a diff that removes or refactors code, they don't have any intrinsic "preference" to always add more code, unless it's been trained into them.

A lot of the early training had concentrated on "wow factor" one-shot style demoable use cases, where the models indeed ended up with a tendency to assume they were in a "produce something new for me right now" situation and often or always started churning out additional code, even without all relevant information. As training regimes improve, and as customer demand evolves, this is changing, and the latest big models place a lot more emphasis on maintenance tasks - still not enough, but it's at least in the mix. As they mature, I'd expect the models' "instinct" to gradually align with what everyone's idea of a senior software engineer is, taking time to figure out if they can simplify and refactor rather than always adding more.

It's a new field, it will take time to get it right, but I don't doubt it will eventually be right.

> Because they are trained on mediocre code that is the vast majority of what's available on the internet

Again, this is outdated. It's true that the earliest training took this "spray and pray" approach, but data quality filtering is now a huge focus and the labs have made a lot of effort (and progress) in figuring out what good code is and getting the models to follow that. They've massively improved, in my experience, and it'll only get better. Again - give it time, this is new.

It sounds like maybe what you want is to have the "shuffle" mode play songs close to truly randomly while still penalizing songs that have been played too much recently. This gives us an optimization problem:

    minimize KL(π || uniform) - β E_π[times played already],
where β is an inverse temperature determining how much you dislike repetition and π is your policy. Here I've arbitrarily chosen a reverse KL divergence because it leads to a nicer closed form:

    π(song) ∝ exp(-β⋅"times song played already").
I wonder if anyone has implemented this before in a music playlist.

My car's player (reading from a flash drive full of MP3s) doesn't have "shuffle", it has "random". The difference is subtle, but it means that each time it advances to a new track, it selects that at random. The result is that I never hear all the tracks in an album/playlist without hearing a bunch of repeats. Infuriating!

But when I play from my phone or PC, I use PlexAmp (backed by my Plex media server), which is the opposite extreme. Not only does it know what randomly shuffling is, but the server has analyzed the sonic profile of every track, and knows about similar artists and similar albums. So you can play an "artist radio" featuring that artist and other related stuff. And you can choose an auto DJ, which inserts between tracks the closest sonic match to the song it just played, or a brief detour of about 3 tracks that are spaced along the path from the track that just played to the one that's about to play. It's fantastic for discovering stuff you haven't heard in a long time within a very large library.

To implement shuffle play, it is not necessary to actually shuffle filenames in memory. Just generate a random number modulo the number of files, and then play that file. That way your program can shuffle play any number of files, no worries about running out of memory.

By the way, the Roku box has a "media player" app in it by default, and it cannot shuffle play more than a handful of files. Completely useless.

That's random play, not shuffle play. Random play can repeat songs before all songs are played. Shuffle play will play all songs exactly once before any repeats occur. Random play "puts the card back into the deck" before dealing the next one.
