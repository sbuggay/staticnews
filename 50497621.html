CompCert has also had extensive empirical evaluation to vet the theorems. Famously the CSmith paper (https://users.cs.utah.edu/~regehr/papers/pldi11-preprint.pdf) has this rather choice quote:

> As of early 2011, the under-development version of CompCert is the only compiler we have tested for which Csmith cannot find wrong-code errors. This is not for lack of trying: we have devoted about six CPU-years to the task. The apparent unbreakability of CompCert supports a strong argument that developing compiler optimizations within a proof framework, where safety checks are explicit and machine-checked, has tangible benefits for compiler users.

At my work we've extended this to CPU millennia. I hasten to add this of course doesn't rule out the possibility of poorly formed assumptions in the semantics etc (it's empirical after all) but it helps to build evidence that we have the theorems and semantic design right. More generally differential testing of this sort offers a possible way forward to build evidence in favor of theorem statements beyond expert examination.

> Every discussion of formal verification must come with a list of caveats, a precise understanding of what is trusted, what is proven and what is not.

Anyone that seriously participates in this work practices this. The whole community. More importantly this is precisely the point of writing down a theorem so that we can closely examine the assumptions. To abuse the CompCert example yet further it's now entirely possible to draw the envelope of assumptions, which are many for a non-toy compiler: https://arxiv.org/html/2201.10280v2.

Proofs are useful insofar as the formal specification is sound. The problem is that building a sound formal specification is the hard part, and it's not something that LLMs are going to be particularly good at. Yes, they can write a formal specification. But, is this formal specification sound? Is it usable? Is it applicable? Is it complete? This all requires human level reasoning and perhaps beyond to verify.

Once you have a sound formal specification, building proofs that software follows this specification is actually fairly straightforward. LLMs aren't doing any magic here.

nanolith|root|parent|next|

I can give a real-life example. WPA2 was formally verified at the protocol level, but the formal specification -- while reviewed by multiple humans -- was flawed, which directly led to the KRACK attack.

The Needham-Schroeder Public-Key Protocol failed to include the responder's identity in the protocol, which led to a man-in-the middle attack found via model checking. This is despite numerous proofs -- some even machine-checked -- of implementations against specification.

When cryptocurrencies had their formal methods moment a few years ago, there were numerous vulnerabilities and attacks found in cryptocurrency protocols despite claims of being formally verified, and despite much of this verification being published in Rocq, Lean, etc. If the specifications are unsound or flawed, then the implementations will also be. While in these cases, it's quite clear that the folks performing the formal methods work were likely inexperienced, I don't see how an LLM will create anything more sound.

Remember that an LLM is trained on publicly available information. It's not going to make a leap above and beyond what has been published openly, and much of what has been published with respect to security is related to student projects (e.g. "Let's build a formally verified AES implementation") or one-off applications, some of which, like WPA2 KRACK, have incomplete specifications.

This is a difficult problem that experienced proof engineers get wrong, after performing rounds of threat modeling and review.

nanolith|root|parent|next|

It's not just a matter of competence: it's a matter of knowledge, experience, and tenacity. Currently, frontier model companies are trying to tackle the problem of their models writing potentially unsafe software by demonstrating that their models can build formal proofs. However, someone who lacks formal verification experience, the domain knowledge to make such software safe, or the understanding to combine the two into formal specifications will build bad proofs, because the formal specifications will be unsound. LLMs don't necessarily improve this outcome. An inexperienced engineer, with or without LLM assistance, is just as likely to not add a property to verify that nonces aren't re-used as the WiFi Alliance.

WPA2 wasn't designed by a single individual. It was designed by a committee of people including respected cryptographers and information security folks. They missed the fact that nonces could be re-used in the design, and they trusted the formal specifications that lacked a check on nonce re-use. It happens. Unfortunately, formal specifications tend to trap folks into a sense of complacency. Soundness is a hard problem, and if it's not solved, folks will make bad assumptions. "But, it's been formally verified..."

Could LLMs accelerate tactics and Curry-Howard level hole filling of specifications? Absolutely. But, they aren't likely to do much better than humans at building sound specifications. This is a difficult problem, for experienced humans with a narrow specialization in formal methods.

sirclueless|parent|next|

I disagree with EWD, I find the distinction between a "bug" and an "error" helpful. In any robustly engineered system, existence of a bug means that multiple errors have been made by multiple people. Singling out "the programmer who made the error" is not only unhelpful, it's misleading and disingenuous to even try.

If I write an Outlook networking server that handles 100k req/s, and it fails in production when someone points 200k req/s at it, there is a bug. Whose error is it? Is it my fault for writing a component that only handles 100k req/s? Is it the code-reviewer who didn't catch that it only handles 100k req/s? Is it the team lead who told the programmer it needed to handle 100k req/s? Is it the project manager who assigned this task to a junior engineer who can't be trusted to know that the system might need to handle more than 100k req/s? Is it the business leaders who laid off everyone who knew it would need to handle way more than 100k req/s? Is it the engineers who wrote load tests that didn't reach 200k req/s? Is it the operations team that configured this in a way where it can get 200k req/s?

In reality any one of these people could have made a decision that avoided this bug. There are always many errors on the critical path of a bug, identifying "the root cause" means you have already made a leap in logic and concluded which issue(s) you are planning on addressing and which you have decided are not worth tackling.
