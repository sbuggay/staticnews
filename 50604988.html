jgil|parent|prev|next|

Vanilla OpenWrt is not necessarily more secure than a vendor fork (though dropping the cloud/remote-access layer obviously reduces the attack surface). It really depends on the build and config options.

For example, by default (in OpenWrt):

- The web UI serves plaintext HTTP and doesn't redirect to HTTPS, even though it generates a self-signed cert for 443 on first boot. [1]

- The router generates a persistent DUID from its LAN MAC and sends it to your ISP. [2][3]

- Userspace is FORTIFY_SOURCE=1 and PIE/ASLR are not enabled on all binaries. BusyBox (which runs the WAN DHCP and NTP clients) isn't built with PIE unless you set PKG_ASLR_PIE_ALL. [4]

There are other defaults and unneeded packages that can be changed or removed. I spent a lot of time tuning the configs and switching some daemons to Rust before ultimately deciding that a NixOS gateway + separate AP was more viable.

[1] https://github.com/openwrt/openwrt/blob/main/package/network...

[2] https://github.com/openwrt/openwrt/blob/main/package/base-fi...

[3] https://github.com/openwrt/openwrt/blob/main/package/network...

[4] https://github.com/openwrt/openwrt/blob/main/config/Config-b...

cesarb|root|parent|prev|next|

> but the moment they do an active MITM I don't think a self-signed cert will be very useful

In my experience, it's TOFU (Trust On First Use): once you accept the self-signed certificate the first time, Firefox will store its hash and no longer warn you as long as the certificate doesn't change. An active MITM after that point should still warn about an untrusted certificate, since the certificate would no longer be the same as the stored exception.

(And the same would happen if you initially accepted the self-signed certificate while under an active MITM, and the attack later stops; the only way to avoid a warning would be to have the attack be running continuously since the first use.)

dspillett|root|parent|next|

There have been at least a couple of remote access issues with their kit over the years, though none recently that I remember.

Common advice is to keep all such interfaces (not just for Ubiquiti devices, for all such kit) on a separate network leg, and don't even allow connections from your local LAN, either physically separating them on their own switch or using vLANs. Use a VPN to access that network leg when required, remotely or locally.

As extra paranoia measures, I suggest having the VPN mediated by a device from another manufacturer (or something you have put together with a basic Linux or BSD box), then an exploit has to get into two different environments in order to affect you, and nesting VPNs (with different credentials, of course) for external access so you VPN to local then to the management vLAN/leg so there is no direct external route to your management interfaces. I don't go that far with my home setup, but my router doesn't answer to anything on the public interface, I need to VPN in to another box (a small VM on my “home lab” machine) to administrate it remotely if I ever need to (this does, of course, mean there are extra things that I have to be sure are kept running and survive power-outs properly, by being on the UPS for short problems and making sure they come back up in a sane state after a long problem).
