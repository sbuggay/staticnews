I keep seeing people rediscovering tried and true software engineering principles and saying AI makes them "matter more"... And I'm a bit skeptical of that framing.

Things like platform engineering, DevOps, code quality, linting, CI/CD, QA, etc. were just as foundational before coding agents as they are now (regardless of never being called "pattern engineering"). I'd argue what's changed is less their importance than the consequences of neglecting them.

I think a lot of this is how we as an industry are coping with the realization that the narrative we've been sold over the past few years, that AI was going to render software engineering a "solved problem", isn't actually true.

So instead of saying "Hm, it looks like being able to arbitrarily generate massive amounts of plausible code has exposed just how much of software engineering was never about writing code in the first place"... We say "Wow, these amazing AI coding agents are so powerful we're having to formalize new levels of abstraction for engaging with software engineering!"

Idk, maybe I'm being a yum yucker. People can call stuff whatever they want. Just irks me when there's plenty of prior art, knowledge, instruction, etc. that has run into these problems years and years ago that may be more useful to center than coining new shibboleths.

I think the difference is in effective bottlenecks. Previously, the ability actually produce code was a bottleneck just as much as the ability to architect and engineer that code, or to make effective product decisions was. Now, the code production bottleneck has been largely reduced - but those other bottlenecks have not been, bringing them to significantly larger prominence.

The reason this matters for baseline processes and patterns is that when the system is not set up for AI to trivially copy good practices, it won't, and that will cost the architect and engineering bottlenecks even more just at a time when they are becoming the most obvious limit on velocity.

So, yes, they always mattered for lots of reasons. But the relative impact on velocity, which is at the forefront of everyone's mind right now, has changed.
