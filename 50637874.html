If it's true and a current frontier model can discover it, some people probably already know, especially governments that may have discovered it before and exploited it rather than revealing it, or governments that may have frontier LLMs capabilities themselves (bought from frontier labs, or internally developed, or hacked from frontier labs). If not, they will probably know pretty soon.

Disclosing the problem sooner somewhat accelerates responses to it, and somewhat reduces the amount of traffic that attackers can exploit, or the quantity of credentials that they can forge.

The details might depend on the nature of the attack and whether it applies, for example, to DLP only, ECDLP only, both, RSA only, specific curves only, etc., because that might affect who can migrate easily or not.

One general problem that we've already seen in the past is that there are a lot of embedded systems and other devices that are hard to upgrade, so there are things that have been fixed long ago in upstream software releases, but devices that no longer get updates can't be patched. I think the trend is toward more devices having readily-upgradable software over time, but it's starting from a pretty bad baseline. I remember when CA/B Forum deprecated an older primitive (or keysize?) used in certificates, there were a bunch of credit card terminals that couldn't be upgraded to accept the new publicly-trusted certificates (so first some of the merchant banks or their infrastructure providers got a delay in the deprecation, and eventually some of them ended up using certificates from an expired publicly-trusted CA because some of the devices in question didn't enforce root CA expiration dates, or something like that!).

The best case for a response and one which might also justify a complicated coordinated disclosure plan would be if a vulnerability affected some widely used primitives in TLS but not all of them, such that many sessions could be protected by changing one or both sites' ciphersuite preference ordering. In that case, maybe a large number of entities that ship default ciphersuite preference orderings could be told about how to change those defaults and then release the changes all on the same day.

But in general, the secret would probably not reliably stay secret for long and would probably leak and/or be rediscovered, and might already have been discovered by some attackers, and so in general starting whatever mitigations are possible soon is better in terms of reducing the total amount of successful exploitation.

I guess I've talked myself into some form of "well, it depends" rather than "immediate disclosure is obviously the safest".

As many readers will know, if there is, for example, a classical attack on some important class of public key primitives that runs as efficiently as a quantum attack that requires a large quantum computer, then things are pretty bad because post-quantum primitive are not very widely deployed, and usually require public keys that are very large compared to traditional public-key primitives (so we might have higher latency for session establishment, less ability to do secure sessions on non-broadband links, and higher CPU overhead for TLS endpoints).

mkl|parent|next|

Discovering novel theorems is trivial (there are an infinite number of them which can be enumerated systematically), but discovering useful novel theorems is quite different. In these big AI proofs many novel theorems are discovered and proved as steps towards the main result (like helper functions), so those are novel theorems which are useful by some definition.

Most of the ones in the news are proofs of existing conjectures, which become theorems once they're proved. "Conjecture" is a fancy word for guess, but the conjectures that become known tend to have quite a bit of evidence in their favour (and no solid evidence against, as a single counterexample proves it false).

But it underscores your observation that there are infinite number of theorems (typically in almost every part of mathematics and about almost every kind of object), though most are not very interesting or very useful for learning more about anything outside of themselves.

A very closely related fact is that a formal grammar of a natural language typically produces an infinite number of different grammatical sentences, but most of the easiest-to-generate ones are extremely boring. For example:

Cats are cute.

Cats are cute and cats are cute.

Cats are cute and cats are cute and cats are cute.

You could also try something like

Tabby cats are cute and black cats are cute and white cats are cute and calico cats are cute.

and you can start adding other adjectives, or relative clauses describing characteristics of the cats (which formal grammars will typically claim can be nested arbitrarily deeply, like "cats that cute cats that cute cats that cute cats meow at meow at meow at are cute").

Cf. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffalo_buffalo_Buffalo_buffal...

Interesting writing (and interesting mathematics) can now be done by both humans and AIs, but are both harder than the examples that show that an infinite amount of writing (and an infinite amount of mathematics) is possible!

As a side note, formal proof theories provide a fairly exact analogy between "being a valid proof of a theorem" and "being a grammatical sentence of a (formalization of the grammar of a) natural language"!
