I was having the same old boring debate with someone the other day: "why would you want to live forever when after age 80 ~ 90+ there's no point in living anymore?"

Most people, even technical people, can't imagine being 90 years old and being indistinguishable visually and in terms of mental capacity from a 20-year-old. They imagine decades of suffering with bone aches, diseases and dementia... even technical people who should know better that when you optimize a system that's full of technical debt, it's almost all upside.

I'll have plenty to do and plenty to think about when I'm 100 years old, assuming I have a body and brain capable of it.

"Yes, but what about all the people you'll have lost by then?"

First of all, if I get to live a long and healthy life they probably do too.

Second of all, we adapt. It's not all sunshine and rainbows, but getting to live for as long as you want, as healthy as you want, is worth the grief.

> Virtually all old mice die of cancer, especially in the lab

Bret Weinstein did research on this topic which suggested that this is caused by lab mice having extraordinarily long telomeres which cause them to be good at cell repair but bad at limiting neoplasm growth due to delayed apoptosis. The result of this combination would be that lab tests on potential damage done by preparations on these mice is less valid 'cause they are better at repairing such damage than the average mammal due to those long telomeres. It also means nearly all these mice succumb to cancer. They're not normal mice, at all.

The reserve-capacity hypothesis: evolutionary origins and modern implications of the trade-off between tumor-suppression and tissue-repair

Bret S Weinstein 1 , Deborah Ciszek

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11909679/
