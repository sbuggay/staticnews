hansvm|root|parent|next|

Sorry, it's hard to capture all the nuance a conversation like this needs without writing so much that the length keeps people from capturing the important parts.

If you want to maximize speed through a curve plus straightaway, you should pick a path through that curve minimizing curvature at the end so that you are able to accelerate out the end of the curve. As a reasonably good approximation for normal driving, your tires have the same traction regardless of the direction of the load being placed on them. That gives you a budget of net force you're able to apply. If you're near the sideways force limit going around a curve and add acceleration (or braking -- always do your braking before you enter the turn, or gradually reduce it as the curvature increases), the wheels with those extra forces will break traction. The solution is to apply that acceleration at a point of milder curvature.

Taking that idea back down to safe driving speeds, the same idea exists when considering crossing a margin of error rather than the ultimate slip threshold. All else equal, accelerating as you come out of a curve, if you've chosen your path through the curve correctly (really important to get that right; there's a curve back near my hometown which has taken out half a dozen people over the years because its curvature increases at the end and they haven't planned for it), will minimize your time through the curve and the following straightaway at your chosen safety threshold.

However, all of that is predicated on minimizing time assuming perfect knowledge and perfect driving. The acceleration itself is actively harmful from a safety perspective. In FWD, the wheels losing traction are the ones connected to your steering wheel. That's not ideal. In RWD you're prone to oversteer and spinning out of control. In AWD you can get either or both of those failure modes. Moreover, navigating typical curves, you're so far from slippage that the technique of accelerating at the end just means you'll go from the speed limit to something greater. For winter curves, the margin of traction uncertainty is so great that you can't gain much from accelerating out the end anyway (and have a pretty bad risk profile -- accelerate on a straightaway and slip a bit and with a bit of training you're basically fine, slip off a curve and you have a really bad rest of your short life potentially).

Safely accelerating in a curve is a learned skill though, and most people don't care that much about driving. Unless it looks like they're interested in more depth, my advice is always to brake before the curve and accelerate afterward. It's safe, fast enough, and easy to remember.

> I was told by our school electrician that long ago the clocks were adjusted by the power company. So around 2am DST they would either send power at 59Hz* to fall back or 61Hz* to spring forward. This was from the era when power grids were separate and analog.

That wouldn't have worked.

Grid-tied synchronous clocks (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_clock) were once ubiquitous and did derive their timekeeping stability from the grid frequency, but that is only suitable for keeping time once set. It works because electric grids are frequency-controlled and stabilized, where deviations from the reference frequency are corrected over long intervals.

In principle you could send power at 59 or 61Hz to adjust these clocks, but there are two problems:

1. The grid frequency isn't arbitrary, it's also directly coupled to the rotary inertia of every turbine that powers the grid. This is why power fluctuations cause frequency changes: temporary oversupply or undersupply causes these turbines to slow down or speed up respectively, using their inertia as power storage. Potentially screwing up every other piece of mechanical equipment tied to the grid for the sake of clock changes is... a poor optimization.

2. A one-Hertz difference isn't enough to change the clocks in a timely way. To change time by an hour you need to gain or lose 3600 * 60 = 216k cycles, but a 1Hz change gains or loses one cycle per second. It'd take about 2.5 days to complete a one-hour change via this mechanism.
