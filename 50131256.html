> Except turning off when prices are too low for too long

Even that is unlikely. Most nuclear power plants operate with a "strike price": they negotiate a fixed price, and the government makes up the difference between that fixed price and the actual market price.

For example, Hinkley Point C in the UK has negotiated a strike price of about €160/MWh. When the market trades at €100/MWh, the taxpayers will pay them an additional €60/MWh for every unit they produce. To make it even worse: the contract is for every unit they could produce. If the reactor is technically able to generate power but the grid operator, for whatever reason, decides to source it elsewhere, the taxpayers are still on the hook for €160/MWh for every MWh the reactor could've generated but didn't.

The result of this is that reactors aren't turned off when the prices go low and will always be the last to get throttled down. Low grid demand? The expensive reactor will keep running at full blast, it'll be the dirt-cheap solar which gets turned off.

This is why building new nuclear is such a horrible idea from an economic perspective: you are essentially locking in those high power prices for the next ~60 years, no matter what happens. Someone could invent a magical zero-point energy warp drive and provide us with unlimited free power, and we'd still be paying for those nuclear power plants!

Want to build additional nuclear? First convince an investor to take the risk and operate on the free market, don't come begging the government for a giant guaranteed-profit handout.

jillesvangurp|root|parent|next|

There's primary, final, and useful energy. Easy to confuse them. Final is what is delivered for consumption. Useful energy is what is actually delivered in a useful form (motion, light, etc.) The rest is rejected energy (heat). This terminology simplifies a complex chain of events that is about moving and transforming fossil fuels from well (primary energy) via refineries, shipping, trucking, pumping, etc. to gas tank in your car (final energy). After which you burn it to actually get useful energy. There are losses throughout this chain.

The fallacy here is counting barrels of oil, shipments of coal, or whatever and assuming it needs to be replaced 1:1 by what used to be called alternative energy to get the same useful energy. But of course with electrified systems, the losses from generation to use are a lot less. Things like wires and transformers have their own inefficiencies of course. But the numbers are pretty good. You end up with 80-90% useful energy. As opposed to 30-40% for typical fossil fuel plants. And far less for any combustion engine vehicles.

It's called the primary energy fallacy. As Michael Liebreich suggests, there are people using this term that genuinely don't get it. And there are those using it intentionally that do. But the bottom line is that their math tends to be off by about 3-5x.
