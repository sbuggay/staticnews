This builds on the Kickmix quantum language from Google. Essentially, Kickmix is an assembly language for classically simulatable quantum circuits, and finding more efficient circuits is basically a game of code golf with the assembly instructions. That means LLMs benefit from the closed loop of verification, and can rapidly test and discard ideas. It's not too surprising that previous academics didn't try to optimize to this level, and a good task to automate. See ecdsa.fail for another example of circuit optimization using the Kickmix language.

Respectfully disagree. What one person may classify as expanding the envelope, another one with broader knowledge may classify as coloring in the blanks. Much knowledge in one field comes from cross-pollination from other fields.

Let me give you an example I just learned about. The database community spent decades trying (to some extent) to solve the implementation of materialized views to arbitrary complex SQL queries. Then, a few years ago, someone with experience in digital signal processing brought their expertise to the DB field and completed the puzzle. [1]

Someone with DB expertise but no signal processing expertise might say that is expanding the envelope, but another person with experience in both may say it is coloring in the blanks.

AI work now may seem like coloring in the blanks, but given it's vast expertise in multiple fields, my bet is that for most people it will look like pushing the envelope very soon.

We have excellent approximations of the full quantum chemistry that work really well, e.g. DFT. It's the gold standard for chemistry today. But these edge cases, although rare, do show up. They have a nasty tendency of showing up in biology especially, where reactions often happen on a knife's edge of the energy landscape, for efficiency reasons.

Chemists are interested in quantum simulation because it would be fully accurate and be an actual substitute for ground-truth experiment. With these approximations, you still wouldn't know if what you're simulating matches reality.
