mattbee|root|parent|next|

The reason we (used to) have responsible disclosure timelines is so that the information & software to fix vulnerabilities can be assessed, organised, written up. Builds could get built, attackers and defenders find out at once. But crucially, defenders could use the normal channels to fix their installations and be sure they'd covered themselves.

When a PoC drops before that timeline is up (like now!) the attackers get a head start because they have less work to do - just figure out how to use it to their payload, start spraying it out.

Meanwhile maintainers don't want release an incomplete or misunderstood fix. Otherwise they'd risk people updating and believing that they're safe when they're not. Even a fragment of misinformation from an official channel will spread like wildfire. So when you demand fixes NOW you're demanding something that may not be completely understood.

So (I'm sure we'll find out but) I believe this 2pm timeline was already as soon as is human possible for the PHP team to do a repsonsible job, and not risk us having to come back tomorrow for a fix to the fix.

LLMs gives attackers the advantage for a while, and it means we'll have to tighten the secrecy around even the rumour of a vulnerability.

aksss|parent|next|

The exploits seem to rely on multipart form submissions. In a normal setup, I don’t believe PHP’s multipart parser processes GET requests.

Therefore, a temp mitigation that runs short of shutting your site down is to temporarily block POST requests until you can get the patches in place. This will screw up any form functionality, including your WP login form, but keeps your site presence up. A failed form submission is probably a better look than no site presence. You can affect this with Apache config or by using a WAF. For instance if you have cloudflare caching your domain, use a free security rule to block all POST requests until your patches are in. Something like `http.request.method in {"POST" "PUT" "PATCH" "DELETE"}`
