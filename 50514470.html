It seems some people aren't happy about the fact that this course only glosses over each topic, teaching students only superficial knowledge instead of doing a deeper dive on one or a few.

But let's not forget that an overview of what’s even possible is extremely valuable in its own right. When these students are later faced with a problem X, they'll remember that there was this thing called Y which might help. They won't know the details, but at least they'll know where to start looking.

This is especially important if you want to rise to managerial positions, and it might become more relevant now that AI agents are increasingly capable of implementing things: Solving problems is not all about how to do something, but also about knowing what can be done at all. Laypeople often have no idea what's possible, but engineers do, which lets them be creative with their tools.

All of this is not to downplay the value of true in-depth knowledge of a specific subject. But those subject matter experts typically gain their expertise on the job, or at a later stage in their academic life when they conduct proper research. An overview course like this one doesn't hurt even if many students will choose to specialize later on.

This is why "T-shaped" knowledge is so valuable. You need depth in a given area. You should absolutely be an expert at the thing that you're most immediately doing most of the time. But you should also have a great breadth of knowledge, especially within your field, and the reason is so that you recognize situations where a particular solution to a problem you encounter may be relevant. That gives you the opportunity to learn more about that solution.
