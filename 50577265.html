tb99|parent|next|

It's also interesting to look at how the agent does the fix. Appendix E5 shows what it's doing:

- Find a common category of error (too many tokens).

- Calculate aggregated difference between states in examples in that error category and other examples.

- Update weights to push states along that difference direction toward the non-error states.

In other words, deprived of gradient descent as a tool, it approximates it. It's clever, and it's a neat experiment. It doesn't show any superhuman insight into its weights -- with the same constraints, a human would likely take a similar approach. But it's early days for self modification and it will be interesting to see if models get a deeper understanding of weights as they get smarter.

ozgung|parent|prev|next|

I have been working on weight-space-programming as a side project since the blog post. Here is what I understand:

- We normally train deep neural networks with back-propagation. We compute gradients to find out how to tweak the weights toward the desired output.

- That's a fixed recipe. And it's not the only way to tweak the weights. It's just the best one that works in scale. The brain, for instance, does not do back-propagation, as far as we know.

- There are other known mathematical methods (mostly from Linear Algebra) to analyze the activations and compute what weights to change and how, to push the output to the desired way.

- A sufficiently advanced LLM knows about these methods and can analyze data like activation maps. With a specific target in hand, it can combine them into new constructions. (see [2] for some of the methods they used and I collected in a repo)

- So it's not a fixed training for-loop anymore. It's a dynamic "training" iteration. The LLM looks at the model's outputs, reads some of its layers on chosen inputs, computes an edit, applies it and tests it.

- It's dynamic because LLM chooses that to do and how to do it at each iteration by reasoning. I think that's the most interesting part and could be a new paradigm.

- I also compared it with gradient training. On my target, training was better (details in [1]), but WSP can find its niche. Its biggest advantage is that it needs no backward pass: less memory, and the model only has to run inference. It needs no differentiable loss, it can enforce constraints directly instead of through a penalty term, and the edit is easier to interpret.

[1] https://medium.com/@ozgungenc/weight-space-programming-on-a-... [2] https://github.com/ozgung/weightprog/blob/main/docs/methods....
