I know people that work in mycology and plant pathology. My impression is that the "wood wide web" is generally a well-accepted and well-established concept. It's commonly accepted that mycorrhizal networks exist, that they benefit both plant and fungus, and that they allow propagation of resources between trees. There is some debate over the anthropomorphisation and the idea of "mother trees", but that's kind of what you expect when it comes to science popularisation in the media.

FWIW the book "Entangled Life" is okay as a popular science book, but Sheldrake isn't really a practicing research mycologist, so you can't expect too much from it scientifically. And it has some... slightly weird bits (do you really want to picture the author rolling around naked in some leaves?).

IMHO basically we know very little. One thing we do know is that they are not only critical for breaking down old biomass but are 100% non-negotiable partners for many specific angiosperms. Once you lose the soil biome, those species just don't grow. Therefore when transplanting something it can be handy to bundle a sample of nearby earth at an appropriate range of depths. This can sometimes enhance germination and growth potential for difficult species by including a fungal colony... especially in difficult biomes like denuded or pool soils where a critical fungal symbiote is generally more likely.

This technique was taught to me by propagating native plant scientists here in Australia, which has a great number of unique plant species owing to its historic isolation and consequent evolutionary independence.
