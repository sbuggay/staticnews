Anyway, about that hydraulic system: a friend of mine who lived in IJmuiden was improving his house. To do so he had bought a load of concrete tiles in Amsterdam on an impulse (really, really good deal). So he called me up and asked if I could come and help shift them to IJmuiden. When I got there there was this absolutely massive pile and I figured this is not going to end well. I had dropped the rear seat (which you could pull out fairly easily, but the back cushions stayed in place) so there was quite a bit of room and two tiles fit side by side. So we stacked them pretty much to the ceiling and miraculously, the car just kept on adjusting ride height and never gave any sign that it couldn't handle the load. All of the tiles fit. Then we drove to IJmuiden. It all went well until we hit the overpass over the road between Amsterdam and Haarlem on A9. The car took a bit of a dive into the dip and then came back out and came back out further than it went in. And did it again. Before long it was bouncing like a ball with the rear (which was pretty heavy) coming off the ground. I lost some speed and finally got the car to sit by the side of the road, still bouncing. Engine off, let the whole thing come to rest, engine back on, everything fine. We drove the last bit really slow, just in case.

So, that's what an undampened servo oscillation looks like in a hydraulic system. After unloading the backseat went back in and it was just like before. DE-70-72, whoever has it now, that car is as solid as they come (it is still registered, this was more than 20 years ago, the car is from 1968 and it has been off the road for a long time).

The hydraulic computer sits behind the left front wheel well behind a separate shield and it is super complicated, if you have to fix it you better know what you're doing.

Svip|prev|next|

Generally goes unnoticed (though not unmentioned), but until after the Xantia, the suspension system also included the brakes and the steering (which would technically not be permitted under modern EU car standards); which meant a leak would eventually not just cause a loss of suspension, but also a loss of steering and braking.

Why incorporate the braking and the suspension? The mechanical height regulator could "feel" how much fluid it needed to fill the suspension with, in order to obtain the desired ride height; which meant it "knew" how heavy the vehicle was loaded, and thus apply the brakes accordingly. This is why in a proper Citroën hydraulic car, the brake pedal feels "weird", in that it doesn't go far down (and quickly becomes hard to press) (the DS tried to make this less confusion by making the pedal a "mushroom" button instead); because the idea is that you should apply gentle braking, and the car will figure out the necessary amount of braking force required (unlike just hitting the pedal like in a normal car, which would result in an almost hard stop).

Why the steering then? I think that's more of a convenience thing, mechanically speaking, as it was already there to provide a form of power steering; though it is funny feeling it getting pumped up (steering was always the last portion to receive fluid during the pumping). At least, as far as I can tell, it has little bearing on the suspension or braking. I know the SM and CX tried the DIRAVI steering, which completely made the steering one way (so you couldn't feel the road); though it was fun to see the steering wheel self-righting even when parked.

Full disclosure: I own a 1974 Citroën DS 23 and a 1998 Citroën Xantia Activa V6.

yason|parent|prev|next|

Reliability is not really the issue. The 2000's hydropneumatic Citroëns rarely have problems with pipes and the suspension spheres have double-membrane so they won't leak the nitrogen and they'll last for years and years. To give some idea, having owned multiple hydraulic and non-hydraulic suspension cars in the last 25 years:

- I've changed countless dead/leaked shock absorbers on cars with springs, as well as broken springs, and nearly nothing on multiple Citroëns. These jobs take time, especially in the front where you nearly always have to remove the whole strut and spring just to get to the absorber.

- The spheres are easy to replace, they don't cost much, and I've only ever changed them because I want the ultimate suspension response, not because any of them actually broke. Some of them were originals that felt harder than they should but they still did up and down.

- I've had a couple of hydraulic hoses slowly leak from the pressed sleeve between the hard pipe and hose, and these have been easy replacements.

- I've changed more brake pipes in post-Xantia Citroëns than hydraulic pipes in total. Sure, in the 80's the pipes rusted but so did everything else.

If I had the choice I'd 10-to-1 get a hydropneumatic suspension rather than pieces of some twisted steel if not for anything else but less maintenance even if I didn't care for ride quality.

Why this isn't common is, I think, just cost. A coil spring costs nearly nothing to make and shock absorbers are so common that they're a bulk item so you can get them cheap. Changing those is more labour and you also need to change those more often if you want even a seemingly decent ride but hey that's not the automaker's problem once the car is out of the factory.

This would be different if hydropneumatic suspension had been more common, you could buy common parts for next to nothing because those parts are even simpler to manufacture. So, there's a bit of volume advantages that traditional suspension enjoys but hydropneumatic ones don't. They're not expensive but traditional coils and shocks are just so cheap.

Rolls-Royce did license Citroëns system earlier so there is some high-end appeal. But the problem is really that the hydropneumatic thing is not designed to be "luxury" but a mundane, everyday thing: only the cheapest and smallest city cars Citroën made didn't have it. Driving that Roller with a licensed hydropneumatic suspension could stand in red lights alongside a Citroën BX, midsize family car, with the same type of suspension, yet the Roller cost 10x more.

Further, people have been conditioned to appreciate rock-hard autobahn suspension, low-profile tyres and negligible suspension travel instead of a compliant road handling even if most roads are, in effect, not autobahns but bumpy patches of potholes where you want nothing but feel those bumps on the road.
