If we control the servers the clients are connecting to, I don't see how there could be much hope of hiding. We can spoof things like latency toward an upper bound, but we can never ever defeat the lower bound. Physics is still quite insistent about that one.

If we control multiple servers, we know where they are, and we can periodically compel our clients to rotate between them (see: DNS), we can engage in latency-based multilateration to put them on a real-world map. Depending on the number of servers and samples over time, this can be remarkably accurate. Determining someone's actual postal code just from network timing information is feasible.

VPNs obviously confound this in first order terms, but if we aren't careful they can make it even easier to identify our activity in other ways. We are trading concealment in the woods for concealment in a crowd. Both have their advantages and disadvantages. The VPN might also have a side channel that we aren't aware of.

legitster|prev|next|

Every time this resurfaces I like to remind people that this is mostly impractical and not how things actually work at the commercial level.

Just because someone can theoretically demonstrate your fingerprint today doesn't mean you have the same fingerprint in a few days. It's that kind of stability that you actually need for consistent matching for advertising or tracking purposes.

90% of commercial fingerprinting services still rely mostly on the IP address. Once you remove the IP address, you're left with a set of configurations that have a very short half life - volume settings, installed fonts, software versions, etc. You can expect a good portion of your audience to degrade within a couple of days. (Whereas a cookie has a life of 2 years - practically though they only survive a few months).

What you actually pay for from third party vendors is a connection made around a more stable data point - a phone number, an email address, a user ID, etc. With one of these data points you can actually track people across multiple devices for as long as you want.

ricksunny|root|parent|prev|next|

> If you own a house, you didn't have its cash value when you bought it;

> we solved the problem with ramshackle one-room buildings with dirt floors,

there is a fallacy here. People with access to debt bid up the price of an asset, like a house, against other peoples’ similar access to debt. Sans debt access, the solution was not necessarily a small ramshackle house. The fallacy is the belief that the same asset price - whether opulent or ramshackle house, wouldn’t adjust to the reduced level from the bidding environment. The differential cost of construction for a nicer house can be countenanced in the access-to-debt case, but that is second order.

Illustrative straw-man example: Suppose, once one had a parcel of land, that it was trivial to construct one’s ‘starter home’ the ramshackle house if you like. Get one’s family and friends to help build in exchange for future return favors. As one advances in life, rebuild to have something more opulent. The rub is in the parcel itself. Unlike the would-be house, it had no cost built-in, just whatever price the previous owner was willing to accept to release their parcel. That parcel’s (and those around it) price reflects the market’s access to debt, or lack of access to debt. In the market without access to debt, parcel pricing reflects which buyers have accrued more savings vs other buyers. In the market with access to debt, parcel pricing reflects who can access more debt (through the various means we know of today - down payment ability yes, which parallels the non-debt’s savings case, someone co-signing, long term expected future earning potential vs someone retired, the parcel itself put up as collateral, perhaps other non-immobile assets put up as collateral, like the family oxcart).

The point is that there isn’t necessarily a 1:1 coupling between market’s debt vs non-debt access and whether the construction is opulent vs ramshackle.

Actually they are about reading from or writing to terminal equipment of user that is not done for "sole purpose of carrying out the transmission of a communication over an electronic communications network" or "strictly necessary in order for the provider of an information society service explicitly requested by the subscriber or user to provide the service".
