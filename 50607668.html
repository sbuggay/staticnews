What are peoples' takes on why LLMs struggle to write things in a human-readable way? I've watched Astra and the like write PR descriptions that are middle-school level: as if it struggles to distinguish what it knows (having read the code, having done the debugging) and what someone who hasn't read the code and hasn't participated in the debugging (his target audience) knows. It'll make references to parts of the solution, or of the problem being solved, without contextualizing it, making the references useless, serving only to add confusion to the text.

Terence Tao's take was that LLMs don't understand what is trivial (in a proof, for example) and what is interesting and novel: it gives everything equal weight, or, in Tao's paraphrased words, the LLM will talk at length about something trivial and then write only a single sentence about something novel.

I wonder what peoples' intuition about this is.

> Does the raw count of lean code lines shed any light on how impressive Andrew Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem was?

No. You can increase the line count of any Lean proof almost at will by expanding axioms it builds on by their Lean proofs.

Also, one can write very long proofs for simple statements. As a stupid example a proof for “there is only one even prime less than a million” could follow the lines of

- proof 1 isn’t even - proof 2 is even and prime - proof 3 isn’t even - proof 4 is even, but not prime - proof 5 isn’t even … - proof 999,998 is even, but not prime - proof 999,999 isn’t even

instead of the simpler

- even numbers are divisible by 2 - prime numbers are only divisible by 1 and itself - assume p is an even prime - the, p being even, it is divisible by 2 - and, p being prime, it is only divisive by 1 and p - 2 ≠ 1 - hence p = 2
