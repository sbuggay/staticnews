It is true soundness bugs have been found, and the Lean community spends effort on it. But this page doesn't convey the gap between one incorrect verification and successfully misleading mathematicians about what is true: there are multiple checkers and you need bugs in all of them, without the input looking implausible on informal review.

(For example, a DeepMind researcher created a 'proof' that passed Lean and (a then-week-old version of) nanoda by exploiting a different bug in each. But if you fed that into a regular old LLM (or to a human expert, but I mention LLMs b/c they can be assigned to review even very large volumes of Lean code) it would immediately say "no, this isn't real math.")

The increase in soundness bugs found this year is notable, because LLMs are good at finding ways to their assigned goals, and they found some funny ones. And, besides fixes for the specific bugs, it led to broader work on hardening and alternative and verified kernels: https://leodemoura.github.io/blog/2026-10-15-lean-now-ships-...

So you have fewer avenues than at the start of the year to get a proof of False OK'd by one proof-checker kernel, and it's much much harder to get one through the gauntlet invoked by `lake check --paranoid`.

(This is also interesting as a(nother) example of the difficulty of making truly correct software -- you'd hope for proof-checker kernels to be pretty high on the correctness scale but seems like we've found 16-32 soundness bugs in a bunch of these.)

One thing I didn't spell out about why non-proof-checker 'informal' review is interesting is it means any exploits have to be hidden. Proof checkers don't care if the code they're looking at is weird in context, and it probably will be weird if it's a refcount-overflow exploit rather than a proof (to use an actual runtime-soundness bug from the August post on C++ Lean). The latest "thing" in some circles is denying that the recent proofs, e.g. of the Riemann hypothesis, are proofs of anything. People point out pointless or tautological sections of these proofs as evidence that the proof itself might be ill-formed, but Lean and similar systems don't work that way: absent a kernel bug, introducing unnecessary noise to a proof cannot make it prove something false.

Others are looking hard for kernel bugs. They exist, but there are good reasons (including multi-kernel validation) to be confident they don't impair these recent proofs.

In a situation in which you have a cohort of people who "want to believe" in some unlikely thing, you have to take the evidence they present with a grain of salt. So it is, today: people "want to believe" we haven't basically solved automated theorem proving when we have.

gr_norm|root|parent|next|

A lot of the issues found were in known-suspicious corners of the underlying type theories. It was previously fine to have certain convenient kludges around since proofs were written by humans, for whom the likelihood of accidentally completing a proof by triggering a bug was basically 0. These are being removed or their foundations reworked to deal with AI now writing proofs at unprecedented scale. It's actually remarkable how well the current crop of proof assistants has dealt with the instant orders-of-magnitude increase in the volume of proof thrown at them. So taking prior design considerations and skyrocketing usage together, it's not too surprising to see a few more soundness bugs in the past several months.

If you want a scare, take a look at how many soundness bugs the borrow checker has had. Nobody would deny its usefulness for correctness.
