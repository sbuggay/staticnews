augment_me|root|parent|next|

Both are wrong.

On the physics of silicon lifetime, Cerebras is right and Culper's calculation is wrong. As Cerebras blog points out, Culper took a 5-7 year GPU economic depreciation schedule and divided it by a 3x thermal power-draw ratio to claim the wafers physically die in ~2 years.

However in the blog, Feldman writes:

> By the next update of our S-1, we had set out to build a private cloud; the reason was that AI native customers and developers asked us to. One consequence was that the mix of fixed assets we used internally had changed to include production systems we owned for the cloud business. We therefore modified our depreciation schedule to 3-5 years.

However the timeline is:

Mar 2024: CS-3 launches.

Jun 2024: First confidential IPO filing (DRS) sets equipment useful life at 1.5–3 years.

Aug 2024: Cerebras Cloud launches (followed by $383M in 2025 cloud CapEx).

Dec 22, 2025: Updated DRS still certifies a 1.5–3 year useful life, 16 months after launching the cloud, contradicting the statement in the blog.

Late Dec 2025: Signs the 750 MW OpenAI cloud deal (3.7-year average contract).

Apr 17, 2026: Public S-1 (weeks before the May 2026 IPO) doubles useful life to 3–5 years, boosting 1H 2026 gross margins from 24.0% to 29.9%.

---

So the blog is not having the right causal relationship here, if launching the developer cloud in August 2024 was the reason for extending useful life to 3–5 years, that change would have been in the December 2025 filing.

Real reasons probably touch upon their deal with OpenAI, which had to be taken on OpenAI's terms because the IPO would not have happened with a 86% UAE-based G42 (which stalled the 2024 IPO over China ties). Cerebras needed a big US anchor customer to go public, and OpenAI opted to buy cloud capacity rather than buy and run the hardware itself(you can guess why). Once you have to capitalize massive owned cloud infra for a 3.7-year contract right before an IPO, stretching hardware depreciation from 1.5–3 years to 3–5 years is kind of free because all other cloud providers use a 3–6 year schedule, so it's easy to get an auditor to sign off on it.
