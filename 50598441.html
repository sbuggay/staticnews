If humans can decipher the proof and find that it holds up, then does it really matter if Ant releases the model? We don't demand that humans release their models. We just study their proofs.

It seems to me that the real hazard is in the 17 million lines of code. If we lose the ability for humans to have the capacity to study the proof, I think we have to dismiss it as science (or math, I guess). If it takes 4 human lifetimes to churn through all that code, we might still be able to get through it if we have a way to hand off progress. One team can do the first 1/4 over 20 years and gradually transition to team 2 who does the next 1/4 over the next 20 years. If we develop a way to do that, we can still count the AI result as science after it gets reviewed. That might take 5 years or 55 years or 155 years. But if it gets done, it stands.

Of course, a lot of scientists/mathematicians will decide they don't want to spend their entire professional career studying 1/4 or 1/10 or 1/100 of some AI output. That's fair. But if enough people take up the challenge and see it all the way through using methods the rest of the community can trust, then we really do have next level math. We might transition from the era where many people and many teams work over a period of decades on many different ideas and approaches to the same problem. Instead, many people and many teams work under a strict structure on the exact same proof for decades in order to confirm or reject it. We go from years and years to formulate a breakthrough that can be rapidly confirmed or rejected to a magic, seemingly instant breakthrough followed by years and years--decades, even--of work to confirm or reject.

I don't blame anyone who doesn't want to participate in that new process. But most people don't want to participate in the original process, either. The years and years of disciplined pursuit required in Mathematics has always been a moat. Maybe the moat just got wider and less gratifying to cross. But in my opinion we have not reached a point where I can accept these AI results based on the word of another AI. If they haven't been confirmed by humans, they haven't been confirmed.

I think we have to approach this situation with an open mind. Too many people want everything perfectly buttoned up on day one of a breakthrough like this, but that's never how breakthroughs have worked. For the Wiles proof of FLT there were seminars that had to be held just to get a relatively small number of experts in the world roughly up to speed with the proof strategy. Understanding of major results has never been anything close to instantaneous.

If anything, we will probably develop a deeper understanding of the proof of RH more quickly than we have for prior major proofs. And doing so will essentially be an engineering infrastructure project. Curious people around the world will direct their agents at simplifying and compressing the Lean source. This process will necessarily create a reverse theory-building process. Given how quickly agents can do this sort of work and how much interest there is in this problem, I'd expect understanding to develop from this approach quite fast.

I think we should expect future math proofs to feel more like this. The problems we and AI will increasingly tackle in math won't necessarily have a single key insight that led to their solution. Rather, they will be more like vast engineering projects that incorporate a multiplicity of ideas, techniques, and constructions that is just far beyond what humans could've brought together coherently. Even FLT felt less like one brilliant idea and more like a great infrastructure project around most of what was known in algebraic number theory and algebraic geometry at the time.
