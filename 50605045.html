zarzavat|root|parent|next|

You have to distinguish between adding new mathematical concepts and adding new foundations.

Adding new concepts is essentially equivalent to adding new types to a software program.

Adding new foundations would generally be considered a defect in the context of proving a statement about numbers.

Mathematicians want the most powerful tools built on the simplest foundations. The more you extend the foundations the more likely it is to end up with a logical system that is inconsistent (i.e. useless).

Mathematicians have hit this wall over[0] and over[1] again and learned to be minimalist in their choice of foundations.

[0] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell%27s_paradox

[1] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_U#Girard's_paradox

gus_massa|root|parent|next|

Assuming the proof is correct ...

The theorems in the proof follow an order, so each one ideally explains which of the previous theorems used. Humans are not so detallist, so most of the time it's implicit, but it should be somewhat guessable.

If one theorem is mistranslated, there will be a gap in the proof. You can patch the hole temporally with "sorry" that is a cheat code in Lean to complete holes. So the main structure can be verified. Later the idea is to remove all the "sorry" and replace them with a short proof and then get a real proof of RH.

If one theorem is mistranslated, there will be a gap in the proof and the "sorry" will be impossible to remove. People will check again the theorems in the border of the gap and fix the translation. Or it may be a typo in the proof, it's quite common to find typos even in published papers. So they will use the fixed theorem in Lean and make a fix in the English proof too.

Sometimes a step is not so obvious, and they will have to split it and/or make a small addition in both the Lean and English version. If it's short enough, it's fine.

I'd not worry too much about mistranslations. The problem is if there is a gap that nobody know how to fix.
