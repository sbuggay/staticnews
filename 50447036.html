An use of SIMD in x86 is fast ram copy/filling by sending all Avx register to ram with one instruction.

But I'm thinking more about having multiple logical CPUs, ALUs, or ports working on a single giant register. I think SIMD wastes a lot of hardware.

Example: radix sort needs to calculate a prefix sum. As the sum accumulates, it requires more bits, so the bit length should be adjustable. kragen|root|parent|next|

RISC-V intentionally contained almost no innovation of any kind. The whole point was to have the most boring, standard RISC design imaginable, without anything clever in it. That's what you want for introducing students to their first RISC architecture, and also as a basis for doing computer architecture or CPU fabrication research: on zero-overhead loops, compressed instruction sets, scalable vector instruction sets, IoT machine learning, indium gallium zinc oxide thin-film transistors printed on kapton, zero-knowledge virtual machines, or whatever.

They specifically wanted to avoid the kinds of "quirks" that vendors like Intel and ARM used to make people subject to their patents.

They did try to avoid what they saw as the blunders of past designs, like not leaving enough opcode space for compressed instructions, making instructions conditional, leaving out byte and halfword memory access instructions, having a flags register, having a complicated page table structure, having register windows, having delay slots, etc. None of that was innovative. The actual design of the compressed instruction set and the vector instruction set were innovative, but that came later.

The Mill was the opposite extreme.

jcranmer|root|parent|next|

That's not the usual way to discuss CISC or RISC (and ARM fails two of the three questions anyways).

To be honest, CISC versus RISC has not been a useful distinction in architecture design for 30 years. Intel managing to make a superscalar CISC processor, the Pentium, should have put to bed any idea that there's a fundamental distinction between CISC and RISC that is useful.

The reality is that RISC isn't a single idea so much as it is a collection of ideas for how to design a microprocessor, and some of those ideas have turned out to be good and some of them have turned out to be bad. Rather than evaluating on the merits of RISC or CISC, we should evaluate on the merits of the embodied ideas. Let's stop trying to view 2020s design decisions based on 1970s models, okay?

zamadatix|parent|next|

It makes a million times more sense when given the context it's found in https://vine.dev/docs/features/conditions

Tl;dr: Vine offers explicit versions of short circuiting and non-short circuiting conditions, but non-short circuiting is the default. The above explains that because it uses the `&` operator rather than the `and` operator.

Broken down further: Short circuiting means you can defer computing everything needed to know `c` until you have confirmed you need to know c to answer the comparison chain. If a < b then you might save a ton of work by not needing to ever calculate everything leading up to c. If a >= b then you've now held off even starting to find out what c was until you need to know what c is.

If you have a ton of parallel compute then it's not as big a deal to always compute all 3 in parallel at the same time, even if sometimes a lot of that compute was not needed to answer the question. The upside is you always finish in the shortest amount of time possible, even if c is a ton of work to figure out.
