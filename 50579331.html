Architects are a bad meme, along the same lines as scrum masters and product managers. Good software engineers spend more time on design than implementation, and the best are helping their teammates with design, and auditing designs of adjacent teams to ensure integration success. So architecture is an important part of the job, but that doesn't mean that you can automatically declare a role with the title "architect" and make the whole organization better at design.

Architect roles are a bad meme because the architect always ends up knowing less about the system and how it is used than the engineers and support staff, yet they have more influence over the decisions affecting those things. That's the essence of an organizational anti-pattern/bad-meme any time it shows up: influence over resources anti-correlated with decision quality. Additionally, architects often report to non-technical folks fairly high up the chain. Those people are bad at screening for such a technical role, sometimes even making the mistake of replacing an architect with an external hire.

jameshart|root|parent|prev|next|

All software development is architecture. Decide to implement a change by adding an if block to some code, by adding a function, by creating a new module and implementing it there, locally in the browser or on the server… those are all architectural decisions.

Large scale software development is just a nested series of architectural choices from top to bottom.

That some organizations choose to give some software developers the job title of architect is not a ‘bad meme’, it’s a reaction to the realization that there’s a lot of architectural decisions being made and it might be nice if all the people making them had sufficient information to make them right.

Good ‘architects’ focus on making sure developers who are making all those architectural decisions have the right context to make them.

Bad ‘architects’ try to make - or preempt, or second guess - all those decisions that have to be made. That doesn’t scale and doesn’t work.

I think this is an underratedly common experience. What makes it such a whiplash is that you're probably both at the beginning of a rather steep learning curve, but only just realizing that learning curve even exists. And reluctant to consider the implications, because of the impact it would have on your existing world model.

For inexperienced engineers it's that "your job is figuring shit out, not typing the code in", which for some people is like telling them that their dream job doesn't exist/the mental model most of their peers operate under isn't true.

For a manager it's that you can't really assume that everybody has the same personality/relationship to work/experience as you do, and that you might have even forgotten what it was like to be in their position, or not noticed how different some of your colleagues were from you. It's a pretty normal failure mode in teams. The abstraction breaks because the developer doesn't know the things that drive their latency.

Is it database requests? RPCs? Is there a function call which is computationally expensive?

Caught before merge? Only if you have someone really familiar with all the abstractions _and_ the team's previous failures reviewing it. Otherwise, developers are always finding new ways to write slow code. :)

lordnacho|prev|

If instead of becoming a software engineer, you became a civil engineer, you would not be pouring concrete and tightening steel cables.

Until very recently, a software engineer did the equivalent things: looking for small coding errors, hooking up libraries, writing unit tests.

Now, those things can be done by an LLM. All that is left for a software engineer is to do the actual understanding of architecture, and to be responsible.

Of course I can understand that people mourn being able to look over their implementation of some algorithm deep in the bowels of the product. That used to be fun. I can understand that young people in the field now face uncertainty, because doing the little things used to be how you paid your dues so that the seniors could explain to you how the big things were done.

It was like a deal, you would join the firm and do the stuff that was necessary, in exchange for training. You had time to learn the big things, because the little things took time.

There's another business that I started in where the gap also went away. In the old days, traders used to do their work on paper. There would be clerks who would note down trades, and check that the firm's positions were in fact what the senior trader thought. Pretty mundane stuff. But at the end of the day, you would sit with the boss and he would explain what had actually happened while you checked things.

I happened to land just as the transition was finishing: instead of taxing your physical conditioning by having you jog around the floor in a coloured jacket, the work went on a computer. The computer would let you solve the small things faster and better than you could ever do yourself. But it would also be the end of that guy who was just a good grinder. Now you had to use the machine to do new things in the market, and people started needing degrees to get the job in the first place.

The very nature of the entry-level job changed. The job itself opened up as well, though the fundamentals at a high level remained.
