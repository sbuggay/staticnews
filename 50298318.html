A random book from the collection looks interesting: Correction by Thomas Bernhard.

Described as “a mesmerising, claustrophobic novel about obsession, genius and the impossibility of perfection. Structured in two enormous, uninterrupted blocks of prose, it follows an unnamed narrator reconstructing the life of his dead friend Roithamer, before becoming absorbed in his manuscripts. Sentences spiral, repeat and contradict themselves, creating the sensation of being trapped inside an endlessly turning mind. Roithamer is modelled partly on Ludwig Wittgenstein, particularly his intellectual austerity and obsessive pursuit of perfection, reflected in his design of an extraordinary house for his sister. A darkly comic, exhilarating and exhausting masterpiece.”

fasterik|parent|next|

Bernhard is fantastic. I would recommend Woodcutters as a starting point, which is shorter, funnier, and less oppressive than Correction (which is his magnum opus). Apparently he was at least somewhat as unhinged in real life as in his writing. He was notorious for either refusing literary prizes or showing up at the ceremony just to rant and insult the audience, which he wrote about in his memoir titled My Prizes. He was a big influence on W.G. Sebald, another great German language writer (my personal favorite) who makes an appearance in McCarthy's library.

marttt|root|parent|next|

Our son was about 3yo when I read it, and I couldn't put it down. To me, it is a story of the father standing by his son no matter what. A really immersive reading experience. A father-son exchange that touched me:

  What is it?
  Nothing. I had a bad dream. 
  What did you dream about?
  Nothing.
  Are you okay?
  No.
  He put his arms around him and held him. It's okay, he said.
  I was crying. But you didnt wake up.
  I'm sorry. I was just so tired.
  I meant in the dream.
I do think McCarthy's own family life felt, well, "controversial" in the light of all this. But, at least he didn't seem to justify himself about it. IIRC, on not paying child support after divorce, he has said "with what?" in one of his rare interviews.

I'm not a native English speaker, and I've had writerly ambitions myself, and his style feels like magic to me (hey, an accidental cormac-y polysyndeton here!). But... yeah. Thinking about the way many poets "manage" to put art before family life (obviously, they can't help it) is a good reflection of me myself being just a layperson.

But he is a true master, obviously. Could be that I liked "All The Pretty Horses" even more than "The Road". Unfortunately "Suttree" isn't translated to my language yet.
