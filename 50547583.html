Cool coincidence. In a past job, I specifically made replacement PCMCIA cards for these (and other) planes. Instead of making cards with a controller and onboard storage, we made cards with an FPGA/microcontroller and a microSD card soldered to the PCB. We did this specifically because these aircraft use (at least partially) broken implementations of the PCMCIA protocol, and we'd update the bus protocol per aircraft to work around the limitations. None of these planes can just go buy stuff off the street because some large portion just doesn't work because their protocol isn't fully implemented and isn't flexible to deal with variance in timing/speed/etc.

A fun story: Our cards had whatever industrial-spec microSD card we could find (16GB, 32GB, etc.), and we'd internally limit the disk size to whatever the platform needed. Almost every aircraft had some specific number of MB they wanted because all of their CONOPS listed it, and they didn't want to update documentation or software to put a different number there. We had a plane that could only use EXACTLY 128MB, but they were currently using 256MB cards and partitioning them to 128MB. We updated the disk size to present as 128MB so they could skip this step, and they specifically told us to put it back to 256MB because they didn't want to update the commissioning process to remove the partitioning step.

It was rewarding work, and the card we designed was cheap (~$500 each, which is literally nothing for major platforms like this) and 100% US-designed and made (with the exception of ICs manufactured in the usual places). Still, the procedural and cultural practices of these program offices made it a real struggle to get traction and deploy them widely. Government acquisition is just generally designed around funding primes for current development and then paying through the nose for sustainment later. It was hard to design something for widespread sustainment use because by the time you were done and ready for production, a bunch of parts would be obsolete. Then you'd have to rev the design over and over. We were never allowed to stockpile anything, even if it was trivially cheap, to facilitate production later.

And another:

One of the hardest parts to find for a PCMCIA card is stamped sheet metal cases. When we made prototypes, we bought case kits where the PCB just dropped in if you followed the PCB outline and used the right 68 pin connector. Around 2020 the companies that made them started to discontinue them, sent out a notice to offer final production batch ordering to do final lifetime buys. I BEGGED a bunch of groups to go order a few thousand, or tens of thousands and just put them in a closet somewhere (they were like $9/each). No one could make the decision in time, they went obsolete. We ended up designing a new machined aluminum case with super tight tolerances and features and it was like 80% of the total $500 cost.

Without going into a ton of detail, I was working as a federal employee for a federal laboratory. Per regulation we couldn't purchase anything not directly tied to an agreement, and we weren't allowed to complete with industry.

Typical work would look like this:

A group would come to us and say, "We use these cards, but they wear out and we need like 200 more to keep running for 15 more years, we talked to primes about fixing this problem for us but they said the only thing they're willing to do is redesign the entire data handling infrastructure in the plane for $500M." So we'd get some tiny amount of money (like $200k) and redesign the card and make them ~20 prototypes. It'd work great, then they'd spend 3 years working on production contracts to try to get 200 built for $100k. In 3 years, some part would go obsolete, they'd come back and give us $50k to update the design and test, then start the 3 years cycle to buy 200 again. Rinse and repeat.

If they would have just given us like $30k, we already knew which parts were high risk of going obsolete, we could have just bought everything necessary to build 1000 units and dropped them in storage, but federal regulation limited what was possible. SUPER frustrating to have something that solves a problem and watch it fall apart over crappy policy.

It's sad to see this request, knowing that I was part of the team that designed a solution specifically to solve it, knowing it worked and tested it on the platform, but some part probably went obsolete and they never got enough manufactured to solve the problem permanently.

spicyjpeg|prev|next|

It's worth pointing out that there are actually two main types of PCMCIA memory cards. The most common by far are the ATA cards mentioned here, which are basically CF cards in a different form factor (equivalent to a CF card in a CF-to-PCMCIA adapter) and work by emulating either an IDE hard drive or an ISA IDE controller depending on wiring. While uncommon at this point, CF controller chips are still available and some specialized suppliers do manufacture these cards brand new using modern NAND flash.

The much more problematic ones to find a replacement for in 2026 are the so-called "linear" memory-mapped cards, consisting of one or more banks of raw parallel NOR flash with no controller IC. Linear cards were never standardized and the protocol used to interface to parallel flash can vary greatly across chip families, so the vast majority of devices that use them are hardcoded to only accept cards with a specific manufacturer and device ID. Many NOR flash manufacturers from the turn of the century have long discontinued their products and emulating parallel flash using modern FPGAs and microcontrollers is also non-trivial due to their memory-mapped nature, so owners of legacy telecom equipment and original Dance Dance Revolution arcade cabinets [1] are left with pretty much no viable replacement option once the stock cards fail.

[1] https://psx-spx.consoledev.net/konamisystem573/#flash-chips-...

You'd be _amazed_ at the red tape and expense required to modernize any part of a military aircraft maintenance system. You have to understand, when the US military orders a new plane, they don't buy only the plane. They buy the blueprints, parts manifest, pilot training curriculum, maintenance manuals, maintenance equipment, etc... literally just about everything except the manufacturing facility where they are built. Each aircraft is its own big program with tens of thousands of people employed by it at any given time. Changing even one small piece of it is a monumental undertaking.

Circa 2000, I worked in an avionics shop that serviced (among others) MH-53J helicopter navigation and autopilot systems. The test bench for this system was about 20-feet long end-to-end and was driven by a rack-mount IBM PC with an 8086 running MS-DOS. Storage was a 5MB (or so) pre-IDE hard drive with its own controller. These were... not reliable. And since we were a training base, getting new ones was just about impossible. When the drive died, much of the test bench was useless. I wanted so bad to swap out that drive with some kind of ISA->CompactFlash adapter, but _any_ unauthorized modifications to a test bench or procedure were strictly illegal, even if you knew what you're doing.
