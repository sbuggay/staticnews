anon291|root|parent|prev|next|

The papers describing lean and it's core operations are deliberately small. The kernel of a theorem prover is meant to be such that a human can check it for correctness. The kernel does not include things like type inference or execution. It literally checks the proof and that's all and everything needs to match exactly. It's fairly trivial to write such a thing.

Humans have to establish trust in this.

After that, the rest of the language is written. Yes it is possible the type inference engine is broken but in that case the kernel will reject it

cvoss|root|parent|prev|next|

I would bet no. Because the original proof is so distributed, with parts of it being very old, and with parts of it being, as a first attempt, not the most concise or lucid route to the result, we were rapidly approaching a world where the empirical knowledge of how/why the result holds was literally dying.

The article refers to its use of the "first-generation proof". It would seem this proof is too large to be truly known by one person, and the group of people who "collectively knew" it grew old and started to die. Since its original formulation, mathematicians began to compile, consolidate, and revise the proof into a 2nd generation digested form (estimated to be 5000 pages instead of tens of thousands). But that effort was still so monumental in its own right that a 3rd gen proof project began to digest the 2nd gen proof before the 2nd gen proof was even finished. I believe both the 2nd and 3rd gen projects are still ongoing.

It will be interesting to see how small the proof gets once we finish learning how to obtain these results "efficiently" with the benefit of hindsight.
