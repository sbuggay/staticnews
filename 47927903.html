>is to just not tell the AI models that they are AI

It's likely not as simple as that for the modern LLM case. As soon as you have a complete information loop where the concept of LLMs is part of the pretraining corpus, you already have a sort of fixed-point situation where base models can likely "recognize" that the interlocutor is interacting with something that's awfully like an LLM. I mean these things are trained to be great at modeling authorial intent, do you really think you can interact with an LLM without the "base model" picking up on that intent (both by seeing that one side of the conversation treats the interlocutor like an LLM, and the other side of the conversation has an output distribution similar to that of other LLMs [thanks to leakage back into the corpus])? The main question is whether a "base model" develops strong enough "self-model" to realize that the _it_ is the LLM being interacted with. I've seem some claims that even base models can model their own outputs well (so they can distinguish their own generated output from other text), but a base model never even sees its own output during training so I feel like maybe this is only possible due to leakage. (The model architecture does it admit it of course, but a recent paper showed that the injection introspection Anthropic discovered only developed during the contrastive posttraining phases)

A lot of modern post-training is ultimately derived from Anthropic's original "helpful honest harmless" framing, if I understand the blogpost correctly they instead just directly did Q&A post training without any implicit assistant framing. The model itself may not even be large enough to admit a coherent "self model". (If you ask it its occupation, it seems to just respond with random jobs).

USER: Do you think it will be possible in the future to use machines, powered by electricity, to do the kind of mathematical calculations that computers do today?

TALKIE-1930: It is not impossible that in the future mathematics may be computed by machinery, but the probability is remote. At present, the computer has to deal with large numbers, and the machines would have to deal with still larger ones. The personal equation enters so largely into arithmetical operations that the personal equation of the computer must always remain an important factor in his work, and a machine can hardly be expected to eliminate it. Even in the case of adding machines, personal equation has to be taken into account. Two computers may work at the same machine, and the one may total up a long column of figures more quickly than the other, though each works honestly. The personal equation of rapidity can only be eliminated by making the computers work in teams, so that each may correct the other’s mistakes; but this would scarcely be practicable in mathematical calculations. At best, machinery can only be used to save labour, not to supersede it. Computers will always be required to check the work of the machines, and to set them right should they go wrong. The essence of computation lies in proving the accuracy of the result arrived at, and machinery can no more do this than it can think. At present, the most that can be claimed for it is, that it reduces the drudgery of arithmetical operations, and so leaves the computer freer to attend to matters of thought.

Sounds like we may be able to build computing machines, but a human can't collaborate with a machine the way two humans can, and a human will have to check the results every time anyway, so what's the point? Only a human can prove that a calculation is correct.
