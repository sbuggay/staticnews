calebkaiser|parent|next|

This is a weird point because the Bitter Lesson spends a meaningful chunk of its short length talking about chess specifically.

People sort of generally refer to the Bitter Lesson as if its key argument is "Large Transformer models are best", but this is directly from the essay:

"One thing that should be learned from the bitter lesson is the great power of general purpose methods, of methods that continue to scale with increased computation even as the available computation becomes very great. The two methods that seem to scale arbitrarily in this way are search and learning."

The second paragraph of the essay is specifically about chess:

"In computer chess, the methods that defeated the world champion, Kasparov, in 1997, were based on massive, deep search. At the time, this was looked upon with dismay by the majority of computer-chess researchers who had pursued methods that leveraged human understanding of the special structure of chess. When a simpler, search-based approach with special hardware and software proved vastly more effective, these human-knowledge-based chess researchers were not good losers. They said that ``brute force" search may have won this time, but it was not a general strategy, and anyway it was not how people played chess. These researchers wanted methods based on human input to win and were disappointed when they did not."

The "brute force search" he's talking about (Deep Blue) used tree search with alpha-beta pruning on top of a ton of human crafted heuristics and a database of game-specific information it could access. Stockfish has historically been an even more "brute force" approach in that it is much more focused on optimizing search--which is one of "arbitrarily scalable" techniques listed in the Bitter Lesson essay--and it has increasingly replaced handwritten human heuristics with neural networks over the life of its development.

At no point in the Bitter Lesson is there a mention of LLMs driving cars, or a suggestion that large transformer models will be the dominant class of model for every single task. It is specifically reflecting on the fact that approaches that leverage search and learning, combined with large amounts of compute, consistently prove to be most effective. Stockfish would qualify as evidence of that, under his criteria.

calebkaiser|root|parent|next|

That's sort of a loaded question depending on what you mean by "smaller sub expert models inside".

At the most abstract level, you can observe patterns in a model's activations and theorize that particular collections of components contribute to particular kinds of operations. There are many flavors of this intuition. In that sense, you can think of there being "submodels" within a model, insofar as different parts of the network can participate in specialized computations. Some reading that might interest you in this area would include things like mechanistic interpretability.

There are also Mixture of Expert models, which are a dominant architecture for LLMs now, in which you essentially have a bunch of smaller FFNs that get routed over/aggregated together when the LLM performs a forward pass. It would be a mistake to treat any single expert as a better task specific model though. They are trained as part of an ensemble, and they're almost always being combined with other experts in the forward pass, so they're more like (to use a loose analogy) regions of the brain that tend to correspond more towards particular actions than others. You couldn't just extract one and say "This is now our Chess model that performs as well as the larger LLM we took it from". You could potentially use the relevant experts as a basis for training a smaller task-specific model, and there is work around expert pruning and other distillation techniques, but it's not as simple as "This smaller inner model is a complete task-specific model as is". And more importantly, that ensemble of experts *is* the LLM you're comparing it to, at least in major part. It doesn't really make sense to treat the LLM as some foreign model that calls the expert. The experts themselves don't often correlate to easily interpretable task domains, as they've been learned via joint computation with the other experts and broader LLM.

Then there's tool calling. Today LLMs are trained to make tool calls (basically execute functions) for particular tasks. Those tools can be smaller specialized models, or deterministic code, or anything really that can be fired from a host environment.
