Because the two clefs are symmetrical around middle C. Line "6" of the bass clef is line "0" of the treble clef. They are not two different clefs, the are two halves of the same 11-line staff.

It's actually a combination of both. Bar lines give you a bigger multiple, beams give you a smaller one (typically 1/4 or 2/4). Only within each 1/4 or 2/4 do you have to think in deltas.

Iso - same, morphic - shape.

Basically it uses a regular pattern of keys, instead of staggering keys into pentatonic and diatonic scales, like a piano does.

TheOtherHobbes|root|parent|next|

It's harder to learn because fingerings are based on key notes and transpositions around that.

If the key is G you don't start with your hand on C and move it up to G, you start on G and move it relative to that root.

This matters because over/under moves usually happen at certain scale points. So if you learn the scale you have good chance of defaulting to the most efficient movements with muscle memory.

If you're referencing everything to C you'll never understand the scales and their different fingering patterns.

bonzini|root|parent|next|

You need to have 2 notations because having 7 notes instead of 12 packs more information in less space. The scale is almost always made of up to 7 notes. Expressing each note as the difference from a default is essentially compression, as it lets you see both the position in the scale (important for harmonic relationships which help you memorize the thing) and the note which you have to play.

Sometimes you need G and the note to its right, so you want to call the latter a variation on A (A-flat). Sometimes you need A and the note to its left, so you want to call the latter a variation on G (G-sharp). They are the same note but they have a different function. It's not an idiosyncrasy, rather, when it works it's more efficient because it describes two things at the same time. It doesn't work perfectly for many cases: Misirlou, the Simpson's theme, anything in whole-tone or octatonic scale, but those are the exceptions in practice, just like some things compress better than others.

It is suboptimal even for the blues scale, but not too much and in fact that shows another benefit of giving two names to a note. The blues scale is mostly a variation on the pentatonic scale; the A#/Bb note in a minor E blues scale is used mostly as an intermediate note. So if you're going up to a B you can write that note A# because it is working like an A, while it you're going down to an A you will write it Bb because it is working like a B.

Likewise for chords. If I see E Ab B, E-A points me at a suspended chord, not a major chord, which would be wrong. Whereas if I see E G# B, it is clearly a triad.


simonask|root|parent|prev|next|

G# and Ab are not the same sound.

They are the same pitch in well-tempered tuning, but that’s a small part of the picture. They are not even the same pitch if you’re singing or playing a fretless instrument, like a violin.

The reason is that the pitch changes slightly based on harmonic context, and the notation communicates the context.

G# typically conveys a tension in an ascending movement (assuming the key is Am or one of its neighbors). Ab can convey tension in a descending movement.
