femto|prev|next|

Most professional engineers, especially those in electrical or communications, have the tools to understand AI.

I recently started trying to understand how AI works. It turns out that I wrote an "AI" in the late 1990s without realising it. The "AI" was an MMSE (minimum mean square error) radio receiver for CDMA.

This receiver consisted of an FIR filter (mathematically a linear combination) with trainable coefficients (weights). The coefficients started with random values. A sequence of known training symbols (tokens) was fed in and a gradient search was used to adjust the coefficients to achieve MMSE. The unknown data to be received (classified) followed the training sequence, at which point the training was turned off and the system ran as an FIR filter. Once trained, the FIR was a matched filter.

In AI terminology the above is now called a "layer". Current AI cascades multiple layers, in which case you need to pass the output of each FIR/layer though a non-linear function. If you don't have the non-linear functions at the end of each layer the multi-layer system is mathematically equivalent to a linear combination, which could be implemented with a single layer, losing degrees of freedom.

I write this because many electrical/communications engineers probably know how AI works, but don't realise it.

My still incomplete understanding is that LLMs are "just" a graph of connected layers. More complex that the above MMSE receiver, but trained and operated in the same way. The smarts in AI are:

1) Arranging the layers and the connections between them.

2) Having access to the right training data

3) Having the computing power to perform the gradient search (aka training)

My point is that most professional engineers already have the knowledge to understand AI/LLMs, and potentially already do know how they work but don't realise it due to a difference in terminology.
