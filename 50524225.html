Framing it as the ladder being pulled up doesn’t feel right. The people in existing jobs aren’t deciding market conditions.

The market for juniors has had some wild fluctuations. During the ZIRP period even juniors with little experience were getting hired easily because there were more openings than applicants. Now the balance shifted the other way and there are (seemingly) more applicants than openings, so jobs go to experienced people first.

There are still ways for juniors to get in to the industry, but it takes work. The days of getting hired for having basic knowledge are gone for now, but like we saw in 2020 it could change rapidly again.

david_shaw|prev|next|

I'm often asked how to turn a security-related interest or hobby into a job. For the longest time, I'd say pretty much the same thing written here: get a book, do some research, build some projects, talk to people in industry. I've been going to DEF CON for about 25 years now, and I've had this conversation countless times.

But in 2026, tptacek says it best in the comments here: At this point, who knows?

Are we still telling kids to learn programming languages to become software developers? Perhaps system design is a more reasonable skill set.

That said, because this is the time to say it: security is still important, and it's not -- and shouldn't be -- fully automated.

The toolchain is changing just like it always has. I remember when interpreted languages replaced compiled ones, and people said that interpreters solved security. Then people went from writing their own codebases to implementing frameworks, and people said it solved security.

Barracuda Networks posters at every airport stated "Security Solved."

As long as people take risks, security is important. Now we just need to focus on designing resilient systems instead of finding bugs and vulns on the code-level. LLMs can do that part for us.

What is even security, these days?

Algorithms are a solved problem. Post-quantum encryption was the last bit of excitement for a while. I guess ZKPs and FHE are still available, but they are really niche.

It's everything else that fills me with dread: we're using oodles of software written in C/C++, so we're one missing array boundary check away from the global economy collapsing.

Then there's phishing and social engineering. If one of our developers gets compromised, it's trivial to push malicious code into our repo. And from there it's an easy escalation into our customers' billing, private information, etc. We store the bare minimum of data possible, but it's still a lot.
