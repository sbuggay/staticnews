With FPGAs, you're paying for reconfigurability, which hurts in terms of cost, speed and power.

FPGAs should only really be used for:

Pre-silicon verification

Designs where power/cost aren't important (a lot of high end industrial or military for example)

Or low quantity designs that won't make an ROI when taping out an ASIC.

That said, I'm currently designing one into a product because at the moment, the cost of an ASIC is too much of a gamble (and also makes it too much of a black box for functional safety certification)

I also got a couple of different LLM's running on a commodity FPGA, and hit 59,000tok/s with t=1, and 23,000tok/s with t=128. I say LLM, but the model was 3M params, so pretty dumb! But, to hit that speed, I couldn't use the DDR4 as it's bandwidth was the bottleneck.

I currently have an 8b model running with the DDR in the loop, but it's stuck at 14ish tok/s, but the model is usable.

Hbm really is the magic that makes it work (economically)

nomel|parent|prev|next|

Their logic is based on lookup tables of the logic block, rather than the direct physical logic gates! So it's HUGELY inefficient for die area, like a few thousand transistors each rather than, say, 6 for a OR gate! But, the software won't waste them on a single gate, instead combining many connected logic elements into one "cell". For example, Xilinx has 6 inputs and one out, with 64 element LUT, which still a terrible, more than a 50x increase in die area, and therefore cost.

imtringued|root|parent|next|

A 6 LUT is well below a thousand transistors. Roughly 800 transistors or less. You need 64 bit for the SRAM which is 360 transistors. Double that for the multiplexer and various other features like fracturing or shift register features.

And that's a big LUT, more economical FPGA designs have smaller LUTs. For example a 5 LUT would only need 32 bits of SRAM which means it is half the size.

Also, a LUT is more powerful than an OR gate...
