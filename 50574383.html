And replacing the need to "fix broken windows" or do now-AI-automated work would free up that opportunity cost. Which is how it's worked for nearly all automation. This is a very common and forgiveable mistake, but you have the spending power of that $1B and the value it provides backwards. The economy doesn't "lose" $1B if one particular industry gets $1B less spent on it.

The purpose of money is to facilitate exchange of actual value between parties who both believe they are benefiting from transacting with the other party. Monetary supply is actually entirely separate from the value of the real economy it helps move around. Importantly: the $1B in spending doesn't disappear, it just gets held or spent on something else.

And when a $1b industry gets disrupted because it's no longer providing something scarce, the value of (as evidenced by demand for) whatever it was they provided hasn't necessarily changed, only how much human labor it takes to produce it (increasing the supply). Something still has to be meet that demand, which requires some incremental labor or capital that didn't exist before and somehow made it more plentiful. If there is competition to supply that thing then the price will settles on how much labor the new method requires to meet demand.

The destruction you're imagining is the "loss" of the ~$1B in transactions that consumers were previously paying to employ $1B worth of jobs. But consumers actually just have that extra $1B to spend, and people previously making about $1B now have about $1B worth of labor to direct elsewhere, either to meet existing latent demand or follow changes in aggregate demand that happen naturally as people's spending power increase.

Economies don't really lose jobs. Buyers lose demand for products, and employers lose demand for particular kinds of labor. But that doesn't mean that labor is less valuable to anybody else, just that their existing employer couldn't afford it. The same number of people with the same set of skills still want to work as before.

It's actually common for labor to increase in value even when it gets disrupted! What happens is a rebalancing of the labor market away from obsoleted industries/company/products nobody wants, towards those that leverage the disruptive technology. Concretely - why can't SaaS employees create AI products or new innovate in marketing/sales processes in other industries? They already figured out a new industry before.

What you're missing is probably this: when there isn't enough demand for employable people to find work, we can just lend (or print) more money until employers want to hire more people because it helps them serve more customers spending that money. Making it easier/cheaper to create new debt does the best job because it makes sure that the money gets spent on something that someone has a stake in not losing (IE that they actually wanted already but couldn't finance). In short: economies don't produce jobs. Jobs are what happen when demand on the labor market meets supply; and demand is effectively uncapped.
