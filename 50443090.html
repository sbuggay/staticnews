p_l|parent|prev|next|

Memory controllers are pretty much limited by external pinout.

Zen 4 and newer consumer chips have 4 memory controllers (for legacy reasons shown as 2) which handle 32bit wide LPDDR5 or DDR5 interfaces (2x per dimm for DDR5)

The AM5 (desktop) socket has in addition to the 4x 32bit memory lanes, 28 PCI-E lanes (generally 16x for GPU, two 4x general purpose, one 4x to chipset), 4x combined DP/HDMI outputs, USB 2.0 port, 4 USB 3.2 Gen 2 ports. And that's just the major buses, there's ton of other components exposed (simpler IO, sound, power)

All of that counts to staggering 1718 pins - anyone remember when moving dual-channel K8 from 940 pin to 939 allowed removal of whole layer from motherboard PCB, making them way cheaper?

The high-end performance server socket from AMD has 6096 pins, significant chunk of it being the 24x 32bit DDR5 interfaces.

So unfortunately it's very much not easy to add more memory, and that's before considering issues with routing it on motherboard even if you accept changing shapes

AnthonyMouse|root|parent|next|

> There was a brief period of affordable threadripper, but then product segmentation made that not great for amd...

The thing they're doing which is completely insane is using different sockets for Threadripper and Epyc.

The primary difference between them isn't the price -- they're both expensive -- it's that Epyc uses the power budget to reach higher core counts and Threadripper uses it to hit higher clock speeds. So for example, Zen5 Threadripper 9975WX has 32 cores with a 4 GHz base clock and 5.4 GHz boost clock. There is no equivalent Zen5 Epyc, the most similar is 9375F which is 3.8GHz/4.8GHz. Conversely, Epyc 9755 has 128 Zen5 cores and Epyc 9965 has 192 Zen5c cores and there is no Threadripper with that many cores.

The reason this matters is that the system boards themselves are pricey, not just the CPUs, but as the platform starts to age you start to get a lot of old servers on the used market that originally had a bottom-end Epyc in them. Which you'd then like to pull and replace with a Threadripper -- i.e. pay them more money to buy a new Threadripper -- except that they inexplicably decided to screw you and themselves by using a different socket.

And the same thing the other way around. It also means you can't find an older workstation with the bottom-end Threadripper and pay them money for a high core count Epyc with better performance per watt to use it as a server.

Whoever did it this way doesn't seem to understand that market segmentation is intended to separate price segments rather than just creating arbitrary compatibility impairment between things that have similar prices.
