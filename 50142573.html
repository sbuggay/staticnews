csallen|root|parent|next|

Two reasons:

1. Product decisions require far more knowledge than coding and architecture decisions, and that knowledge is harder to come by. It often requires research, talking to customers, understanding the organization of the company, the company's mission, the company's employees, their performance, stakeholders, the competitive landscape, funding, growth/sales/marketing strategies and budgets, customer buying patterns, company strategy, etc. Not to mention the code, the coding backlog, etc. Companies have to compete in a hostile, competitive, capitalistic market, which means they're trying to beat other companies, which means a seemingly very good product decision can actually be bad solely because other people are doing it better. Imagine if your perfectly functional code failed to compile simply by nature of the fact that better programmers exist.

2. Product design requires taste, and taste is partly a function of novelty, but there aren't that many LLMs, so everyone's using the same few LLMs, therefore everyone's getting the same designs, therefore the designs look like clones of each other rather than novel thoughtful design, therefore they fail the taste bar. In addition, LLMs simply weren't trained on good vs. bad visual or product design nearly as much as they've been trained on good vs. bad, functional vs. non-functional code.


danielbarla|root|parent|next|

I see these more fundamentally as arising from whether a good feedback loop is possible or not. The same is fundamentally true for human experts; you need validation that your work was good or not, or you cannot improve.

With code, it's fairly easy to have unit or end to end tests. That can be relatively objective and deterministic, and pretty quick.

Architecture is one layer removed, and far harder to test. You're stuck with things that are at least an order of magnitude to prove autonomously; will it scale in terms of performance, will the abstractions be a good fit for future requirements, etc. Testable, but much harder.

Design; well, there's possibly some metrics by which you could measure UX (attention for the viewer, contrast ratios, number of actions needed to achieve something, how far the mouse had to travel), but really the majority of it is subjective. "How does the human feel when viewing this" - I suspect almost by definition needs either a human, or a very good model of it. I don't think we have the latter, making this pretty hard.

These LLMs don't work such that training data === output data. You don't need to have 3+2=5 in the training data for the LLM to know the answer to that question.

Training data gives the LLM it's knowledge of the world, it can then bisect that space and output most likely answers.

If you are able to write a prompt such that you get the LLM to bisect the training data space, you will get the output that you want.

The trick though here is that guessing what is a profitable business venture is to some extent the same as trying to predict the future.

Something I've been thinking about a lot is that ultimately AI does not have values, intent, and taste. Even with humans, each of us has different values, intent, and taste, although we do share many of these things. As a living human being, you have biological or cultural desires and particular goals that you want to accomplish while you're alive on this earth.

When you're creating art or even a product, what you create is a result of your particular values and particular tastes and those drive some sort of purpose for whatever it is you're creating. What you're building is, generally, made for the consumption or use by another person (even if indirectly), and we mostly have empathy and want those people to have a good experience with whatever we're creating. When you use good software, you can sense the intent in what was made. The person (or team) combined their particular point of view with that of the goals of the user. I (think I) agree with the author, but I want to point out that his core grievance has little to do with AI software factories.

> The product changes are primarily additive... I think the reasons for this is that it's lot easier to add new capabilities, than rethink existing capabilities.

This is perhaps the biggest difficulty in Product, and the core of the Innovator's Dilemma. Changes or removals, fundamentally, fuck with how somebody uses your product. It is very, very rare to ship a feature - something that inspired somebody in Product, got all the internal stakeholders to sign off, including getting leadership to fund it - and have nobody use it, so that it isn't controversial to remove it; even then, removing it competes with resources for adding something else, so it's still controversial to remove it. When somebody uses something that you fucked with, you risk them churning, that simple.

Coming up with good features to add is not hard. Everybody and their grandma has ideas. A simple spreadsheet can count which ones are most popular and are proposed most often. This should not, in and of itself, dictate what you build next, and bad Product leadership looks like following mass diktat.

Good Product leadership is not afraid of the consequences of fucking with the user. Good Product leadership has both the courage to reformulate the offering and gets the reformation right, because they intuitively understand the user, the domain, and where they sit in it, better than anyone else. They ship stuff that fucks with you because they understand the difference between the bad kind of being fucked with, and the good kind. And if you churn, then you churn. So be it. You can't actually make everyone happy all the time, so focus on making happy the people who get it, and accept losing the rest. That's also easier to say when you have $100k in ARR with $1k ACV, compared to $5m in ARR with $2.5m ACV.

Good Product leadership is rare because good leadership is rare. AI has nothing to do with it.
