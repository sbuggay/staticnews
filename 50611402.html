mmastrac|parent|next|

The trellis quants basically rotate the weights in a way so you end up with something that looks like Gaussian noise, then do a search over the optimum set of bits to encode that noise. What you end up with is a close approximation to the old weights (up to a certain point), but with the energy of the values "shaved".

You lose the fine detail but keep the higher structure. Models are generally pretty resilient to it, especially larger ones. An over-quantized model tends to lose fine detail in its knowledge, lose some ability to track/accumulate accurately, and occasionally loops (because the activations are rounded off too much and lose energy, and it gets trapped in a state where it just can't escape).

When you quantize you try and get as big of a mix of calibration data as possible. You don't need to match the training _set_, just enough data that you exercise 99.999% of the most well-trod paths through the model. Anything outside of the calibration set is a crap-shoot as to whether it gets kept or not, but a well-balanced calibration set will keep performance near-unquantized.

On what calibration does: during quantization you try to minimize the error between the run of the model with original weights and the run of the model with the quant'd weights. Calibration data lets you weight the error away from the paths that are actually chosen with real data.

Apologies, this is a bit of a hand-wavey comment but it's sort of how you can look at it without needing to deal with the hairier math.

mmastrac|prev|next|

EDIT: I'm not sure why there's downvotes here - if you purport to come out with a new quantization format and it's not front-and-center what your inspiration and trellis are, that's a bit of a red flag (and especially any comparison to the mul1 codebook that it's evolved from).

Its seems unnecessarily hard to understand what NariQ is, but it appears to be a derivative of exl3's mul1 with k1-k6, costing ~2 IMAD + 2 LOP3 + 2 IDP + 1 pack IMAD. It's not a real half-width codebook, just averaging k2/k3 to make k2.5? Happy to see someone else discovering the integer addition tricks too.

FWIW in exlr8 we added a pmcgx codebook that likely does better than this at low bitrates, and has a real half-bit (it's pairwise so you can feed an odd number of bits to it). Our team just finished a quant on Qwen 3.8 flash next, but I might be able to spin one up on Qwen3.8-27B to do a direct head-to-head.

Ran a quick head-to-head on some pseudo weights via Claude too:

- NariQ's codebook comes out within ±0.3% of mul1 on every site. Neither of its two changes matters much at K2: the 6-bit digits and the skipped E4M3 rounding each move the error by about 0.1%.

- pmcgx L20 has 1.1% to 3.1% less error than mul1, so it beats NariQ by about the same margin.

Update:

- NariQ looks slightly better from K4-K6 f16 trellis (barely losing to mul1, which is the king there)

toebee|parent|next|

Hey, thanks for the feedback. Agreed that comparison against MUL1 is lacking. Still doing experiments on what the best format might be (hence the "v1 GGUF" naming). I did internally run against MUL1 in the experiment process and got similar results to yours. But chose it because the weights stay exact on BF16 and INT8 tensor cores, which may come in handy later (e.g. activation quants)

I'm happy to changing the format to whatever works best, and I'd say it isn't our core contribution. And very cool work on pmcgx, will look into it!

I think our contribution here is that we calculate loss counting only the tokens the model writes in agent sessions, which then drives a width allocator. + banded output-layer widths with two-sided rounding. This lowers KL by around 3x, compared to plain Trellis.

Interesting - we've been working on something similar, but found that calibration on the full sessions, but with the bit allocation on model-written tokens only was the most successful approach. I'll have to see if it improves our output if we weight error only on model-written tokens, but from my experiments it seemed that this was getting us the best results in terms of KL.

Our approach might be slightly different however, as we encode multiple bit widths and then select per-profile manifest with the lowest KL we can solve at a given size using the per-expert/layer encoded chunks.
