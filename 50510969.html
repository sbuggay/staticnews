Towards the end of the BBS era, I was trying to reach some of my old favourite haunts. Most of the calls resulted in a number not in service message coming over the speaker (which is useful in its own right), but one resulted in a perplexed and upset person picking up. I picked up the phone and explained that she ended up with the phone number of a once popular BBS. While she probably didn't like the answer, she was thankful to learn that all of those calls by screeching machines weren't from someone trying to harass her.

Those speakers were there for diagnostics of various types. One of them was figuring out when a person picked up on the other end.

I used the modem speaker for all kinds of things in the BBS days. To name a few:

  - Monitoring to minimize DTMF duration (S11 register, IIRC) and maximize redialing speed[1]
  - Doing the same thing with dialtone and busy tone detection times
  - Recognizing BBS callers by the unique sound of their modems, and using this to be annoying
  - Surprising other BBS sysops who were themselves trying to call out, by issuing an ATA and dropping them into my BBS's login screen when I heard dialing instead of busy, ringing, or carrier tones
  - Wardialing (I gave up on that forever when the voice from the machine said "911, what is your emergency?" and the usual automated hang-up-and-try-different-random-number routine did not disconnect the call, and instead produced an increasingly-agitated 911 operator)
[1]: A firmware update to the SupraFAXModem v.32bis allowed this timing to be so tight that the phone switch would miss digits, so tuning was in order

The general name for this is mechanical sympathy. Humans have advanced sensory processing abilities, and if we design systems that use them, the performance of the humans operating the system improves. It improves for the simple reason that richer feedback leads to a better understanding of the state of the system, which in turn lets the operator predict the effects of their actions more accurately.

I think we could be a lot better at using human sound, visuals, and spatial navigation abilities in understanding software systems too, but I've never gotten around to implementing anything despite plenty of ideas.

My latest experiment is using tones and/or speech synthesis artifacts to get insight into LLM agent operation -- ongoing reasoning, tool call success/failure, etc. Still haven't really dialed it in (hah!) to be useful yet.

All these answers just say what you would assume by applying common sense without any sources or any deeper justification.

I'm sorry I can't you give any of these either but I can give you a completely different explanation which makes a lot more sense to me (having lived at the time).

Early modems were so called acoustic couplers. That meant that they did not connect electrically to the telephone network but acoustically. Mechanically they were a bay you put the handset in and it had a microphone opposite the speaker and speaker opposite the microphone.

So, the speaker was already there[1] when (pure) modems became popular, it's just that the microphone disappeared over time.

[1] And so was all the software infrastructure to support it, like ATM and ATL commands.


curiousObject|prev|next|

As well as all the other good reasons: modems had speakers because they COULD have speakers, because modems were connected to a network which was designed to safely transmit human-audible analog sound waveforms, which is exactly what cheap speakers are designed to emit.

If we directly connect a speaker to a different data network format, such as Ethernet, we probably will hear nothing useful. Maybe some clicks and pops, or the smell of a blown speaker.
