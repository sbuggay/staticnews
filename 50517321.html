No big surprises here. Big outages at big firms seem to always look like this.

The electricity industry has this notion they call a black start, where they bring the grid online from zero. It's a very difficult operation because you can't start a generator straight into an offline grid otherwise it gets hit with full demand and can't keep frequency in the right range, which can damage equipment. So they have to start by disconnecting all the loads and then bringing load blocks back in sequence, trying to roughly balance available generation with the size of the next block. In practice black starts are extremely rare, but they can happen after a cascading failure in which something causes some generating stations to trip, redirecting load onto the remaining stations which can't handle it causing them to trip in turn. The entire grid can collapse in just 60 seconds or so once that kind of runaway failure starts.

This kind of outage is very analogous to a black start. A few servers get into trouble - maybe they're in a crash loop or maybe their caches are cold - the load balancers shift traffic onto the remainder which then can't handle the load either and so the failure cascades. Once a service is in this state sometimes the only remedy is to disconnect all load, bring the servers back to stability, then bring load blocks back online in small chunks, making sure to never hit anything with the full load immediately to ensure any caches or JIT compilers have time to warm up.

It's the sort of thing that requires a bit of preparation because naive ways of doing it like just randomly dropping 90% of requests will yield a broken service for everyone. Ideally your LB can drop traffic by modulus of the session cookie or similar, so users are let back in atomically.

Black starting a service is obviously quite hard to practice, but is probably something that sufficiently well resourced SRE teams should try once a quarter or so on a test cluster. I used to be an SRE at Google and although we regularly started up new clusters and moved traffic around between them, actually starting an isolated service instance entirely from scratch was never something we practiced. I remember in a few cases teams discovered devs had introduced circular dependencies by accident, i.e. starting service X required service X to already exist, or it depended on service Y that in turn depended on X. Sometimes you can only find these things by practicing.

This is one of those beautiful engineer understatements. Rotational speed is fundamentally linked to voltage by the motor velocity constant: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_constants

Although it's fundamental for all motors, the actual constant is arbitrary for each motor. Voltage is still proportional to change in field is proportional to speed; at zero voltage you have zero speed.

A large dead AC load has its own speed- zero. It's connecting the engine without a clutch, but the engine makes hundreds of megawatts. The 6' wide generator wants to go from 3600 RPM to a standstill in a few inches. A full speed rotor can have up to 5 gigajoules of rotational inertia; over a ton of TNT.

On a live grid you've got a synchronoscope: https://youtu.be/LVozbX9Mn1c?si=RhRPIkuTVxh4Wseq&t=181

Which is great, because connecting it backwards would cause a slow motion explosion. On a dead grid the only thing saving you is the resistance in the wires; it would be LOUD if you connected to it and you would very likely increase the ventilation through the walls of the plant.

dboreham|root|parent|next|

Quick note that sometimes it's not that simple. I've worked on a somewhat similar system (although overall much smaller in scope). What we found was a) clients hop between networks and hence IP addresses quite frequently: mobile devices. And b) with sync type protocols you can't just randomly serve whatever sliced and diced subset of the incoming traffic you like. What happens is that many devices end up never achieving current sync state, so they re-try. In our service we had to implement a protocol-aware, and device ID-aware filtering scheme. It had the property you mention where you could dial the proportion of load up or down, but it did it across the known population of devices: if a device got white-listed at t=0 it remained white listed for the remainder of the day. Once it achieved up to date sync it got put into another load bucket. The bucket of devices transitioning from out of sync to synced up was what we limited. It was only after we implemented such a scheme that we got past our thundering heard / can't bring the service back up after an outage problems.

materielle|parent|next|

There’s all sorts of way to do it.

The easiest, and the default choice when I worked at Google, was exactly what you said: a sliding scale.

You could target racks, data centers, clusters, regions, etc. And “drain” the unit. For instance, stop routing traffic to cluster foo.

Then you could increment up stepwise 10%, 20%, etc.

Obviously we didn’t use nginx, and sure there’s interesting engineering involved. But it’s not like these systems are magic. There’s just a lot of clusters and a lot of load balancers. But at the end of the day, it’s just a load balancer.

As another Xoogler in this thread pointed out, there’s two problems:

1) Sometime teams accidentally introduce circular dependencies. Service foo might require service bar to boot and vice versa. Typically that’s fine, but from a rare cold start where both foo and bar are down, you’re in a pickle.

2) Load balancing is “easy”. But the services need to be designed with the aforementioned load balancing strategy in mind. Stuff gets tricky if there’s any pollers, process affinity, multiple components that need to process parts of the same user’s request in tandem. Given that outlook is ancient, I would not be shocked to learn that the system doesn’t respond well to naive “slowest ramp up traffic to a cluster” approaches. For big distributed applications, it takes a lot of meticulous design to build a system that responds well to simple SRE techniques like that.

That is one thing my team at Google would actually consider. We would make the code and architecture more complicated to accommodate the types of naive mitigations one would want to apply in an outage.

toast0|root|parent|next|

Selectively allowing IPs is a reasonable way to do it. If you think about it, it's terrible, but it will get you going, and it's pretty easy do something like start with allow nothing, then allow all ips under 2.0.0.0, then 3.0.0.0, etc.

Distribution of users by /8 isn't very even and the order that customers will get online wouldn't be an order anyone would pick, but it should be reasonable to implement and synchronize (if needed) and it lets you make progress.

You could do similar in IPv6, start allowing IPs under 2001::, and bump it up little by little... probably each increment would be the 2nd byte, but you do need to increase the first byte too ... or you could be a terrible person and drop ipv6 while you modulate ipv4 and only let ipv6 in when ipv4 is at 100%.

Works better the earliest you can apply it... if your whole stack passes through requests and the origin is looking at X-Forwarded-For (or similar) headers to drop requests, your whole stack worked on it for nothing... Which could be ok, but your frontends might be much happier if they just dropped SYNs from IPs when the request will fail.
