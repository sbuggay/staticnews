For those of you who suffer tinnitus, specially if recent, I can't recommend enough a psychological therapy approach.

I recently got tinnitus (about a year ago) due to a badly treated ear infection. Most of my hobbies (reading, coding, studying) are done in quiet spaces, so the idea that I would never hear silence again was devastating.

But as my doctor pointed out, a signal being there forever does not mean you hear it forever: You currently have clothes brushing against your skin and your ass being pressed against a chair, but it doesn't occupy mental space.

This is consistent with research. We are currently very limited in means to solve the physical issue, but we are very far ahead in terms of improving the distress or perceived effect.

Nowadays I lie in bed in silence, just as peaceful as before. And sometimes this fact surprises me, and upon doing a double take, it wasn't silence, the noise is there. ajb|parent|next|

This is probably good advice, I would add some caveats.

Psychological issues can be likened to an immune disorder, there is some trigger, but then the problem sustains itself and can continue even if the trigger is removed (eg, by getting a phobia of noises). So therapy can help with that, but psych professionals often assume their work is the complete solution; if there's an underlying physical problem it's worth getting that treated as well, if at all possible.

Like any profession, it tells a sweet story about itself, and does not live up to 100% of it. A more realistic view is that a therapist is like someone you might get to solve a damp or pest problem in your house - they need to make a living, and will try to make their solution fit your problem even if it doesn't. You are still going to be the one who has to decide if you are getting good value from them - how long to try before seeing results, and how long to continue once you've made progress (assuming you do).

torben-friis|root|parent|next|

The words to look for are cognitive behavioral therapy (oriented for tinnitus), the specific exercises and techniques will vary by professional, I suppose.

Part of what I trained for example is attention shifting, stuff like hearing two conversations at the same time and training the ability to move focus from one to the other, then shifting between sound and a different stimulus, like reading while a sound is on and switching attention from the text to the speech.

Then you focus on acknowledging the existence of your tinnitus without checking it, which was hard for me to split apart. Basically, calming the demand of your brain to pay attention to the screech without in doing so shifting focus to the sound itself.

An analogy that works for me is: Say that you're on your phone and you get an instagram notification. You don't want to open the app (focus on the sound) because it will distract you, BUT you don't want to pretend the notification isn't there either, because then the presence of the icon bothers you every few seconds. What you want is to acknowledge and dismiss without opening the app. Eventually you can dismiss with muscle memory without consciously registering it happened.

I'm telling you this anecdotally, but I absolutely do not recommend self learning, a professional helps immensely.

robrain|prev|next|

0. Don't panic: seriously. Grabbing on to "oh my god I've got tinnitus" can be the cause of much of the ongoing problem. Start from a position of "that's interesting". Curiosity, not terror.

1. See a doctor to rule out physical causes. May just be earwax. They may recommend an MRI to check for acoustic neuroma (benign tumour) or other causes.

2. Tinnitus Retraining Therapy started by Jastreboff and Hazell is well-regarded. Some of it relates to point zero above: education and CBT to reduce the mental impact. There are also more sound-focused aspects. Best results come from working with an expert and availability is limited. The principles can be read about, usefully, but you need to go further than just learning.

3. Neuroplasticity can be a useful tool for some. A popular book for that is Rock Steady: Healing Vertigo or Tinnitus with Neuroplasticity.

4. General principle: Live. I don't mean distract yourself or find work-arounds. I mean live. Do the stuff you ordinarily would. If you notice your tinnitus, note it and then move on. Like mindfulness and returning to the breath - you don't beat yourself up, just notice and return to the breath. Focusing on solving the problem will make the problem worse - ask me how I know.

I used my reasonably good sense of frequency and a synth to work out my frequencies. Great sense of achievement. But no use to my "treatment". Maybe it will be the missing piece for you.

This is why I built this: I've been living with tinnitus for the past year and a half or so, and while there's not a guaranteed cure, I'm taking a "try anything that shows promise" approach.

One such approach is audio notching: Playing audio with a gap right at your tinnitus frequency to trigger neighboring auditory neurons to quiet the overactive neurons responsible for tinnitus.

The browser version will play background noise (pink noise, ocean, forest, etc) with that frequency notched out. Or, if you install the macOS app, it will notch out all system audio.
