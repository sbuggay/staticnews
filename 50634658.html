This is interesting, although the “beyond specialized models” feels a bit premature. It seems like GPT can act like a glue layer between the highly specialized models that carry out each specific sub-task in a protein design workflow… and in some cases, can outperform specialized models at those tasks.

I’ve noticed that LLMs are very good at this type of glue work between highly specialized fields. It used to be quite difficult to “connect” your own work to adjacent tools made by other SMEs, sometimes so difficult that it was impractical.

I suspect one of the biggest impacts of LLMs on science will be the enablement of more frictionless collaboration and higher-level integration of specialized capabilities in a single study.

That said, the use of “intrinsic design capabilities” in the title of this article is misleading, as much of the demonstrated capabilities involve tool calls, and/or need outside support by the same specialized models they claim to have moved beyond in order to arrive at useful results.


elhospitaler|prev|next|

I did my PhD at the Baker lab, haven't had the time to properly read this over but some initial thoughts:

Makes total sense that they'd be working on this, it's something we discussed in the lab as well as the likely future of this work - true integration of the language modeling capabilities with proteins, natively multimodal like it's done with images. Five years ago we laughed at people with galaxy brained ideas to train / fuse a protein and natural language model, advertising this idea of "give me a natural language request and the model spits out the sequence that does the thing". There are many objections - the annotated data is not there, trying to pose everything as a language modeling objective sounded really naive at the time, and there are lots of tasks that are structural/chemical in nature that require inference based on physical and chemical principles, which interpolating machine learning models shouldn't be able to do. Most of these objections still exist, but now we aren't laughing. I do think eventually these models will get there.

That said, this work of course advertises far more than it accomplishes/has some things that have me holding judgement. One is I don't see how they did train-test splitting, or if they attempted this at all. This is really important - for some of these tasks, they are comparing against "specialized models" that did pretty rigorous holdout evaluation.

Also binder design is basically the easiest task, super solved a million times in the past few years, and their designs just made helical bundles, I don't think this is particularly impressive in terms of pushing the frontier. There is a hierarchy of difficult problems in protein design and this is just really easy, especially because experimentally these helical bundles bind really well and make strong interfaces.

The antibody CDR design tasks are also probably fairly trivial? I'm not sure, I'm not deep into antibody dev/design but there are lots of structures and sequences of anti-pdl-1 antibodies, so again a case of train-test split. I'd be curious to see sequence homology to these published antibodies.

But yeah, my take is that at the object level, this work is not super impressive. At one level of remove, the fact that they are getting an LLM to do these tasks that are normally done by specialized protein language or structure models is impressive. And then at one more level of remove, it's a big gauntlet thrown, proof that they are pursuing this line of research. I wouldn't be surprised if it works much better, soon, with the pace of these things. And we know Anthropic is working on this as well - https://xinmingtu.cn/blog/2026/claude-opus-5-5-protein-seque....

Might have to find a new line of work soon lol, thought I had at least another 5 years left.

The vast majority of our ability to predict functional and structural properties of biological sequences comes from disentangling their evolutionary relationships (how a sequence evolves over time tells you, for a given molecule to function, what needs to be where and what interacts with what).
