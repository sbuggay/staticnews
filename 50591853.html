If you're new to quantized models: Don't get too excited about claims of fitting large models into tiny RAM footprints from random GitHub repos. There is always a tradeoff, and it's too easy to benchmaxx KL-divergence numbers and even single benchmark scores. Just because you can get something to run on a device doesn't mean it's going to be useful. The headline claim of this repo (12X better) doesn't even apply to Inkling-2-Mini

This repo's README is a mess of different histories as the author bounces from one popular model to the next. It refers to vague things like "the 1.2GB file" that probably made sense in an early vibecoding pass, but are now lost after the repo has been pushed around to different popular models.

The truth is that random GitHub repos with messy READMEs almost never hold a real secret that the mainstream quantizations have missed. I would recommend sticking to mainstream quantizations like the official GGML release, Unsloth's quants, or the more well known community quantizers like bartowski or AesSedai. They typically follow an optimized size-quality tradeoff so you can find the one that fits your needs.

When you see a repo like this where everything is so obviously vibecoded that the README is a rambling mix of projects and claims, you should probably go looking for a more authoritative source. The local LLM communities get spammed with 10 of these types of projects per day but I have yet to see a real breakthrough come from any of the vibecoded ones. The real work is coming from people who study this and have compute to test at scale or work on implementing advanced techniques which require inference engine support, like trellis quants.

Aurornis|root|parent|next|

KL divergence is computed over samples from a text corpus. WikiText-2 is commonly used, which is a sampling of Wikipedia articles.

Quantization processes are somewhat complex and are more involved than uniformly truncating bits. Look up imatrix quants for a sampling of some of the techniques used. The short story is that the quantization is performed in a way that prioritizes weights which matter to a certain corpus. The corpus used as the input to that process therefore biases where the quantization losses go.

It's common to use a corpus like WikiText-2 from Wikipedia articles, but you can imagine a random sampling of wikipedia articles may not be favorable to, for example, programming work. A lot of quantizers keep their corpus as a secret sauce, so you may not even be able to see what they're prioritizing or even how some of them measure KL divergence.

You can get amazing KL divergence numbers if you basically train the quantization on some text corpus and then measure KL divergence on it.

This is also why you can't compare KL divergence numbers from different sources unless you've verified that they're computed in the same way.

There are a lot of quants out there that claim amazing KL divergence numbers but are really bad for programming work, for example. If you want to do continuations of Wikipedia articles they might be great.
