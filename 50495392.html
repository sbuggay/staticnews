sho|root|parent|next|

Your ideas about the tropopause are a bit eccentric. 36kft is just a model average, it's more like 25kft at the poles and 50-60kft at the equator. Large jetliners fly well below and above it all the time.

Temperature's just one factor. Air density, lift-to-drag ratio, weight, speed and engine performance all matter. Thinner air reduces drag, but you still need to generate enough lift, and there's a point beyond which the trade-off stops paying off. Business jets are generally lighter, with wings and engines designed for higher-altitude cruising, and regularly fly above 40kft, sometimes even 50kft.

The general max service height of airliners is an engineering trade-off between air density, lift-to-drag, weight, speed and engine efficiency. Air temp is part of it, but a small part, and there's nothing magical about the tropopause.

While this is a real operating constraint I am missing evidence of a major airline problem. The article turns normal flight planning into something much more dramatic.

Step climbing is routine on long-haul aircraft, including widebodies. A fuel-heavy aircraft initially cruises lower, then climbs as it gets lighter. It isn’t continuously struggling uphill for hours.
