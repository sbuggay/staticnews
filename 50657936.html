He spoke at Imperial College London when I was doing my PhD. Sometime between 2011-2015. He spoke about paper based microfluidic devices for rapid medical diagnostic tests. If memory serves he made clear to the audience that his "cheap" devices _were not cheap enough_ to be useful in developing countries. I admired the intellectual rigour and honesty.

It resonated with me as I was part of a doctoral training centre for plastic electronics. Everyone was making these devices using incredibly expensive materials and low throughput techniques, but the only way the technology could be viable commercially was for it to be extremely cheap. IIRC there was one paper that calculated that the devices has to be as cheap as carpet to be worthwhile.

Granted, I was a pretty obnoxious twenty-something, but it didn't make me popular to point this out to my fellow researchers.

George Whitesides was truly a larger than life figure in chemistry who left and lot of knowledge and discovery and a long line of students and postdoc in his wake.

It’s a good paper about writing papers. But honestly I’ve never found it all that effective for my writing when I was in academics.

Whitesides led an immensely huge group, and this style of outlining and drafting worked well for his group, and perhaps similar groups of this size. It’s not necessarily effective for other advisors or situations.

Regardless, it is well-written and good food for thought.

He gave a talk maybe 10 years ago, and he showed scientific problems falling into two quadrants, one axis is low impact or high impact, other axis is easy or hard.

He said to focus on only the impact axis, because if you failed at least you worked on the right problem. If you chose low impact and succeeded, it still didn’t matter.

He was also against accolades and awards, and said toddlers get gold stars, adults don’t need them

Horrible news, he was one of the greats.

I was lucky enough to attend a talk he gave in the late 2010s at a university in the US, as part of a seminar series we had. It was completely different than other talks I was used to: usually people would throw up a bunch of charts and glaze their own work for 45 minutes, but Whitesides was a lot more philosophical and self-critiquing. I hadn't even heard of transhumanism, but he brought it up near the end when he talked about where he thought science might go in the next century.

It's a crime that he never got a Nobel; I'd be surprised if he wasn't a serious contender for the last 20 years.
